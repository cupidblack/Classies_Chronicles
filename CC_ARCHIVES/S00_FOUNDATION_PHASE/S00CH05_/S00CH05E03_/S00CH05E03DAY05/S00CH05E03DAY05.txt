### THE NEW LEGON COMPOUND — FRIDAY MORNING

The request was still waiting.

At the newsroom desk, a small status label occupied the top-right corner of the Kuala Lumpur records inquiry:

**QUEUED — TRANSMISSION NOT CONFIRMED.**

The Legal Journalist had asked three trainees to review the correspondence before the weekend. The Cooperative Intern, the Liaison Intern and the Liaison Personnel had each been given a different section of the documentation.

Their assignment appeared simple: establish precisely what had happened to the revised request.

The first trainee placed a scanned document on the screen.

"Here. It has a receipt stamp. Doesn't that establish that it was received?"

The journalist leaned closer.

"Which document does the stamp acknowledge?"

"The request."

"Look again."

The trainee enlarged the image. The stamp belonged to an earlier distribution register. It acknowledged the circulation of one of the notices, not the revised request for the release of the sealed archive.

The distinction was small, but consequential.

A document could be prepared without being sent. It could be sent without reaching its destination. It could arrive without being acknowledged. And even an acknowledged document could be received by someone who had no authority to act upon it.

The journalist drew five columns on the whiteboard:

- Prepared.
- Transmitted.
- Received.
- Acknowledged.
- Authorized.

"These are not interchangeable states," the journalist explained. "And authorization is not simply the next stamp in the sequence."

The Cooperative Intern began transferring the available evidence into the new register.

A thick folder slipped from the edge of the desk. Its contents scattered across the floor, while a loose rubber stamp rolled beneath the chair. When the Liaison Intern retrieved it, the stamp had somehow attached itself to the sleeve of the Cooperative Intern.

The stamp read **PENDING**.

Nobody had to explain the joke.

For once, the paperwork had accurately described the situation.

### KUALA LUMPUR — THE FIELD OBSERVATION

Far from New Legon, the deployed team continued its observation-only assignment.

The building's four emergency notices remained in their respective positions. Their languages differed, their layouts were not identical, and the disputed arrows continued to present a problem that the team had no authority to resolve.

The field observers did not test either stairwell.

They watched how people moved through the public areas, recorded the positioning of the notices and compared the visible building features with the available documentation.

A maintenance worker moved a trolley through the corridor. A visitor paused near the lift to examine one of the signs. Another person passed without stopping.

The team recorded these ordinary movements as part of the environment in which the notices were being displayed.

One observer photographed the distance between the lift and the nearest notice. Another documented the angle from which the fourth arrow became visible.

They were learning something important about the difference between a sign's existence and its practical meaning.

An instruction might be legible on paper yet ambiguous when encountered by someone moving through an unfamiliar building.

The team had no live-stream connection to New Legon. Nothing they were observing was appearing on the laboratory screens in Accra. Their photographs, field notes and recordings would remain with their mission records until an authorized return and debriefing made their delivery possible.

They could observe.

They could document.

They could not turn observation into permission.

### THE STUDY DESK — A FRIDAY REVIEW

Back at the compound, the trainees were given a different assignment.

The week's Eternal Dreamfall reading had reached its Friday review.

Week 98 had covered the Septuagint continuation involving Judges and Ruth.

The discussion considered two very different portraits of communal life.

Judges repeatedly presented the consequences of failed leadership, fragmented responsibility and decisions made without regard for the wider community. Ruth, by contrast, offered a story of loyalty, practical care, commitment and the restoration of security through responsible relationships.

The Legal Journalist paused at the contrast.

"One of the dangers in an organization," the journalist observed, "is believing that because someone can make a decision, that person necessarily has the right to make it."

The trainees looked back at the Kuala Lumpur file.

The connection was not that the biblical accounts provided a legal procedure for releasing archived records. They did not.

Rather, the week's reading encouraged reflection on responsibility, authority and the consequences of individual decisions for other people.

Today's YouVersion verse was also placed beside the study notes: 1 Corinthians 15:58.

Its emphasis on steadfastness and meaningful work was particularly appropriate to a case in which careful work had not yet produced a convenient answer.

The journalist closed the study folder.

"Sometimes the most responsible thing you can do is continue doing the right work without pretending that the result has already arrived."

### THE HEALTH AND SAFETY EXERCISE

The afternoon exercise came from HS1030, *Proactive Safety: Mastering Hazard Identification and Risk Management*.

The trainees were given a simulated workplace image showing an electrical extension cable crossing a pedestrian walkway.

The first question was straightforward.

What was the hazard?

The second required more thought.

What was the risk?

The journalist reminded them that a hazard is a potential source of harm, whereas risk concerns the likelihood and consequence of that harm.

They worked through the HIRA process, considered the 5×5 risk matrix and compared possible controls.

One trainee initially proposed putting up a warning sign.

The journalist asked whether the cable could simply be removed from the walkway.

The trainee looked again at the image.

"Elimination."

"Exactly. Why warn people to avoid a hazard when you can remove it?"

They entered the proposed control into the exercise's risk register.

The journalist then returned to the four notices in Kuala Lumpur.

"The conflicting directions are a potential safety hazard. But we cannot calculate the actual risk of using either route merely by looking at the photographs. We need the current building layout, relevant conditions and the responsible safety authority."

The exercise ended with a clear boundary.

No trainee was authorized to select an evacuation route for the Kuala Lumpur building.

### THE NEWSROOM — TWO COUNTRIES, TWO WARNINGS

The afternoon news review introduced two additional items.

The first concerned Ghana.

A Deloitte Ghana analysis of the 2027 Budget warned that businesses could face higher effective tax and compliance costs even without new headline taxes.

The journalist asked the trainees what would be wrong with summarizing the article as:

"No new taxes. Therefore, no additional business costs."

The answer became obvious when they considered the details.

Tax rates, exemptions, administrative requirements and enforcement obligations were different matters.

The second item concerned Malaysia.

Bernama reported that Bursa Malaysia had opened lower amid renewed global risk aversion and scrutiny of technology stocks.

The journalist placed the two headlines beside one another.

"One country is examining the implications of its budget and tax administration. Another is responding to global financial uncertainty. Neither headline tells us what happened inside our Kuala Lumpur building."

The trainees entered the articles into the research register, preserving their publishers, dates and actual claims.

The newsroom pinboard had become more useful because it was no longer simply a collection of interesting headlines.

It was a record of what could reasonably be said.

### THE R&D DISPLAY — AN UNEXPECTED CHANGE

Late in the afternoon, the Millennium Probe produced a new reading.

The historical reference remained:

**23 July 2003 — Las Vegas, Nevada.**

The new Probe reading was considerably different from the previous day's.

The console now displayed a probe timestamp in March 1980, rather than March 1994.

Its negative displacement had increased substantially.

The instrument specialist preserved the new output beside the previous snapshot.

The first question was whether the reading represented a new destination.

The second was whether it explained the team's actual position.

Neither conclusion was justified.

Kuala Lumpur remained the supplied geographic location of the deployed team, but the Probe's timestamp did not establish the team's individual temporal coordinates.

The specialist entered a new investigation item:

**PROBE DISPLACEMENT CHANGE — SOURCE SNAPSHOT PRESERVED; FIELD POSITION NOT RECONCILED.**

No recall was initiated.

No new deployment was authorized.

The instrument had changed its answer. That did not mean the team had moved.

### THE EVENING REGISTER

Before closing the day's work, the trainees reviewed the updated case register.

The revised request remained queued, with no verified transmission or receipt confirmation.

The sealed archive box remained unopened.

The authority of the person or institution entitled to request its release remained unresolved.

The four emergency notices still required comparison against the building's current layout and the applicable safety authority's instructions.

The three previously recorded declined access attempts remained under investigation. Their originating service account had not been identified.

The field team remained deployed, with no live communication or confirmed change in mission status.

The journalist reviewed the final entry.

"Have we made progress?"

The Cooperative Intern considered the question.

"We know more precisely what we don't know."

The journalist smiled.

"That is progress, provided we preserve the distinction."

Outside, the New Legon compound was settling into Friday evening. The five departmental apartment blocks stood quietly beside one another, each carrying its own responsibilities.

The field team remained somewhere in Kuala Lumpur, beyond the reach of an ordinary live conversation.

The Probe continued to calculate.

The records remained incomplete.

And the request for the sealed box was still waiting for the one thing that no stamp could manufacture:

the authority to ask.

# Part 5: The Receipt That Could Not Give Orders

*Provisional fictional manuscript for Friday, 20 November 2026. This is an editorial draft, not an approved, consent-cleared, or published portrayal.*

### 1. The word on the receipt

The machine had printed **DELIVERED** in large green letters. Beneath it, in type so small that the Cooperative Intern had to lean over the desk, the actual status read: **Accepted into outgoing queue. Awaiting recipient acknowledgement.**

“Then it hasn't been delivered,” she said.

Across the correspondence table, Michael McCauley was deciding whether he had the patience for a machine that congratulated itself before doing its work.

“Not to the recipient,” he agreed. “Only to our confidence.”

It was Friday at Koware Group's New Legon headquarters. Five single-storey, three-bedroom departmental apartment blocks shared the compound in an irregular cluster. Between two of them someone had left a bicycle across a walkway, and an industrious breeze was turning a security guard's newspaper into a very small sail. Inside, a handover was meant to be taking shape before the weekend.

The work had begun on Thursday with two deliberately separate inquiries. One concerned the contradictory emergency signs photographed at a building in Kuala Lumpur: four languages, four notices, and one arrow disagreeing with the other three. The other concerned a sealed archive box and the still-unverified identity of whoever had authority to ask that it be opened.

Neither inquiry had become permission to inspect a corridor. Neither had given Koware the authority to open the box. And at the end of Thursday, neither inquiry had a confirmed transmission receipt.

Now the printer had produced a celebratory green lie.

The Cooperative Intern set three slips on the table: the prepared safety inquiry, the prepared archival-access inquiry, and the machine receipt. She had been assigned to help Michael make the week's handover usable, not merely neat. Her first substantial decision was to put the receipt in a folder labelled **LOCAL QUEUE ONLY**.

“The template calls it successful,” said the Liaison Intern, arriving with a second tray.

“The template did not receive the letter,” she answered.

Michael looked up. “Good. Keep going.”

He was the Legal Journalist whose chapter-duty check covered both Thursday and Friday. The cooperative and liaison interns, with Liaison Personnel, were the newly foregrounded Friday roles. Michael could teach them rules; today he needed them to notice what his rules had missed.

### 2. One building, two responsibilities

By midmorning the team had separated the work into two trails that could never authenticate one another simply by sitting in the same file.

The **occupant-safety trail** asked the responsible managers of the Malaysian premises to identify the approved emergency directions, assess the inconsistent notices promptly, and communicate safe instructions through their own competent local channels. It did not prescribe a staircase from photographs or direct the Edubeam observers to test either passage.

The **archive-custody trail** asked the custodian's established channel for a verifiable access procedure and proof of who might lawfully authorize inspection. It did not nominate an authorized requester. The Thursday exterior-condition report remained exactly what it was: a description of the visible outside of a sealed container, with the sender's independent signing authority still unresolved.

Liaison Personnel, whose strength was spotting how a message might be misunderstood by someone who had not attended the discussion, read both drafts aloud.

“The safety one still says ‘please confirm the signs are safe,’” they observed. “That asks for a verdict without telling them we cannot verify a route.”

The Cooperative Intern changed it to a request for the **premises' responsible authority to assess and communicate the current approved evacuation guidance**.

“And the records one?” Michael asked.

“It says ‘we request access on behalf of the investigators.’ On behalf of whom? I would ask us for identification if I received it.”

That sentence changed too. No one pretended that Koware's wish to understand the records created a legal entitlement.

A news alert crossed the desk. Citi Newsroom reported that Ghana's Revenue Authority had found invoice-compliance irregularities at fourteen businesses at Achimota Retail Centre, including alleged use of unauthorized invoicing systems. The Liaison Intern pointed at the receipt printer.

“Even a convincing invoice has to come from an authorized system,” he said.

“And a convincing receipt,” Michael replied, “must still say what event it actually proves. We aren't investigating those businesses. We are learning why our own words matter.”

On another screen, a Malaysian news summary noted a planned early-warning-system simulation around Parliament on 23 November, with a wider test scheduled for 29 November. They did not transfer the story into an assertion that such a test had already occurred, or that it certified this particular building.

Outside, a gust pushed the security guard's newspaper into the bicycle's wheel. The guard freed it, studied his ruined fold, and muttered something about evidence requiring better custody. It was the most cheerful risk assessment of the morning.

### 3. Kuala Lumpur: what an ordinary witness can tell you

The field observers' Kuala Lumpur strand existed on a different clock from the Accra office. The current Probe offered a **March 1980 scenario**; its oscillating display was not proof that a person had arrived at that precise date, and the team had no live-stream connection to New Legon. In the provisional field scene, their observations would become a report only after a properly recorded return or permitted urgent Edubeam message.

There was no heroic music as the observers approached a row of shops. A delivery bicycle rattled past; rainwater had settled in the uneven edge of a pavement, and a boy was negotiating with an older man over the price of a newspaper as though the fate of commerce depended on a few coins. Warm air carried cooking oil, traffic exhaust and the aroma of rice wrapped for breakfast.

The team had come to understand how instructions traveled between ordinary people and official buildings in the city of that period, not to produce a retrospective verdict on any present-day emergency sign.

A radio in a shop carried a familiar official cadence. The proprietor adjusted its volume with a practiced tap. He knew which office collected a neighborhood form, which counter issued a receipt, and which clerk was likely to send a visitor elsewhere. He also knew exactly what he did not know.

“You have a question about a building?” he asked.

“About how to learn who issued an instruction,” said one observer.

“Then ask the person who keeps the register. I sell newspapers. I can tell you what people say. I cannot sign for the register.”

The distinction was not delivered as wisdom. He had an impatient customer waiting to buy cigarettes and a newspaper under his elbow.

On the wall behind him, a calendar showed the month of March. A television in the neighboring shop carried the glow of colour broadcasting—already part of Peninsular Malaysia's television experience by 1980. The team had been trained not to turn a plausible period detail into a precise date certificate. The calendar, like the radio, located a scene; it did not establish an Edubeam transfer log.

Their recorder captured the ordinary conversation and the shopfront ambience. A second observer made a small sketch of where the proprietor kept his notices: one near the till, another beside a doorway, a third folded under a stack of receipts. None was an official emergency-direction order. They were evidence of a communication culture, not evidence about the sealed box in the other case.

The team leader saw one observer staring too long at a narrow service passage behind the shops.

“Curious?” the leader asked.

“Very.”

“Then write down the question. We aren't going down an unknown passage to satisfy it.”

The observer pocketed the pencil and left the passage alone. That restraint would make for a less exciting photograph—and a more defensible report.

### 4. The reading that would not become a rubber stamp

At midday Michael asked the Cooperative Intern to place the week's Eternal Dreamfall card beside the two revised inquiries.

Its provisional assignment was Week 98 of the five-year reading map: the Septuagint tradition of *Judges* and *Ruth*. Monday through Thursday had covered four balanced portions; Friday was for reflection rather than a newly invented fifth assignment.

They had reached a troubling contrast. *Judges* showed the devastation that follows when force and conviction outrun responsible judgment. *Ruth* offered a less theatrical kind of courage: care carried through ordinary obligations, without a grand permission slip from the universe. Michael did not pretend that ancient suffering existed to furnish a tidy office comparison. He asked the interns what principle could responsibly travel into their own day.

“Knowing that a decision matters,” the Cooperative Intern said, “doesn't prove I am the right person to make it.”

“And caring about a person,” said the Liaison Intern, “might mean refusing to send them somewhere dangerous just to get an answer.”

Michael had opened the YouVersion Verse of the Day before the meeting. It was **1 Corinthians 15:58 (NIV)**, with its encouragement to stand firm and labor faithfully without imagining that good work is wasted. He considered the undelivered queue receipt, the corrected letters and the still-unanswered authority question.

“We can work steadily,” he said, “without dressing an unfinished task as a completed one.”

The intern smiled. “Even a Friday task?”

“Especially a Friday task.”

The humor lasted until the Liaison Intern raised the exact issue nobody wanted to hear.

“There's a blank in the archive letter. The system won't let me close the case without a requester name.”

“Then the case stays open,” Michael answered.

“But the weekly report will show a red box.”

“A red box is less dangerous than a false signature.”

### 5. Kuala Lumpur: the price of a confident arrow

Later in the field strand, the observers paused beneath a covered walkway while rain struck the road in sudden silver needles. The smallest of the group laughed at the way a passing bus could transform a shallow puddle into a public announcement.

A family hurried into the shelter. The father gathered shopping bags and the mother held a child's hand; the child kept pointing at a hand-painted sign farther down the street. It indicated a public counter. The arrow was clear; the lettering was less so beneath a new coat of paint.

“What does it say?” the child asked.

The mother looked once and then asked an attendant nearby. He gave directions, but he did not claim to be the officer at the counter. The parents thanked him and walked toward the entrance to confirm matters for themselves.

The team recorded the exchange as an example of everyday wayfinding, not as a test of emergency arrangements. The leader reminded the others that people often trusted signs because signs seemed impersonal. But a sign could be old, displaced, poorly translated or simply wrong. Real responsibility lived in the chain of people who could check, correct and communicate.

At a small table, one observer wrote the date shown by the local newspaper on a separate line from the date carried by the Probe. A second wrote the apparent origin of each instruction beside its exact wording. Nothing was smoothed into certainty.

Their lunch arrived wrapped in paper. Someone had ordered food too spicy for a companion and tried to conceal the mistake behind heroic composure. Within a minute the companion was red-eyed and urgently purchasing a drink.

“Log that as a hazardous substance,” the first observer whispered.

“Log it as your terrible ordering,” came the reply.

They laughed, finished the meal, and continued collecting ordinary observations. They had not opened a mystery box, inspected a staircase or spoken directly with the team in Accra. More importantly, they had not confused being present in a city with being entitled to alter it.

### 6. The two receipts

Back at New Legon, the Liaison Intern found the first real change of the day. The general correspondence service had returned an **outbound handoff confirmation** for the safety inquiry: the message had left Koware's queue and been accepted by the external delivery service. It did **not** show that the building's responsible manager had received, read or acted upon the request.

The archive letter remained in draft. The unverified **Authorized Requester** field was still blank. Nobody filled it with a convenient alias.

Michael asked the interns to make two entries in the week-end register:

**Safety inquiry — external-service handoff confirmed; recipient acknowledgement and local safety decision pending.**

**Archive-access inquiry — prepared but not submitted; requester authority unverified; sealed box unopened.**

The Cooperative Intern wrote them without abbreviating away the limits. The result was neither a complete victory nor a failure. It was a small, real improvement in what the team could responsibly claim inside its fictional case.

Then Liaison Personnel spotted a fresh problem. The exterior-condition report carried a suffix resembling the mark beneath one of the four photographed notices. But in the files before them, the cropped image had lost its upper margin. Nobody could tell whether the visible characters belonged to the notice's original printing, an annotation added later, or the photograph's own filing code.

“Shall I mark the reference as linked?” the intern asked.

Michael studied the two pages. The thought that the box might contain an explanation was almost painfully attractive.

“Mark the resemblance,” he said. “Preserve both originals as received. Request an uncropped source if an authorized person can supply one. Until then, there is no proven link.”

The Liaison Intern looked disappointed. The Cooperative Intern looked relieved.

Outside, the guard retrieved his battered newspaper. “Some good news in there?” Michael called through the window.

“Plenty of headlines,” the guard answered. “I'll tell you which ones I have actually read when I finish.”

### 7. A clock that changed, a crew that did not

Near the day's close, Enka opened the laboratory's three-column register: **Present**, **Historical reference**, **Probe calculation**.

For the present, it was Friday, 20 November 2026 in Accra. The historical service still pointed to 23 July 2003, associated in the research notes with Las Vegas. A newspaper archive from that date described plans for additional local television newscasts later in the year: a plan, not proof that those broadcasts already existed on that July morning.

The new Millennium Probe snapshot placed its calculated scenario on **18 March 1980** in Kuala Lumpur. Yesterday's source capture had displayed **August 1988**. The oscillation was striking enough to tempt a dramatic explanation and insufficient to support one. It did not send yesterday's field observers backward eight years, recover a missing report or revise anyone's arrival certificate.

“Wouldn't it be easier if the number told us where everyone was?” the Cooperative Intern asked.

“Much easier,” Enka replied. “Also much less reliable.”

They kept the field team's **collective Kuala Lumpur deployment designation** separate from the **probe's changing target time**. Individual member coordinates, a confirmed deployment timestamp and a verified return remained unavailable. Nobody in the room claimed to have received a live video feed from the team.

Enka looked at the blank return column and closed the display without filling it.

“We can prepare a retrieval check for the proper mission window,” he said. “We cannot invent the window by watching the needle move.”

### 8. What Friday could honestly finish

By evening the compound was quieter. One department's kitchen clattered with washing-up. A motorbike idled near the gate. The Cooperative Intern returned the bicycle to its proper place and checked that the walkways were clear. It was not a heroic act. It was the kind of hazard correction that did not require speculation about another country's emergency routes.

Michael gathered the team around the register for a short handover. The safety message had progressed beyond the local queue. The archive request had not. The box remained closed. The four notices still disagreed. The field crew was not declared returned. A misleading green label had been replaced by an honest sequence of delivery states.

“One thing is still bothering me,” the Liaison Intern admitted. “People want a clear answer. What if we keep refusing one?”

“Then we owe them a clear account of what we do know,” Michael said. “And we owe the people at risk the right person to answer what we don't.”

He handed the completed handover to the Cooperative Intern for a final check. She found one last phrase and crossed it out: **Case resolved for the week**. In its place she wrote: **Friday review completed; safety and custody inquiries remain open.**

Michael signed the internal review as reviewed—not as a legal authorization to enter another building, release another person's records or direct an evacuation.

The most important empty space on the form stayed empty.

A little later, in a field scene no live stream could carry home, the Kuala Lumpur observers closed their notebook against the damp air and set it safely in its case. Their experiences would matter when a properly evidenced return made a debrief possible. Until then the team in Accra would have to work with the records it genuinely had.

Somewhere between those two silences—the blank requester line in New Legon and the unread field notebook in Kuala Lumpur—the next week's problem was already waiting.

**The receipt had proved a message moved. It had not given anyone the authority to move a person.**

## Part 5: The Report That Arrived Afterward  
**Friday, 20 November 2026**

The report arrived without a voice.

It was a packet of photographs, field notes, and instrument records from a returned Edubeam team. No one at Koware could ask its authors a question in real time; the packet had travelled back with them, and the people now reviewing it had to work with what it contained.

At the New Legon Estates in Accra, Enka spread the pages across a table in one of Koware’s five adjoining, single-storey apartment blocks. Each block housed a different department. The shared boundary walls meant that a conversation in one department could sometimes be heard—unintentionally—next door.

“Then we keep the gaps visible,” Enka said, looking at a line in the report that had been underlined twice.

The line read: **Possible match. Not confirmed.**

Across the table, a colleague had drafted a summary that called the match “the missing authorization.”

Enka drew a neat line through those words.

“A resemblance is a reason to compare,” he said. “It isn’t the comparison.”

The colleague nodded, and rewrote the sentence: **A suffix resembles the mark on one of the disputed notices; the relationship is unknown.**

That was less satisfying. It was also true.

### The city in the field notes

The report included a short sequence of observations made by the previous team during its time in Kuala Lumpur. It was not a live view of the team now deployed there. It was a record returned afterward, with the original observations kept separate from later interpretation.

In the field sequence, the team moved cautiously through an unfamiliar city environment. They recorded what they could see and hear from places they were permitted to occupy: the rhythm of passing traffic, people pausing beneath a shop awning, a hand-painted sign whose lettering could not be read clearly in the image. When a sudden burst of rain sent pedestrians towards shelter, the team stepped back beneath a covered edge rather than crowding a doorway.

They did not stop anyone to ask about the archive box. They did not test a route or approach a building entrance beyond their remit. Their notes described the scene; they did not claim to explain it.

One photograph showed a narrow strip of paper caught at the edge of a display case. A note beside it read: **Could be a version mark. Scale and origin uncertain.**

The photograph’s caption had originally said **matching mark**. Someone had corrected it.

That small edit mattered. A match might suggest a conclusion. A resemblance allowed the next person to keep looking.

### Four plans and one blank

Back at the Accra table, the team compared the field packet with the unresolved case notes. Four emergency-direction notices still disagreed. A possible archive box had been located, but it remained sealed. The office still had no verified answer to the question of who could legitimately request access.

The disagreement was not solved by counting arrows, notices, or signatures.

A Liaison Assistant had prepared a form with a space for a requester’s name. The space was blank. Beside it, someone had written **Use the senior manager?** and added a question mark.

Enka read the note, then slid the form back across the table.

“Seniority might tell us who holds a senior position,” he said. “It doesn’t establish who has authority under the records process.”

“Then we ask the custodian which office is entitled to make the request,” the assistant said.

“And how we can verify that answer,” Enka replied.

The assistant added that second question. There was no dramatic breakthrough. The box remained sealed. But the new draft no longer pretended that Koware could grant itself permission.

In the neighboring block, someone dropped a stack of meeting folders. A colleague heard the thump through the shared wall and called, “Is that evidence?”

“Only that the shelf needs reviewing,” came the answer.

A laugh travelled through the doorway. For a moment, the blank requester field seemed less like a defeat and more like a useful warning: a form should not supply an authority that the evidence had not.

### Two clocks that did not become a journey

Enka carried the field packet into the review room. The Millennium Probe record showed a destination time of **18 March 1980, 02:51:32 UTC**. The historical-reference display showed **23 July 2003, 21:31:41 UTC**.

He wrote the two values on separate lines.

The Probe reading was a system output, not proof that a person had travelled, returned, or witnessed a particular event. The historical reference was a different record again. Neither explained the four notices, and neither put Enka—or any named team member—in either place.

The report from the returned team gave the reviewers something concrete to examine: an observation, a caption, and a correction. The current deployment remained separate. Its members were not appearing on a live stream, and the report did not make their whereabouts or condition newly known.

In the margin, Enka wrote: **Keep the original observation. Keep the correction. Do not turn either into a story about what we wish had happened.**

The field record offered no answer about the seal. The suffix might matter, or it might be coincidence. The requester remained unidentified. And no one had established whether the mark in the photograph and the mark on the notice shared an origin.

By the time the folders were gathered, the request had become more careful, the report had become more accurate, and the mystery had become smaller in one useful way: they could now say precisely what they did not know.

# CLASSIES CHRONICLES  
## Season 00 — Foundation Phase  
### Chapter 05 — The Signal Before Sunrise  
### Episode 03 — *The Custodian’s Key* — working title  
# Part 5: The Headline They Held Back

*Provisional fictional chronicle for Friday, 20 November 2026. Calendar authorization, character-canon approval, public-portrayal consent, and some requested research sources remain unverified. This is an editorial draft, not an accepted or published installment.*

### 1. The morning after an unfinished answer

Friday arrived with two messages still waiting to become something more.

On the correspondence desk at Koware’s New Legon headquarters, the first draft asked the building’s responsible contact to identify the person qualified to confirm current safety guidance. The second asked the records custodian who could properly request access to the sealed box.

They were separate questions. They had separate routes. Neither draft had a confirmed delivery receipt.

Michael McCauley placed them side by side, then moved them apart again.

“Why did you do that?” the Liaison Assistant asked.

“So nobody mistakes two envelopes for one authorization.”

The assistant gave him a look. “I was asking about the papers.”

“I know.”

For the first time that morning, Michael smiled before answering. He had spent yesterday correcting a sentence that claimed more authority than anyone had established. Today he meant to keep the correction from turning into a ritual of suspicion. The assistant had found the transfer reference. That work mattered, even though the draft had needed repair.

Enka Yamoah joined them with a tablet. Its screen showed three headings from the previous day’s review: **OBSERVED**, **CALCULATED**, and **CONFIRMED BY A PERSON**.

“What’s new?” Michael asked.

“Nothing confirmed,” Enka said. “That is different from nothing happening.”

He had not received a live feed from the Kuala Lumpur team. The field group could not speak with headquarters on a live stream across the Edubeam mission. Any report about their conditions had to come through the proper record or after their return—not by pretending a delayed observation was a conversation.

The assistant looked at the drafts. “If we can’t tell the team which direction to take, what can we tell them?”

“That we don’t have verified authority to choose for the building,” Michael said. “And that the people responsible for the premises must provide current guidance. We don’t turn a photograph into an emergency instruction.”

The distinction did not solve the four-sign problem. It did keep a question from becoming a command.

### 2. A view from a place headquarters could not call

The field picture on the review table was not a live window. It was a reconstruction from the Kuala Lumpur destination and the observations available to the production team—a scene imagined for the audience, not a new message from the deployed group.

In the reconstructed moment, the city moved around the observers: traffic threading through an ordinary workday, people intent on errands and appointments, buildings holding their own routines. The team stayed within its observation-only assignment. No one tested either disputed route. No one asked a passer-by to follow them. No one treated the number of signs as proof that any one instruction was right.

A field notebook recorded the useful limits: four notices were visible in the supplied materials; their directions conflicted; their issuing authority and present validity were not established. The notes did not certify the occupants’ safest route, the building’s current layout, or the condition of each team member.

That was not a dramatic discovery. It was a boundary.

At headquarters, Michael read the reconstruction card and removed a sentence that sounded as if the team had just reported in.

“We can show the audience what the assignment feels like,” Enka said. “We can’t make the scene a transmission.”

Michael nodded. “And we can’t let the audience mistake a fictional reconstruction for a field report.”

The assistant amended the card: **FIELD SCENE — FICTIONALIZED FROM THE ASSIGNMENT; NO LIVE CONTACT IMPLIED.**

It was a small edit, but it changed what the scene claimed.

### 3. Friday’s reading was a review, not a new assignment

A study card sat beside the correspondence. The Eternal Dreamfall five-year reading-plan file identified Week 98 as the Septuagint continuation covering Judges and Ruth. The weekly list did not allocate a new passage to Friday. Under the project’s stated four-day reading-week assumption, Monday through Thursday were reading days; Friday through Sunday were for review and preview.

Michael turned to the week’s notes. The difficult material in Judges could not be reduced to a tidy slogan, and Ruth’s story could not be used to claim that anyone had earned the right to decide another person’s path.

He wrote a question in the margin: **When does confidence become an excuse to take authority that belongs to someone else?**

The assistant glanced at it. “That sounds like the email again.”

“It sounds like a question worth reviewing.”

A dated search result from Christianity.com listed 2 Timothy 3:16–17 for November 20, 2026, but the requested Bible.com selection could not be independently confirmed. Michael did not label the alternate result as YouVersion’s Verse of the Day. He noted its source, then left the attribution open for editorial verification.

That restraint did not make the verse useless. Its theme of teaching and correction gave him a way to think about yesterday’s mistake without turning correction into blame. The assistant had written an overconfident phrase; Michael had caught it; Michael could also acknowledge that he had not made the authority boundary clear enough.

“Let’s keep the correction in the document,” he said. “Not attach a person’s name to it as if one person created the whole problem.”

The assistant revised the note and added the missing source caveat.

### 4. A number is not a journey

At the laboratory console, Enka compared the historical reference with the Probe’s calculated destination.

The historical clock showed **23 July 2003**, associated in the supplied production notes with Las Vegas, Nevada. The Probe record showed **18 March 1980**, in a scenario associated with Kuala Lumpur. Neither timestamp proved that a particular person had stood at either location. The Probe’s decimals could describe a calculation; they could not sign a visitor into a building.

“Could the dates be connected?” the assistant asked.

“They can be compared,” Enka replied. “That is not the same as proving a connection.”

He preserved the captured values instead of overwriting them with a cleaner summary. The Probe’s current-time field, phase label and boundary dates remained attached to the same fallback snapshot. He marked the destination as a Probe scenario, not verified future or historical journalism.

On the board, Michael added a fourth heading: **INTERPRETATION — NOT ESTABLISHED**.

The assistant looked at the four headings. “We’re making the board bigger.”

“We’re making the claims smaller,” Michael said.

“That seems less exciting.”

“Only if you think the point is to fill every blank.”

### 5. The headline that waited

Later, the Legal Journalist’s pinboard received a proposed current-news item with a confident title and no independently checked article behind it. The headline looked polished. Its date and location were not enough to prove it was accurate.

The assistant reached for a marker. “Should I put it under Ghana, Malaysia or world?”

“Not yet,” Michael said.

The assistant paused. “So we’re not reporting it?”

“We’re recording that it needs a source. Those are different things.”

The proposed item went beneath a new label: **UNVERIFIED — DO NOT PRESENT AS NEWS**. No real-world claim was attached to the Kuala Lumpur investigation, the sealed box, or Koware’s fictional work. The research review also found no verified day-specific headline for Enka’s July 2003 Las Vegas reference or the March 1980 Probe scenario.

The board looked less complete. It was more honest.

The office produced its own small interruption. A stack of draft forms slid sideways as someone tried to make room for the pinboard notes. The assistant caught them before they reached the floor; one page landed upside down across the two outgoing-message folders.

“Which one is that?” Enka asked.

The assistant turned it over. It was the field marked **REQUESTER**.

Still blank.

No one filled it in simply to make the page look finished.

### 6. The sentence they could defend

Before the end of the workday, Michael read both proposed inquiries aloud. The first asked the responsible local contact to identify the official who could confirm current safety guidance for the premises. The second asked the custodian to state who could submit an authorized records request and what evidence of that authority was required.

Neither nominated a route. Neither claimed that the box had been opened. Neither identified a requester whose authority had not been established. Their preparation did not prove that they had been transmitted or acknowledged.

Enka kept the two clock records beside them: historical reference, Probe calculation. The team’s Kuala Lumpur designation remained distinct from the physical position or condition of any individual member. Nothing in the records established a return or an extraction.

Michael read the last line on the board: **The right to ask is not the right to enter—and neither is the right to tell people where to run.**

“Should we add a date?” the assistant asked.

“Only when we know what the date belongs to.”

The assistant set down the marker.

The investigation had not found the issuing authority. It had not authenticated the original instruction, reconciled it with the building as it stood, or established who could request release of the sealed record. The team’s observation-only boundary remained in place.

But one thing had changed at headquarters: an unverified headline would no longer become a fact just because it fit the space on the board.

# Part 5: The Reply Meant for Someone Else

*Provisional draft. Editorial and portrayal-rights review pending; not an approved or published instalment.*

### 1. One reply, two acknowledgements

The custodian’s answer reached Koware at four minutes past eight, and it contained two acknowledgements that could not both be true.

Yaw Kweku, the Liaison Personnel, carried the printout into reception the way other people carry a tray of full glasses: slowly, elbows in. The striped canvas sling across his chest bulged with the week’s handover receipts. Somewhere inside it, as always, a small private weather of binder clips rattled.

“Two attachments,” he said. “Both say *Acknowledgement*. Both carry the transfer reference from the archive box. They disagree.”

It was Friday, the half-day, when the New Legon blocks ran on a strict ration of hours: nine until two, then the weekend. Michael McCauley had come in early to turn the reception pinboard into something a visitor could read without a guide. He was still holding a fistful of pins and a half-finished cup of tea.

Efua Nara, the Cooperative Intern, was already writing in the ink-stained spiral journal that doubled as the office’s decision log: *Friday. 08:04. Custodian’s reply received. Two attachments. Entry by E. N.* At the corner terminal Sena Teki, the Liaison Intern, clicked her lavender pen once, twice, and stopped.

The covering note was courteous and short. Koware’s inquiry about who might request the records had been received. Eligibility would be considered, but any request would need written evidence of the applicant’s authority and a statement of purpose. No access was granted. Then came the sentence everyone read twice: *This is the first communication on record concerning this transfer reference.*

The first attachment was addressed to Koware’s registered channel, dated Thursday, and said exactly that in a more formal typeface.

The second attachment should not have been there.

It carried the same reference and the same crest. Its date was 6 November, thirteen days earlier, and its heading said that a request concerning the reference had been *received*. In the box marked *Applicant of record*, a thick black bar lay across the text.

Michael read the heading, then the date. Then he closed the file the way a person closes a door that has opened onto somebody else’s bedroom.

“That isn’t ours.”

“It’s attached to ours,” Yaw said.

“A letter delivered to the wrong house is still addressed to somebody.”

Across the room the lavender pen had gone silent. Sena sat with the stillness of someone who has just heard a glass break in another room.

“Pastor,” she said. “I have to tell you something. Please let me finish before you say anything kind.”

### 2. Thirty minutes slow

In Kuala Lumpur it was Tuesday, the eighteenth of March, 1980, and the clocks of the peninsula were wrong in the most official way possible.

Peninsular Malaysia kept a time seven and a half hours ahead of Greenwich: half an hour behind Sabah and Sarawak, and half an hour out of step with the sun, a custom that would last until the final night of 1981. Every clock on the street agreed about it with perfect confidence. At twenty-one minutes past ten the radio in the coffee shop on the corner said so, and so did the wall clock above the till, and the old man under the wall clock, and the boy delivering blocks of ice by bicycle.

The liaison technician’s wrist said fifty-one minutes past.

“Fast,” the old man said, without looking at it. “Everybody who comes from outside has a fast watch.”

“We’re from outside,” the team lead agreed, which was true in more directions than the old man could have counted.

There were four of them at two iron tables on the five-foot way: a team lead, a records analyst, a liaison technician and a safety observer. They carried nothing a 1980 pocket could not explain, apart from one thing the records analyst kept folded inside his jacket and had not once taken out. Across the road a block of pale concrete stood with its bamboo scaffolding half stripped away, the kind of building that smells of wet paint for a month after the tenants arrive. A signwriter’s van was parked at its door with the back open on racks of narrow painted panels, drying.

On the table lay a newspaper borrowed for the price of a cup of coffee. Its front page was still digesting the Chinese foreign minister’s tour of the region: his remarks about Malaysian rubber and palm oil, and the claim that China’s purchases had tilted the trade balance in Malaysia’s favour for the first time. The old man read the headline aloud to nobody in particular, with the air of a person who has been given good news by a source he does not entirely trust.

“Everybody wants our rubber,” he said. “Until the price falls.”

The team lead stirred her coffee. The rules of the work were simple, and she said them aloud at the start of every field day: they were observers. They drank the coffee. They listened. They did not ask about the building, and they did not promise anybody anything, because a promise from a visitor was the one thing a place could not absorb.

The records analyst’s eyes drifted to the racks of panels. “Not yet,” the team lead said quietly, without turning her head, and he went back to his coffee.

The liaison technician slid a thumbnail under the crown of his watch, pulled it out, and took thirty minutes off the day. The old man watched the procedure with the quiet satisfaction of someone whose clock has been vindicated.

### 3. What Sena had seen

“When the file opened,” Sena said, “I did what I always do. I selected the whole page.”

Nobody spoke. Yaw had stopped rattling.

“It’s a habit. Some people check a pocket for their keys; I check a page for what’s hiding in it. The black bar had been drawn over the text, not through it. The name was underneath. I read it before I decided to.” She turned her lavender pen end over end. “I haven’t written it down. For four minutes it sat on my clipboard, and then I pasted a blank over it. It’s still in my head, and I can’t make it leave.”

Michael McCauley said nothing for as long as the pastor in him allowed a hard sentence to finish landing.

“Thank you for telling me before I asked,” he said.

“It isn’t a gift,” Sena said. “It’s a leak.”

Efua Nara had already leaned forward, journal open, eyebrows doing what they did when she could see a good outcome and somebody’s discomfort in the same glance. “Then we know something. We could ask the building contact whether the name is theirs. We could ask the Corporate Secretary’s office whether anyone has dealt with the custodian before. And if more of us know, it isn’t one person carrying it alone—”

“No,” Yaw said.

Everyone turned. Yaw Kweku, who could reconcile two colleagues for an hour without once taking a side, had never in anyone’s memory said that word to Efua.

“Not because you’re wrong that it’s heavy,” he said. His voice had gone quiet and level, the way a drawer closes when it is pushed in properly. “But everybody knowing isn’t the same as everybody allowed. If we spread it so it’s lighter, all we’ve done is make it ours.”

Efua opened her mouth, closed it, and nodded slowly, with the expression of someone hearing her own habit described kindly.

“Four things,” Michael said. He did not raise his voice. “First, Sena, you tell nobody the name. Not Efua, not Yaw, and not me. Second, you write a liaison note that records only what happened: the time, how the page was opened, who was told that an unintended disclosure occurred. Not the content. Third, attachment two goes into quarantine, closed, until the custodian tells us what to do with it. Fourth, we write to the custodian today and tell them their redaction failed.”

“It could tell us who else wants the box,” Sena said. Her voice cracked on the last word. “It could save the inquiry a week.”

“It could.” Michael did not look away. “It’s also the one thing in this building I’m certain we weren’t given. Seeing isn’t the same as being shown.” He stopped, because the sentence had come out more elegantly than he’d meant and he distrusted elegance. “Say that to me again in a month, if it still sounds easy.”

Efua wrote in the decision log: *Decision. Quarantine attachment 2. Notify custodian of failed redaction. No use of content. No onward disclosure. Evidence: attachment headers only (reference, date, heading). Handover recipient: M. McCauley; Monday steward.* She underlined *No onward disclosure* twice and, because she was honest, wrote in the margin: *Efua tried to start one. Yaw stopped it.*

Sena wrote her liaison note without once using the name. It took her four attempts to produce a sentence that was not an accusation against herself.

Edliex, the office’s AI, which had listened to the whole exchange without volunteering a word, offered a single observation when Michael invited it. “Three people have now been told that a name exists,” it said. “Nobody has been told the name. Record that distinction exactly as it stands. On Monday it will be the difference between a mistake and a leak.”

“Thank you, Edliex. Efua, put that in the log.”

She already had.

Yaw found the building’s reply in the same sweep, where it had spent the night beneath an automatic message about office hours. It was shorter than the custodian’s and a great deal clearer. The building’s own safety officer had read Koware’s inquiry. Interim directions for occupants had been posted at the stairwell entrances on the officer’s authority, and the panel with the odd fourth arrow had been covered pending review. A formal correction would follow. Koware was asked, politely and without ambiguity, not to circulate the photographs of the notices as though they were guidance.

Michael read it twice for the sheer pleasure of it. Somebody with the right to decide where people should run had decided.

“Red ink,” he said. Efua found the stamp, and every printed photograph in the dossier received the same words in its top right corner: *NOT GUIDANCE*. The stamp hit the paper with a very satisfying thud.

### 4. The small avalanche

By ten the letters were drafted, the quarantine tray was sealed, and the office had the brittle brightness of a place that has survived a morning.

Yaw was crossing reception with the revised notices pressed to his chest when the buckle of his canvas sling gave way. The strap sagged, the flap burst, and the week’s supply of binder clips left the bag in a single silver cascade.

They hit the tiles like hail. Several hundred small steel bows skated outwards in every direction, and Michael McCauley, emerging from the meeting room with a pinboard header in one hand and a fresh cup of tea in the other, set his full weight on the first of them.

What followed lasted about three seconds and would be retold, with improvements, for weeks. Michael glided past the visitors’ bench with his arms out and his expression composed into a great and quiet dignity. He passed the pinboard, which reached for him. He executed a half turn no one could have rehearsed, found a second clip with the other foot, and stopped, upright, tea level, in the middle of the foyer, in the attitude of a man who has just learned that the floor has become a matter of opinion.

Nobody breathed.

“Nobody move,” Efua said, in the voice she kept for other people’s safety. “Near miss.”

“I am perfectly aware,” Michael said, without moving his lips.

Sena laughed first, then Yaw, helplessly, in the middle of his scattered clips, and then Michael himself, a short undignified wheeze of relief, and the tea sloshed in the cup and, miraculously, stayed.

Efua did not laugh until the floor was safe. She was already on her knees at the edge of the spill with her journal under one arm, sweeping clips into a tray with the side of her hand and talking in short sentences she plainly did not mind saying aloud. *Spot it. Judge it. Fix the cause. Tell the next shift.* The spill, she said, was not the hazard; the spill was the symptom. The hazard was a buckle that had been failing all week, and a bag carried at speed across a hard floor. The clips went into a lidded box. The sling went to be mended. The new rule went into the log with a name and a date.

“Why that order?” Yaw asked, a little humbled.

“Because the quickest answer is always the first one,” Efua said. “You pick them up. And then the buckle is still broken, and tomorrow someone steps on something else.”

Michael sat on the visitors’ bench to let his pulse catch up and wrote the incident into the near-miss register: *Reception floor. Small objects underfoot. No injury.* He decided not to record that he had looked graceful.

What he did not write was that all morning he had been wanting to fix a symptom, a name sitting in a young woman’s memory, and that Efua’s rule had landed on him a little harder than the clips had. The leak was not Sena’s cause. The cause was a black bar drawn over text instead of through it. His letter to the custodian would say so, plainly and kindly, and in that order.

### 5. Looking back, looking ahead

Fridays belonged to review. The Eternal Dreamfall reading plan gave the working week four days of reading and kept the other three for looking back and looking ahead, and Michael had always thought the proportion wiser than it looked.

He took his worn notebook to the smaller meeting room. The week had been *Judges* and *Ruth* in the Greek Septuagint tradition. He had spent its early days inside a book whose refrain returns like a bell at the end of nearly every disaster: in those days there was no king in Israel, and everyone did what was right in their own eyes. It was not a boast. It was a diagnosis. When each person is the final authority on what is right, the weakest in the room pay the bill.

Then, on Thursday, came *Ruth*, and an act of law that looked almost ordinary beside the chaos before it. In the fourth chapter a man takes his case to the city gate, sits down in public among ten elders as witnesses, and offers a nearer relative the first chance to act before he acts himself. Nothing is taken. Nothing is hidden. Authority is exercised in the open and in order, and out of that slow, witnessed process something kind is made.

Beside Thursday’s question, *Does a person become entitled to act merely by being convinced they are right?*, he wrote today’s addition: *And does a person become entitled to know merely by being able to?*

Sena found him there, her lavender pen limp in her hand.

“I think I’ve wasted the best thing I found all week,” she said. “It’s the only real lead anyone has had, and I can’t use it. I can’t even unremember it. It feels like I did all that work for nothing.”

He turned to the verse he had copied that morning. Today’s Verse of the Day on the YouVersion page came from 1 Corinthians 15: stand firm, let nothing move you, give yourself fully to the Lord’s work, because you know that your labour in the Lord is not in vain.

“Read it as a labourer,” he said, “not as a detective. It doesn’t say your finding wasn’t in vain. It says your labour wasn’t.”

“My labour was *looking*.”

“Your labour this morning was telling me before I asked. That wasn’t looking. That was standing firm.” He tapped the page. “It isn’t a verse about winning. It’s a verse about work nobody sees finished.”

Sena was quiet for a moment. “And next week?”

He smiled. The plan’s week beginning on the twenty-second moved on to *1 Kingdoms*, the Greek Septuagint name for the book English readers call First Samuel, and he had read ahead, because he always did. A boy, a voice in the night, and a repeated mistake: Samuel hears his name called three times and runs three times to the wrong person, because he cannot know whose voice it is. Only when an older man recognises what is happening does the boy learn what to say.

“A voice with no verified source,” Sena said.

“And a boy who would rather ask three times than guess once.” Michael closed the notebook. “I’ll take that as a preview.”

### 6. A ladder on polished floor

By midday the road in Kuala Lumpur had filled with lunch. Pots rang. A radio in an upstairs window argued with a radio in the window opposite. The signwriter’s van was unloading again, and the four observers had moved to a bench across from the new building, where they could be four people with sandwiches rather than four people with a purpose.

The foreman wore a pencil behind each ear. He shouted in Malay to the apprentice, in English to the van, and in a third language, made entirely of gestures, to the man holding the tin. The apprentice was a thin boy of perhaps seventeen, perched near the top of a wooden ladder in the lobby doorway, reaching out to set a panel above the stairwell. The ladder’s feet stood on terrazzo the cleaners had polished that morning to a shine you could have shaved in.

The safety observer saw it the way a person trained for a lifetime to see sees a wasp: before deciding to. The left foot of the ladder was already moving.

She was across the road before anyone had finished the thought. The records analyst said her name sharply. The team lead, after one calculation, said nothing at all.

The ladder slid. Not far—a hand’s breadth, then a second—with the slow, inevitable grace of a thing that has made up its mind. The apprentice’s arms flew out. A tin of black enamel left his belt and hung in the air for one impossible second before bouncing down the stairs like a drum.

The safety observer reached the ladder on the third hand’s breadth. She put both hands on the rails and her weight into the foot, and said in her plainest Malay: “Steady. I have it.”

The boy came down rung by rung, grey in the face, and apologised to the stairs. The foreman arrived at a run. Nobody was hurt. The tin lay at the bottom of the stairwell, drawing a long black comma across the terrazzo.

“Sorry, miss,” the foreman said, mopping his forehead. “Floor’s like glass. Everybody says put something under the feet and nobody has time.”

“A folded sack,” she said. “Wet it. It grips.”

He grunted, then laughed, then did it. The ladder stood on its damp sack as steady as a pillar. The safety observer walked back across the road with her hands shaking slightly, sat on the bench, and said nothing to the others for a while.

“We don’t intervene,” the records analyst said at last, gently.

“We don’t change outcomes,” the team lead corrected. “We don’t pick winners. We also don’t watch a boy fall off a ladder for the sake of a tidy report.” She took a long breath. “We’ll write down exactly what she did, how long it took and that it was small. If the next team reads it and thinks we were wrong, they’ll say so. I would rather be told.”

Up on the ladder the apprentice had resumed work with an expression of enormous concentration and a new respect for the floor. The foreman held a sign plan on a clipboard, the kind with columns for each panel: location, wording, direction. At the bottom of the sheet was a box marked *Approved*, with a line beneath it. The line was empty.

“The fourth one’s a different run,” the foreman told the boy, in the tone of a man repeating something he has said before. “The office changed the stairs again. They telephoned. Write it the way I tell you. They’ll sign when the stairs are finished.”

The records analyst did not look up from his sandwich. He kept absolutely still, which was how he listened.

The boy painted the fourth arrow pointing left, where the other three pointed right. Afterwards the foreman said, “Put a mark on it, so the office knows it’s the later one,” and the boy, with his own idea of what a mark should look like, lettered a short run of characters in the bottom corner with the tip of his smallest brush.

The analyst took a laminated card from his inside pocket, turned it face-down on his knee, and glanced at it once in the shadow of the bench. A tiny sequence ran along the lower edge of the fourth photograph. He looked across the road at the new panel.

“Resembles,” he said, very quietly, to no one.

“Resembles what?” the technician murmured.

“I can’t say *matches*. Different paint, a different year, perhaps a different hand. A mark that small, I’d want to see in daylight, under a glass, with someone who can tell me whose hand it is.” He turned the card back over. “It resembles.”
