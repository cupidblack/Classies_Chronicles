### Chapter 05 — The Signal Before Sunrise
### Episode 03 — The Custodian's Key (provisional working title)
## Part 4: The Name That Could Not Be Borrowed

The message was ready to leave Accra. It had a reference number, a courteous closing, and one dangerous verb.

**AUTHORISE.**

Pastor Michael McCauley put one hand on the edge of the Liaison Assistant's desk before the message could be sent.

“Who authorised it?”

The assistant looked at the line. “We did. Well—the form says we did.”

“That is not the same thing.”

The draft concerned an archive box in a building in Kuala Lumpur. Koware's deployed observers had traced the box while investigating four emergency-direction notices, printed in four languages, whose arrows did not agree. A record suggested that the original signs and their revisions might be explained by material inside the box. The box was sealed. Nobody in the Koware team had permission to open it.

The message, however, read: *Inspection authorised, pending condition verification.*

Michael struck out the first two words. “An organisation can be very good at arranging a question,” he said, “and still have no authority to issue the answer.”

The assistant read the sentence again. “That sounds like the opening of a sermon.”

“It is the closing of an unsendable email.”

### 1. Four arrows and a fifth instruction

It was Thursday, 19 November 2026, at the Koware headquarters in New Legon, Accra. The Legal Journalist had come in early because the building custodian's promised condition report had not appeared in the verified correspondence register. The Liaison Officer worked beside the assistant, checking whether the delay belonged to the sender, the transmission channel, or the receiving desk. None of those explanations could yet be chosen.

On the wall, a reproduction of the Kuala Lumpur notices was pinned beneath a large word: **OBSERVATIONS**. Three arrows pointed towards one route; the fourth pointed elsewhere. Their authority was still disputed. No one at Koware was treating the arrows as an approved evacuation plan.

“Could we just ask the field team to check the fourth corridor?” the Liaison Assistant asked.

Michael looked up. “Not to test whether it is safe. They can report what is visible from a place they are authorised to occupy. Any uncertainty affecting occupants goes to the building's responsible safety personnel. A doubtful exit is not a puzzle for visitors to solve with their feet.”

The Liaison Officer added a separate line to the outgoing inquiry: *Please identify the currently authorised building safety contact and the verified guidance applicable to occupants. The observer team will not independently test the disputed route.*

It was a small addition. It changed the immediate mission. A request for a historic file was no longer allowed to distract from a present question about safe instructions.

### 2. The reading that refused an easy hero

Michael had carried his Eternal Dreamfall notes into the office. The week's reading concerned the Greek Septuagint tradition of *Judges* and *Ruth*. Thursday's provisional allocation was *Judges* 16–20: Samson's loss of judgment and strength, competing claims to religious legitimacy, and increasingly terrible communal decisions. The later chapters included grave violence; Michael refused to use the suffering of victims as a convenient metaphor for office paperwork.

Instead he wrote a harder question on the margin of his notes: *What happens when people call a choice right because nobody has stopped them?*

“Does anyone in those chapters simply become right by being confident?” the assistant asked.

“No. And their confidence often makes the damage worse.”

“That would make an inconvenient company slogan.”

“Then don't put it on a poster.”

He had also seen the day's YouVersion reading, 1 Thessalonians 5:16–18 (NIV). Its charge to rejoice, pray, and **“give thanks in all circumstances”** unsettled him in a useful way. He was irritated by the unanswered message; gratitude was not an instruction to pretend the delay was good. He could be grateful that nobody had acted on an unverified order. He could also work to end the uncertainty.

He reopened the draft instead of blaming the assistant for almost sending it. Together they turned a declaration into three questions: who owned the archive, who could request access, and who was authorised to confirm a safe route today?

The assistant's shoulders relaxed. It was possible, Michael realised, to correct a mistake without making someone afraid to report the next one.

### 3. A low number with a large shadow

In a separate room, Enka studied the Millennium Probe's record alongside the historical-reference clock. The present production date was November 2026. The historical display pointed to July 2003, associated with Las Vegas, Nevada. The probe displayed a reference in March 1994 and a negative displacement of roughly eleven thousand nine hundred days. Its phase label read October 16 through December 20; its oscillation index was small, its reported amplitude large.

Those values were precise outputs of a fictional experimental system. They were not a passenger list.

Enka's physical position for the working story was New Legon. The deployed team's supplied field location remained Kuala Lumpur. The Las Vegas entry was a historical reference, not evidence that Enka had gone there; the probe's 1994 date was not evidence that anyone had travelled into the past.

A new entry appeared on the lab's story-board: *Physical return of any named field member — unverified.*

The Liaison Officer arrived with the revised archive inquiry.

“We found the missing authority?” Enka asked.

“We found that we were about to invent it.”

Enka read the corrections and approved the limited question for fictional dispatch, not the opening of the box. Then he noticed a smaller note: *Current evacuation guidance requested independently.*

“That stays ahead of the old box,” he said.

The lab had been tempted to treat a calculated coordinate as a completed journey. The office had been tempted to treat a properly formatted message as permission. Different instruments; the same temptation.

### 4. The paper bird

At the correspondence desk, a small calamity interrupted the solemnity.

The Liaison Assistant tried to weigh down the drafts with an empty stapler. The stapler sprang open, knocked a folder upright, and sent a loose paper cover gliding across the desk like a determined white bird. It landed face down in Michael's tea saucer.

Nobody moved for a full second.

Then Michael lifted the wet cover by two dry corners and read its title: **FINAL APPROVAL**.

“Excellent,” he said. “Our approval has gone for a swim.”

Even the Liaison Officer laughed. The actual records were dry; the saucer had caught only a duplicate cover sheet. They discarded the damaged sheet, checked the originals, moved drinks away from the documents and placed the drafts in a lidded tray. The assistant recorded the mishap as an office near miss, not as evidence about Kuala Lumpur.

“It's amazing,” the assistant said, “how many problems begin with something labelled final.”

Michael was still smiling when the verified channel signalled a new attachment.

### 5. What a condition report can say

It was shortly before nine in Accra, late afternoon in Kuala Lumpur. The incoming attachment described the **outside** of the located archive container. The sender identified a custodian's inspection, an intact visible seal, and no apparent exterior damage at the time of that inspection. A document identifier accompanied the report.

The Liaison Officer compared the registered sender information with the existing correspondence channel and logged receipt. Michael read the report twice. Then he called Enka.

“We have a condition report,” Michael said.

“Has it been authenticated?”

“The document came through the channel on record. Its claimed authorship and the authority of the signatories still need the ordinary custodian-side confirmation. Even if those checks pass, it establishes the condition of the outside. It does not establish what is inside—or who may open it.”

“Good. Record exactly that.”

The Liaison Assistant, seeing the unbroken seal mentioned in the text, reached for a green marker to colour the archive-box entry *CLEARED*.

Michael caught the marker halfway across the table.

“Use amber,” he said. “Received is not cleared.”

The assistant drew a narrow amber stripe beside the file. Under it they entered four states: *report received; outward condition described; signatory authority not independently confirmed; access permission not granted.*

The room grew quiet. They had gained information and, at the same time, had kept the limits of that information intact.

### 6. The public story on another pinboard

Another clipping hung beside the case board: a Malaysian report published in October about fire-safety-manager appointments due to begin for designated buildings in April 2027. It raised a sensible question about who should manage a building's fire arrangements. It did not tell the team whether this particular building belonged to that category, or who currently held authority over its notices.

From Accra, a November news item about Ghana's 2027 economic programme and the promised role of monitoring institutions was also circulating. To Michael it suggested an analogy, not evidence: an announced plan and a measured result belonged in different columns.

He moved both items to a separate board headed **BACKGROUND — NOT CASE EVIDENCE**.

There was an almost painful satisfaction in doing it. On the main board, the case remained smaller than the headlines around it. But what remained on the main board could be defended.

### 7. A name missing on purpose

Before the Malaysian office closed, the field contact passed on one more observation. The archive's transfer record had a reference suffix that resembled a small version mark visible near the fourth emergency notice. The resemblance was suggestive; the images had not been authenticated as originals, and the transfer record gave no explanation of the mark.

The Liaison Assistant leaned forward. “That means they're connected.”

“It means they may be,” Michael answered. “Don't let one resemblance turn into a biography.”

“Could we send a person to compare the originals?”

“Only after the custodian identifies who can authorise access and the building's safety arrangements permit it.”

The contact in Kuala Lumpur confirmed that the team would remain in its observation role. No box was opened, no disputed route tested, and no field member was recorded as returned to Accra. The group location remained Kuala Lumpur; individual attendance and return evidence would require their own checks.

Enka listened to the exchange from the New Legon room. What he liked least was how little it sounded like a victory.

Yet the afternoon's outcome had a shape. One incorrect instruction had been stopped. One useful condition report had arrived without being promoted beyond its meaning. The authorised person had not been guessed into existence. The present safety question had been routed to people positioned to answer it.

The Liaison Assistant placed the completed draft in the outgoing tray. Above the space where someone had originally written *Authorised Requester*, the amended communication now asked:

*Which office, under the current records rules, may legitimately request access, and how can that authority be independently verified?*

Michael read the sentence and nodded.

The box remained sealed. The arrows still disagreed. The version mark had acquired a possible partner but not an explanation.

For once, the blank space on the form was the most truthful thing in the room.
