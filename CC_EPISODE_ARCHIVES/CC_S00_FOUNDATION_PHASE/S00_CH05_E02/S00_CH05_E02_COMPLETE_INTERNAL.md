## Chapter 05 — The Signal Before Sunrise
## Episode 02 — The Authority Behind the Arrow

*Eight parts. Ten working days. One unanswered instruction.*

### Prologue — When the arrow moved

In Kuala Lumpur, four notices beside a lift gave an instruction that should have been impossible to misunderstand. Malay, English and Mandarin directed people toward one stairwell. The Tamil notice pointed elsewhere. It sat slightly lower on the wall and carried a date along its bottom edge. The paper looked ordinary. The implication did not.

A photograph crossed time zones and reached a laboratory screen in New Legon Estates, Accra. It showed the four notices in one frame. A second photograph, already circulating elsewhere, showed only three.

An arrow had not physically moved. The story around it had.

At Koware Group, an investigation would come to concern more than a building. It would draw in researchers, safety specialists, concierges, registrars, operations managers, journalists and programmers; it would expose how easily an institution could turn a partial record into a confident instruction. And it would unfold alongside a more unsettling puzzle: a Millennium Probe whose future dates continued to change, even when its recorded inputs had not been forgotten.

Enka had one instruction for everyone involved: **do not turn a thing observed into a thing authorized merely because it is convenient.**

---

## Part 1: The Notice in Four Languages

The morning briefing began with the rustle of dry leaves between Koware's five single-storey apartment blocks. Each three-bedroom block housed a department; the short paths linking them made meetings easy and privacy harder. Beyond the New Legon Estates compound, Accra was already moving. Within it, an entire international assignment had narrowed to four laminated sheets.

Gifty Bright, Director of Research & Development, examined the enlarged corridor image while Obed Ali, the R&D Lab Specialist, arranged the source files by acquisition time. Angie Sylvia, the Liaison Technician, took down the field team's last verified message. On the adjacent monitor, Enka watched from a workstation and kept three notebooks open: the present operation, the historical reference and the Millennium Probe.

“Which arrow is correct?” someone asked.

“That question is too early,” Gifty said. “First, which image is complete?”

Obed enlarged the bottom edge of the fourth notice. It showed a printed date but not a clearly legible approving signature. The team could establish what appeared in the photographs. It could not establish that the notice represented current emergency instructions.

The Kuala Lumpur observers had been assigned to document the situation, not to evacuate the building, test stairwells or replace its safety notices. Their transmitted location was a field-position reference, not proof that a timestamp from the Probe put them in any particular century. They photographed the notices in context and withdrew from the disputed direction signs rather than treating one as a navigation command.

At first, Obed tried to make his argument with a three-column chart. He set a heavy binder on the edge of a wheeled stool; the stool quietly slid away and left him standing, pointing at a blank wall while his audience stared at the chart on the other side of the room. He recovered with dignity that fooled nobody. The room laughed before returning to the evidence.

Then Enka brought the discussion back. Earlier, his Eternal Dreamfall study had revisited the Septuagint—the long history of carrying sacred writing into another language. The study's enduring lesson was not that every translation was suspect. It was that words, source and meaning must be held together; multiplying versions does not automatically multiply authority. Enka paired that reflection with Psalm 119:11, which speaks of keeping a teaching inwardly rather than merely displaying it.

“This is not four votes,” he told the room. “It may be one instruction reproduced four times, or several versions produced under different rules. Before we trust any direction, we need the originating instruction and the present safety authority.”

The Legal Journalist pinned a factual note in the reception area: **KUALA LUMPUR OBSERVATION — CONFLICTING DIRECTIONS; AUTHORIZATION UNCONFIRMED.** It had a date and source status, not a sensational conclusion. Staff passing toward their separate workstations paused to read it. A story that might have vanished in an email had become a shared institutional question.

Edliex stayed in listening mode through the first discussion. When Enka invited its feedback, the AI did not choose a stairwell. It separated the record into observation, interpretation and authority, then asked for the uncropped original, issue history, translated variants and a custodian who could answer for the building's current arrangement. In conversational mode it added, “Precision describes a reading. It does not prove the claim we attach to the reading.”

The first challenge card was filed under **LANGUAGE**. Three agreeing directions were still not a verified route. For the next step, the team needed the part of the record that everyone wanted to cut away.

## Part 2: The Cropped Truth

On Tuesday, a screenshot arrived with a neat border and a persuasive caption: *Emergency instructions verified in three languages.* The fourth notice was outside the crop. The corridor itself had not changed; the viewer's evidence had.

Josephine Ohemeng, the Human & Employment Resources Manager, had asked Joycelyn Ennison, the Health & Safety Technician, to review communication hazards in the workplace. The case was ideal and disturbing. If a translation dropped a condition, if a screenshot removed a contradictory direction, or if staff confused distribution with approval, a bad instruction might travel faster than its correction.

Joycelyn placed the full image and the screenshot side by side. “The correct response isn't to let our observer decide which way to run,” she said. “A conflicting safety notice is a hazard to report through the building's responsible safety personnel. The observer preserves evidence and stays within the approved task.”

At the back of the room, Pauline Mbuso and Cornelius Jekora were comparing what the problem meant for their own work. Neither cared about a grand theory of temporal mechanics as much as whether instructions were legible to the people expected to follow them. Cornelius added that a rumor about rising fares on a busy commute could spread in the same way: someone copied the price but left off the route. Pauline countered that the opposite happened too—people saw a familiar route and assumed an old fare still applied. Neither offered a rumor as news.

As Cornelius collected the demonstration notices, the tape holding an example to the table detached and followed him halfway across the room, trailing a ridiculous paper tail from his shoe. Pauline caught it before he reached the doorway. The laughter helped the room remember the lesson: an unnoticed attachment can travel.

In the afternoon, Enka contacted the building manager through the authorized channel and requested an origin trace: who drafted the instruction, who commissioned the translations, who approved display, and whether residents had received a matching message in another medium. The manager did not confirm the issuing authority. He agreed to check the distribution history.

Meanwhile, a copy of the cropped screenshot was reported to have been forwarded beyond its original recipient. The team could not know how many people had seen it. Enka resisted a public correction that itself might repeat an unverified route; the message to the manager instead requested that the responsible building safety team assess the discrepancy and issue verified guidance through its own procedures.

The second challenge card was filed under **FRAME**. It could be unlocked only by comparing what the image showed with what the image excluded. By evening, the concern was no longer merely that a line might be mistranslated. It was that the partial truth had already acquired the appearance of completeness.

## Part 3: The Missing Approval Line

On Wednesday, a distribution register arrived. The four copies had been installed; the record gave quantities and dispatch steps. It was a modest victory—until Isaac Barry, the Technical Support & Operations Manager, asked the question nobody could find on the form.

“Where is the approval of the instruction itself?”

Bonsu Bolt, the Concierge Liaison, had arranged a safe visitor-access check at the New Legon reception point. Jacob Buckman reviewed whether the welcome and direction signs could be understood by someone arriving for the first time. Byron O'Laughlin added a different concern: people who were anxious, unfamiliar with a building or dependent on clear support could be especially vulnerable to ambiguous signage. None of these roles had authority to certify a Kuala Lumpur escape route.

Isaac made two columns on the board: **WHAT THE REGISTER SHOWS** and **WHAT IT DOES NOT SHOW**. Distribution went in the first. Origin, translation approval and current-route validation went in the second. A logistics success was not the same as a safety decision.

Bolt attempted to help by holding three directional placards for the visitors' comprehension demonstration. The elastic cord of the demonstration stand caught his polished visitor token and spun the entire placard assembly toward him; for one spectacular second he appeared to be wearing a four-sided signpost. Jacob rescued the stand. Bolt gave a ceremonious bow and returned the token to his pocket. For once, even Isaac smiled.

On the screen, a field photograph showed a slight difference in the mounting height of the fourth notice. Obed cautioned that mounting height could mean many things: a later replacement, a different installer, or simple inconsistency. It was a clue to investigate, not a date of issue.

Edliex listened as Isaac read the register aloud. When given the floor, it drew a dependency chain on the monitor: **draft → approve → translate → approve translations → distribute → validate against current building**. The chain was a working model, not a certified account of what had occurred. Several boxes had evidence; others were empty.

For Koware's own operations, Isaac requested a similar review of course welcome messages and service-card QR instructions. The 2026 action-plan proposals called for practical Health & Safety lessons, service-card trials and a Buzzjuice platform supporting training and transactions. A record that someone scanned a code could not, on its own, prove a course was delivered or a recurring payment was settled. These were proposed quality-assurance checks against an action plan, not achievements being announced.

By dusk, the team had a dated distribution entry and a sharper absence. The third challenge card bore **REGISTER**. The empty approval space had become more informative than a dozen tidy signatures in the wrong places.

## Part 4: The Date That Would Not Stay Still

Thursday's confusion arrived in two directions. The building manager still had not produced the original instruction. At the R&D laboratory, the Millennium Probe displayed a future-oriented date that did not match an earlier snapshot.

Enka called a technical review. The historical-reference setting was associated with July 2003 in Las Vegas; the production present belonged to Accra in November 2026; the Probe generated a separate future reading from a changing calculation. Those were three different clocks, and no one was entitled to fuse them into a single description of the Kuala Lumpur team's physical situation.

James Evans, the Director of Academic Programs, brought academic-record discipline to the question. Steven Sarpong, the Academic Registrar, asked for the input version beside every date. Matthew Ebenezer, the Academic Partnerships & Outreach Officer, wanted to know what an external collaborator could responsibly repeat. “A number without its calculation record may be perfectly precise and still be impossible to interpret,” James observed.

The laboratory's projector stand suddenly folded inward as Steven adjusted a page; two cardboard exhibit wings closed around him like the covers of an enormous book. He emerged with his notes intact and a rueful grin. Then he wrote the three clocks in separate columns.

Enka took an older Probe design sheet from the records case. He spoke of the early experimental arrangement associated with the Micro Computer Technology Institute of London and the cloned Edubeam, but he did not claim that those notes authenticated a present operator, an actual device deployment or today's Probe result. Historical custody and current operation needed different evidence.

The newest probe value was locked into an observation record with its collection time. Nobody deleted the earlier one. A change in output could be consistent with an intentional oscillation model, a changed input, an implementation defect or a misunderstood field. Until the algorithm and snapshots were reconciled, all four remained possibilities to investigate rather than conclusions to announce.

The Academic desk was asked to propose a reproducible comparison method for the time records. The partnerships desk would hold external statements until their claims were source-bound. Enka's visible question to Edliex was simple: “What remains stable when the date changes?”

“The history of what was observed,” Edliex answered after reviewing the available records. “If you preserve it.”

Back in the notice inquiry, a faint file-transfer reference emerged from older correspondence. It suggested a route into the records office, not yet a discovered document. The fourth challenge card was filed under **CLOCK**: preserve the snapshot, the source and the limits of inference. In one building an arrow had several versions; in another system a date had several values. The lesson was unsettlingly alike.

## Part 5: The Copies That Agreed

Friday brought the first whole-week review. The Quality Assurance & Compliance Manager, Moses Larbi, had a table full of versions, while Gloria Mica, the Faculty Coordinator, and the liaison tutor Robert Amegashie checked whether the day's lesson could be taught without pretending the mystery was solved.

Four notices. A cropped screenshot. A distribution register. An unanswered request for the originating instruction. A shifting Probe archive. None of them answered the same question.

“Suppose we find the original,” Gloria said. “Will it settle the route?”

“Only if the building has not changed in a way that makes it obsolete,” Moses replied. “A lawful decision made years ago isn't automatically a safe instruction today.”

The lesson mattered to the proposed Health & Safety course in Koware's 2026 plan. One section would teach hazard identification and communicating emergency information in accessible language; another would teach evidence custody. The academic staff drafted a review checklist and assigned it for supervised technical validation. No course launch, credential or accreditation was declared complete.

A stack of freshly collated evidence sheets escaped the edge of Moses's clipboard when the spring clip snapped free. The pages lifted in one perfect wave and settled around his shoes like a paper shoreline. Nobody was hurt. For a moment even Moses laughed. Then he insisted every page be recounted and the original order reconstructed.

At the review meeting, Johnson Ortsin joined briefly to assess workload. Mathias Agbenorku, Director of Operations, had raised concerns about pursuing every attractive lead while the principal approval request remained unanswered. Derek Shiraska, the Production Coordinator, offered a practical compromise: distinguish urgent safety escalation from archival research, put each task with its accountable office, and avoid sending unbriefed observers to test a route. Gifty retained scientific questions; Joycelyn held workplace-safety review; the responsible Kuala Lumpur building personnel retained local emergency authority.

The Legal Journalist updated the pinboard without removing the earlier note. Beneath it appeared a second line: **DISTRIBUTION DOCUMENTED; CURRENT ROUTE NOT VERIFIED.** Other headlines—transport costs, public events, technology developments—continued to accumulate, but they were kept in their own sections, with origin and date visible.

Enka returned to the Psalm from earlier in the week. The problem with an instruction was not solved by possessing it, repeating it or decorating it with an official mark. Someone had to understand what it required, how it was established and to whom responsibility belonged. The Eternal Dreamfall study of translation had become a study of accountability.

The fifth challenge card was **VERSION**. Before the staff dispersed, a new message arrived: an archival work-order number could be connected to an older transfer. The original instruction remained out of sight, but its possible trail had acquired a name—**44-E**.

## Part 6: The Line Beneath the Arrow

On the following Monday, Work Order 44-E lay at the center of the operations meeting. Johnson Ortsin spread the dependency chart across a long table. Mathias placed estimated effort beside each step. Derek read the restrictions on the Kuala Lumpur assignment back to the room.

Johnson pointed to the three notices that agreed. “Our tendency is to call that a majority.”

“A majority of copies,” Derek replied. “Not a majority of independent approvals.”

The remote observer's earlier record and the building manager's correspondence suggested a storage transfer. A regional archive contact indicated that a matching box might exist. No one had seen its contents, and the building manager had not established the authority by which it could be released. The original could be missing, incomplete, altered or irrelevant to the current building. Each possibility remained open.

Edliex listened to the agenda, then entered conversational mode when Enka gave permission. It advised keeping three workstreams distinct: evidence retrieval; local building-safety escalation; and Probe calculation review. The team's existing observation-only mandate was not expanded by that advice. A note written under each workstream named who could ask, who could decide and what a report would have to show before it could justify action.

During the critical-path demonstration, Derek pulled a retractable planning chart from its casing. The roller snapped back and swallowed the paper until only the words **DO NOT ASSUME** protruded. Johnson stared at it. “At least the schedule knows our policy,” he said. A few people had to look away to hide their smiles.

The workstreams extended into the rest of Koware's proposed projects. Johnson asked for a failure-to-delivery map for the Buzzjuice transaction and subscription workflow: where an order starts, which component acknowledges payment, which evidence proves fulfillment, and who owns a retry. The service-card and Oxford House stewardship proposals needed similarly traceable budgets and approvals. Derek would prepare dependency maps; Mathias would review operations risk; the appropriate finance, legal and project owners would still need to validate the plans. No software fault was claimed fixed and no prospective house facility was represented as open.

A secondary pinboard item about electricity-maintenance notices led to a readiness check for local power protection and offline copies, not to a claim that the New Legon compound was scheduled for an outage. The team took the same approach with every public bulletin: dates, named areas and authorized sources mattered.

Late in the afternoon, word arrived that a storage box matching the older transfer reference had been located. It remained sealed. In the room, the discovery produced the thrill of a locked door at the end of a long passage.

Enka declined to call the mystery solved. “We have found the place a document might be. We have not read the document.”

The sixth challenge card bore **CUSTODY**. Finding the box was a new lead. Opening it would require a different kind of key.

## Part 7: The Name the Paper Could Not Give

Tuesday was Mubarak Mohsen's day to bring the facility records into order. The sealed-box request showed a transfer number, a date and a convincing stamp. Above the release section was the field that mattered: **authorized requester**. No qualifying name had been supplied.

Elijah Peter, the Facility Butler, confirmed the case-file inventory. Lovemore Saiga, the Landscape Gardener, paused during a compound-safety check to point out that a physical boundary was useful only when everyone knew who was responsible for it. Their work remained local to Koware's New Legon facilities; no one was quietly reassigned to the Kuala Lumpur site.

Mubarak began to stamp the paper **FOR REVIEW**, but the rubber stamp bounced off an overfull tray, struck a blank scrap and left the words sideways across his own wrist. Elijah handed him a tissue without comment. Mubarak looked at the accidental mark. “Even a stamp needs context,” he said. The office fell briefly quiet before Elijah burst out laughing.

The office's first draft message to the observers was too strong. It said that the original instruction had been located and that the field team could proceed to check the route. Mubarak refused to let it leave. They had located a possible box; they had neither opened it nor authenticated its contents; they had received no local safety authorization for a route inspection. He asked the issuing office to identify the responsible release authority and the current building-safety officer.

Enka deleted the proposed imperative. In its place he approved a narrower internal request: confirm the custodian, retain the box seal and transfer trail, and seek lawful disclosure through the appropriate channel. Separately, the building's authorized safety staff should investigate the conflicting notices and communicate any valid interim guidance to occupants. Koware's team would not invent that guidance.

The incident put a strain between the programme office and facility desk. Johnson wanted momentum; Mubarak wanted a defensible release. Neither was wrong about the urgency. They differed over what urgency permitted. Enka refused to resolve the argument by giving either man authority he did not have. The distinction made their next handover more careful, though not more cheerful.

When the final staff member left the room, the request was neatly prepared. Its signature space remained empty. Below it stood a second question: even if the originating document could be lawfully retrieved, did it still match the current building?

The seventh challenge card was **REQUESTER**. A name might unlock the box. It could not by itself turn an old diagram into a present safety instruction.

## Part 8: The Message That Stayed Unsent

By Wednesday, the reception pinboard had become a map of Koware's questions. The Legal Journalist had divided it into dated reporting, internal proposals and items requiring verification. A draft headline on the Kuala Lumpur inquiry carried the word **CONFIRMED**. He struck it through and wrote **REPORTED**. The Public Relations & Press Coordinator, Josh Lafa, resisted the temptation to turn an unresolved safety question into a dramatic social post. Terry Joan, the Marketing Manager, had already prepared a visual concept, but agreed it could not depict either stairwell as the approved exit.

They were interrupted by the office display backdrop tipping gently from its frame and settling around Josh's shoulders like an enormous press-conference cape. Terry extracted him while the Legal Journalist moved the pinboard farther from the stand. No one proposed publishing the photograph. Josh made the mistake of bowing to the room.

That afternoon, the pinboard carried a verified national item: Ghana's finance minister was due to present the 2027 Budget on Thursday, with the New Economy programme central to the anticipated policy discussion. Koware's Marketing and programme desks were authorized within the story to review possible implications for skills, manufacturing and technology. Nobody described a government programme as a grant awarded to Koware. The newsroom's rule was strict: the opportunity to research an announcement was not the same as permission to announce a partnership.

On Thursday, International Relations Coordinator Pascal Quagrainie reviewed the possible cross-border implications of the Kuala Lumpur evidence inquiry. Veronica Samiatu, the Global Partnerships Registrar, arranged communications by date, sender and recipient. NanaYaa Adom, the Global Partnerships Liaison, drafted a receipt-and-acknowledgment register so no one would confuse a message *sent* with a message *received*, or a receipt with understanding.

Reports of disruption affecting Microsoft Outlook and Teams underscored the risk of relying on a single channel for sensitive approvals. The team made an offline decision record and a secondary-contact proposal. They did not claim Koware's inbox had failed, and they did not claim new contact with the deployed observers. A document could be ready on one desk while still missing its authorized recipient.

Veronica's folders were organized with such severity that when NanaYaa attempted to remove one, a binder's elastic strap flipped a harmless shower of index tabs over her head. She froze under a small snowfall of bright labels; Pascal's expression lasted longer than the laughter. Then they put the index tabs back in order.

The Legal Journalist then read the Budget coverage aloud. The New Economy programme—reported as a four-year US$10 billion initiative for 2027–2030, with a proposed GH¢30 billion allocation in 2027—warranted detailed analysis. The Ghana Stock Exchange's interest in blockchain and digital assets belonged on a separate technology card, with explicit attention to investor safeguards. Neither item was evidence for the Kuala Lumpur notice. On the international panel, a Reuters report concerning Malaysia's investigation into possible AI-chip re-exports raised an ethical technology-governance question, not an allegation against the building or the Classies team. They were distinct stories in the same country.

Friday began with planning for an event that could place Koware employees among real businesses instead of inside a meeting room. The Ecobank JoyBusiness SME Fair was opening at GHUD Park, Accra Mall, for 13–15 November. Thomisia Daniel, the Events & Tours Coordinator, suggested an observation visit. Ingrid Razak, the Community Outreach Officer, wanted to hear from exhibitors about their needs, especially small businesses navigating digital payments and market access. Ceda Bertto, the Booking Agent, kept a list of interested people and a separate column for actual confirmations. A separate creative-industry opportunity, the dEX Design Conference at the National Theatre, was logged for consideration rather than falsely treated as attended.

Ceda attempted to slide an overstuffed folder underneath the meeting-room door while carrying attendance sheets. The folder stuck; the papers sprang up, and the largest form landed on Ceda's head like a giant paper hat. The meeting dissolved in a warm laugh. Then Ceda stood up and pointed at the empty booking column. “Interest is not a reservation.”

That sentence returned the room to Work Order 44-E.

The box's location was indicated. Its transfer history had been traced. Its contents were still unverified; release authority was still missing. The disputed notice could not be validated by counting translations, tracing distribution or presenting an old stamp. Even a genuine instruction would have to be tested against the building as it stood now—by its responsible personnel, through valid procedures. The observers had not been authorized to select a route. No fresh authenticated message resolved their individual position or condition.

Enka reopened the draft he had almost sent. It had said: *We found the instruction. Proceed.* He looked at the words long enough to see how much they hid. A sealed box was not an instruction; an old document was not a current route; an observer was not the building safety officer. He deleted the sentence and left only the properly scoped requests in the approval queue.

Edliex, having listened to the meeting, was invited to give its closing assessment.

“The investigation has advanced,” it said. “Not because the arrow has been chosen, but because the team can now state what each record is capable of proving.”

Enka closed the message window without pressing send. Around the compound, the five departments resumed their separate work. The newsroom prepared its next verification board; the programme desk checked project dependencies; the academic staff planned safer instruction design; the international team preserved a communications trail; and the laboratory archived another Probe snapshot without treating its future reading as a passenger manifest.

The eighth challenge card was **RELEASE**. Eight cards now stood in a row: LANGUAGE, FRAME, REGISTER, CLOCK, VERSION, CUSTODY, REQUESTER, RELEASE. None opened the building's sealed box. Together, they might open a better way of deciding what to do next.

The message remained unsent. The authority behind the arrow remained unverified. But the people around Enka had learned to recognize the precise point at which confidence must give way to evidence.

### Next episode — The box, the broadcast and the next threshold

Koware's next ten-working-day cycle turns toward governance, corporate records, editorial responsibility, creative production, talent preparation and platform architecture. The stakeholder and board agendas require careful distinctions between proposals and adopted decisions; the Corporate Secretary and Administrative Auditor will be pressed for document provenance; the Legal Journalist will track any updates to the New Economy programme, technology regulation and the ongoing approval request. Later, the Creative & Design and Talent teams will prepare field-safe communication concepts, while the Enterprise Architect and platform team examine the transaction and evidence workflow behind Buzzjuice's service-card and course initiatives.

A proposed Edubeam follow-up would be a restricted observation-and-documentation assignment: a team lead, records analyst and liaison technician, supported remotely by R&D and the responsible local building personnel. Kuala Lumpur remains the candidate observation setting. There is no approved departure, no validated new team roster, no release of the sealed box and no authenticated Probe destination for that next deployment. The most recent retained Probe snapshot must be compared with a newly acquired observation before any estimated date, phase position, return window or equipment state can be represented as confirmed.

The returning team's first required debrief—if and when its return or contact is actually established—will distinguish what was photographed, what was transferred, what was authorized, and whether any current route was independently checked by the proper safety authority. Until then, there is no invented homecoming.

And at the bottom of the pinboard, the Legal Journalist leaves one square empty. It is reserved for a name that has not yet been supplied.

### The Classies Challenge — Episode 02: Eight Locks, No Shortcut

Eight challenge cards were earned along the way: **LANGUAGE, FRAME, REGISTER, CLOCK, VERSION, CUSTODY, REQUESTER, RELEASE**. The original daily challenges teach why each card matters. Teams may compare their reasoning, but no team may treat a clue, stamp, receipt or screenshot as an automatic authorization. Palmier points and Buzzjuice score integration remain a future feature; no points have been awarded here.

**Final mission:** You have the four conflicting notices, the cropped screenshot, the distribution register, changing Probe snapshots, Work Order 44-E, a sealed archive-box reference, an unsigned release request and a draft instruction ready to transmit. Your team must submit an **eight-lock proof**: for every card, identify (1) the source artifact, (2) the precise claim it can support, (3) a tempting but impermissible claim, and (4) the correct role or process authorized to resolve the gap. You must also reconcile **two independent timestamp streams** and show where the proposed outgoing message belongs in the evidence chain. A bare multiple-choice selection, even if lucky, does not unlock the episode or the eventual chapter challenge. Split the locks among teammates if you wish, but reconcile the complete proof before choosing. **Which proposed complete decision package can advance the inquiry without converting observation into unauthorized action?**

**A.** Accept the three matching translations as a consensus; preserve the images; use the work order as installation approval; send the observers to confirm the popular stairwell; request the box afterward.

**B.** Trace the uncropped originals and translation lineage; retain each Probe snapshot separately; verify source and custody; obtain the correct release authority; seek current route assessment and occupant guidance from the responsible building safety personnel; send only claims actually verified through a documented channel.

**C.** Treat the dated fourth notice as the latest approved version; publish a warning immediately; send the observers to test that route; ask the custodian for approval once the route is known.

**D.** Open the box because the transfer reference identifies it; use any document found as the governing instruction; correct the four translations and publish them before checking whether the building layout has changed.

**E.** Withhold all communication indefinitely, including the report of contradictory signage; discard earlier Probe snapshots once the newest future date appears; close the case unless an archive official contacts Koware unprompted.
