# CLASSIES CHRONICLES
## Season 00 · Chapter 05: The Signal Before Sunrise
# Episode 02 — Four Arrows, No Signature

### Part 1: The Notice in Four Languages

The first notice arrived before the reception lights had warmed to their usual amber. It was a single sheet in a transparent sleeve, four columns of words beneath an enormous black arrow. Someone had clipped it to the reception pinboard without leaving a name, a delivery note or a request for approval.

Bonsu Bolt found it while checking the visitor desk. Beyond the windows, the five single-storey apartment blocks occupied by Koware's departments stood close enough to make a conversation travel between them. Each department managed its own block; none could pretend that a visitor sent the wrong way would be somebody else's problem.

The heading read: **TEMPORARY VISITOR ROUTE — FOLLOW THE ARROW**. The English column stood beside Twi, Ga and French, though no one present had yet validated that the four versions were equivalent. The arrow pointed towards the shared passage between blocks. The fourth column had a smaller line beneath its heading; in the others, that line appeared to have vanished.

Bolt didn't know whether the columns said precisely the same thing. That was the problem. A visitor might reasonably trust the biggest arrow more than the smallest words.

He lifted the sleeve. A second arrow, printed on the reverse of a folded visitor sign, sprang open and spun on its hinge until it pointed firmly at his shoes. The first two arrivals watched him stand there, accused by a sign of taking the wrong route on his own floor. Bolt folded the sign with as much dignity as the situation allowed.

In the Administrative block, the Legal Journalist, Michael McCauley, was preparing the morning pinboard. He had arranged verified stories under two columns: **What happened** and **What is still being checked**. A Ghanaian Cyber Security Authority advisory about reporting suspicious activity remained visible from the previous month. Beside it was a separate international item about the approaching climate conference in Antalya. Headlines, he had insisted, were beginnings of inquiries, never substitutes for evidence.

“Is that ours?” he asked, holding the four-language notice by its corners.

“It has our building names,” Bolt said. “It does not have our approval.”

Enka arrived with a notebook held shut by an elastic band. He had been thinking about the old study question that kept returning to him: what becomes of a person who answers before listening? On the screen by his desk, an assigned verse from Proverbs had the same uncompromising force: *He that answereth a matter before he heareth it, it is folly and shame unto him.*

“Take it down?” someone asked.

“Preserve it first,” Enka replied. “Then put up the route we can actually verify.”

At the Koware briefing, Edliex listened without interrupting. It was the AI's first mode: silent capture of the discussion, its uncertainties and the people responsible for resolving them. Jacques Asiedu, the Corporate Secretary, separated the sheet from its claims. There was a physical paper. There was text attributed to no accountable office. There was an arrow suggesting a movement instruction. None of those facts proved that the instruction had been authorized.

Vera Agbenahevi sketched the five-block cluster as zones rather than a detailed security plan. She marked the visitor desk and the ordinary approved route without publishing private entry details. On the unknown sheet, she drew a question mark where the arrow seemed to cross a departmental boundary.

Only when the meeting reached the feedback stage did Edliex speak.

“Four languages do not constitute four approvals. A translation can preserve words while altering a condition. I recommend treating the notice as unverified until each version and its issuing authority are independently checked.”

Then conversational mode began. Bolt asked the question everyone had avoided: “Who is asking our visitors to move?”

Edliex answered, “The paper is. We have not established who instructed the paper.”

That afternoon, a visitor showed Michael a photograph of the notice already circulating in a private group. In the image, the tiny line beneath the fourth language had been cropped away. The arrow seemed more commanding than it had on the wall.

Enka watched the photograph enlarge on the screen. Their mystery was no longer a sheet of paper. It could travel farther than any person in the room.

### Part 2: The Cropped Truth

By the following morning, three people had asked Bolt whether visitors were being redirected for a safety emergency. None had seen an official warning. All had seen the same photograph.

The image looked decisive: arrow, four languages, a clean white border. Michael placed it beside the uncropped photograph taken at reception. On the original, a narrow strip of page extended beneath the arrow. The circulating image had removed precisely the strip that could qualify the instruction.

At the Tech block, Vera and the platform team inspected copies without opening an unknown attachment or following the link embedded in its forwarded caption. The screenshot's text was not a valid authentication trail. An image could preserve pixels while deleting the history that would explain them.

Cornelius from facilities brought the original notice sleeve in a clean evidence envelope. A strip of removable tape had attached itself to the sole of his shoe; when he stepped forward it tugged a lightweight chair along for one improbable step. The chair stopped. Cornelius looked down as though the building had tried to follow him. Nobody let the laughter erase the fact that the route remained unverified.

“Who made the crop?” Enka asked.

Michael shook his head. “We can establish that it's cropped. We cannot establish who cropped it.”

The distinction mattered. He could write a clarification for the pinboard without alleging misconduct by a real person. He replaced the viral image with a plain statement: **A circulating notice is under verification. For current visitor directions, ask reception. Do not rely on forwarded images.** Jacques logged who reviewed the wording and when.

At noon, a messenger from a neighboring office described receiving the same notice as a flattened PDF. That version had a footer absent from the screenshot. The footer named a document control number, but its final digits were blurred. The messenger had already left. Bolt did not chase a person through traffic on the strength of a rumor; instead, he recorded the messenger's volunteered description and notified the proper contact channel.

In their second meeting, Edliex listened first. Michael spoke about the Cyber Security Authority's public emphasis on reporting suspicious activity. Vera contrasted a forwarded screenshot with a source file. Enka asked a question from the morning's study: whether speed had become their measure of truth.

“Feedback mode,” Jacques said.

“Partial agreement between copies may indicate common origin rather than independent confirmation,” Edliex replied. “Preserve the full frame, compare the visible margins and collect the source and authorization separately.”

In conversational mode, the AI helped Vera map which blocks had received which version. One copy had arrived through the reception desk; another through an internal shared drive; a third through a hand-carried folder. They were not three independent witnesses to a safety order. They might be three routes from one file.

Late that day, a fourth PDF appeared in a folder used for pending notices. Its title matched the others. Its arrow did not. It carried no signature block at all.

### Part 3: The Unsigned Fourth Notice

The fourth notice named the same buildings and used the same four-column arrangement, but its arrow ended at a different boundary. The first three versions disagreed in margins and cropping; this one disagreed about where a visitor should go.

Jacques printed a comparison sheet and laid it beside the others. When he pulled the lowest folder from an overfilled stack, the documents above it fanned down over his head like a paper waterfall. For a moment he was nothing but a pair of shoes beneath administrative correspondence. He emerged holding exactly one page—the new notice—and said, “Apparently the files have elected their own secretary.” The room's brief laughter ended when they saw the arrow.

The fourth page used a familiar title but had no issuing person's name, no verifiable date of approval and no recorded change request. Its digital properties reported that it had been exported, but an export time was not an approval time. Even the printer's mark could identify a device without identifying who ordered the document.

Enka made a risky suggestion: they could send a corrected notice immediately and stop the confusion.

“No,” Michael said. “A correction that assumes which original is right may amplify an instruction we never owned.”

The disagreement followed them across the open walkway. It was not personal, but it left a visible silence when both reached for the same door. Enka held it. Michael entered without treating the courtesy as a surrender.

Inside the Design block, Kingsford Howard examined alignment rather than language. A designer could reproduce a logo, an arrow and a document number in seconds. The important question was not whether the page resembled Koware's communication style but whether it passed the documented path from proposal to review, authorization and distribution.

The team interviewed the people who could speak to those stages without asking anybody to surrender private passwords, messages or diary entries. No one could produce an accepted approval for the fourth notice. Their best evidence so far was negative and bounded: the particular records examined did not show it. It was not proof that no authorization could exist elsewhere.

A call reached Enka from the remote team whose last-known operational base was Kuala Lumpur. The connection was short and the audio poor. They had not been asked to approve a visitor-route notice, one voice said, but a file named with a similar arrow symbol had appeared among mission-related reference material. The caller promised a controlled comparison after contact could be re-established; there was no claim that the team had returned, changed location or resolved its field assignment.

Edliex waited through the meeting's listening phase, then said in feedback mode, “Do not fuse these two claims: the existence of a similar symbol and the identity of its author.”

The AI entered conversational mode only after Enka asked whether the remote report proved interference. It did not.

At closing, they sealed the original sleeve and recorded the four versions in a neutral evidence list. The fourth page remained unsigned. Yet the reception printer began to show a queued instruction that nobody in the room remembered submitting.

### Part 4: The Instruction No One Could Sign

The queued instruction asked staff to move archival boxes from one block to another before the day's visitor period ended. It referred to “the revised arrow route” as if authorization had already been established.

A power interruption left the check-in desk briefly dark just as a trolley reached the covered passage. On it stood two empty record crates. One wheel slipped from the slight slope of the floor and rolled the trolley a harmless arm's length toward Jacques, who caught the handle while his paper cup hopped once inside an empty crate. The little parade stopped against his palm. What remained was a real safety concern: boxes could obstruct a visitor route even if the instruction had been legitimate.

Joycelyn Ennison, the Health & Safety Technician, stopped the transfer, sent visitors to the established staffed desk and kept the passage clear until the ordinary lighting was restored. No one had to prove malicious intent before pausing work where a route, people and equipment could conflict.

“We have a standing visitor path,” she told the team. “Do not alter it for a screenshot. Keep access unobstructed. Confirm the hazard, the authorization and the alternative before moving anything.”

Her intervention changed the morning. It was no longer a competition to identify the person behind the paper; it was a practical test of whether Koware could act safely under uncertain information. Bolt stood down the improvised diversion. Pauline from housekeeping helped clear a waiting area, while Michael arranged a notice telling visitors whom to approach for verified directions. Nobody portrayed a private stranger as a threat, and nobody announced an emergency they could not confirm.

In the Administrative block, a meeting convened with each of Edliex's modes clearly separated. During listening, Michael read the unsigned instruction aloud and Jacques listed the controls missing from it. During feedback, Edliex identified three gaps: no approved owner, no verified destination, and no sign-off for the movement of records. During conversation, Enka challenged the AI.

“What if the instruction is genuine and delay makes things worse?”

“Then the safe path is an expedited verification,” Edliex replied. “Uncertainty is not permission to move blind. A real emergency uses established emergency procedures, not an unsigned paper that arrived through an unknown chain.”

The answer stung Enka. He had wanted action to prove leadership. Now the discipline of stopping was being asked of him in front of everyone.

He called the department stewards. Each confirmed that no authorized archive transfer had been announced through the channels they checked. That did not settle the origin of the paper, but it allowed them to leave the records where they were while keeping the site accessible.

By evening they discovered something more troubling. The same sentence—**release after route confirmation**—appeared on all four digital files. It had been placed in a field that ordinary viewers could not see in the printed notices.

The instruction that no one could sign had a common ancestor.

### Part 5: The Copies That Agreed

The document-control table was filled with matching filenames. The four PDFs had different visible arrows, yet the hidden release field, export pattern and several minor typographic errors were identical. To an impatient eye, the files agreed. To Vera, they looked related, like four witnesses repeating a sentence they had been taught.

She asked Kingsford to lay transparent overlays on the designs. He pulled a binder too quickly; its accordion-style index unfolded around his knees, trapping him in a concertina of harmless paper tabs. He stood still while the team regarded him as a newly installed filing cabinet. Then he freed one hand and pointed to the same stray dot beside every arrowhead.

“A source mark,” he said. “One parent template.”

The R&D desk had a limited job: inspect permitted file metadata and the printed marks, not pronounce that a device had reached across years. Jacques kept a separate table for physical objects, screen images and interpretations. Enka asked that the team test whether the apparently corroborating versions were simply different renders of one unapproved document.

At reception, Michael refreshed the pinboard. A genuine Ghanaian headline recalled the Cyber Security Authority’s October public advisory encouraging people to report suspicious cyber activity. He wrote a second line beneath it: **Source: Cyber Security Authority, Ghana; check the original advisory before explaining its effect.** On the international board, the forthcoming UN climate talks in Antalya offered another useful lesson: a room of delegates and a shared document did not automatically mean agreement had been reached. The subjects were different, but the requirement to distinguish discussion from decision was familiar.

The team carried the overlays to the fifth block, where Enka had asked for a cross-department review. Nobody voted on the truth by counting copies. They tested source independence, physical sequence and administrative control. The printable parts of the four notices could all be traced back to a common layout, but one had acquired an extra line at some point after export. They could not yet tell whether that line was a warning, a correction or a deliberate insertion.

By the end of the meeting, Edliex had listened to everyone's observations. In feedback mode it rejected a tempting calculation: “Four copies multiplied by confidence are still one uncertain lineage.” In conversational mode, it asked whether staff would prefer a fast statement about the cause or a verified statement about what people should do now.

“Now,” Bolt answered. “Visitors need a safe route today, not our theory about yesterday.”

They issued the latter. The original approved visitor procedure remained in force. Any proposed change required a named owner, reviewed wording and a recorded authorization before display. They retained all four notices under controlled access as evidence of the unresolved discrepancy.

The apparent agreement of the copies no longer frightened them. What frightened Enka was the possibility that the shared source file had been prepared for a use none of them could see.

### Part 6: The Line Beneath the Arrow

A handheld lamp revealed the thin line under the arrow more clearly than the screen had. It was not a sentence on the printed paper. It was a narrow registration strip—a sequence of small marks used to align layers when the notice was assembled.

Pauline, who had spent the morning making sure the public space remained clear, noticed that the strip sat closer to the arrowhead on one copy than on the others. She did not need to be a graphic designer to recognize that two things meant to be stacked precisely had slipped.

Kingsford held the transparent layer against the light. Bolt reached for the loose edge, but the rolled transparency sprang back and wrapped itself around his forearm in a tight spiral. He regarded his arm as if it had been placed under transparent arrest. Kingsford freed the sheet before anyone tried to unroll it against the original paper.

“Don't change the evidence just because it has attacked you,” Vera said. Then she traced the alignment marks with a pencil on a separate copy.

They had been reading the arrow as an instruction. Now they began reading its physical construction. The strip could show which copy had been scaled, rotated or reassembled. It could not, by itself, show who had done it or why.

An authorized technician checked the reproducible files in a protected workspace. The comparison suggested a later adjustment to at least one arrow layer; the editor who made it remained unknown. The change could be deliberate, accidental or a step in an unrelated mock-up. They marked each possibility rather than selecting the most exciting one.

Enka returned to the day's reading, not to extract a prophecy but to resist his own impatience. Words could be correctly copied while their purpose was falsified. The ancient caution against answering before hearing was no guarantee of victory. It was a discipline to repeat when evidence invited a shortcut.

In the meeting, Edliex listened first to the physical findings and their limitations. Feedback mode highlighted the distinction between **orientation evidence** and **authority evidence**. In conversational mode, it challenged Vera's proposed digital reconstruction: “Can the reconstruction be independently repeated without editing the source?”

“Yes,” she replied, “provided we retain the original and record every transformation.”

Michael placed a modest headline above the photographs on the pinboard: **A sign may point clearly and still lack the authority to direct anyone.** It was Koware's own explanation of its ongoing inquiry, not an accusation against a named outsider.

That evening, the remote channel from Kuala Lumpur produced a brief text receipt. It contained no instruction to move visitors. It did contain the same arrangement of four directional marks. Before the team could verify the sender's full context, the connection ended. Enka wrote **receipt only; explanation pending** in his notebook.

The line beneath the arrow had opened a second question: had the visitor notices borrowed a design from the remote team's reference material, or had both inherited it from something older?

### Part 7: The Name the Paper Could Not Give

The missing name was now a temptation. A rumor had attached itself to the unsigned page, and a few staff wanted to know whom to blame. Michael refused to put a person's name on the pinboard without evidence, consent and a defensible reason to publish it.

He brought the story to Jacques, who set out three different questions: who created the template; who approved the instruction; and who physically displayed the sheet. The answers might be three people, one person or no person they could currently identify. A blank author field did not settle any of them.

A tall cardboard document tube had been left near the records desk. Jacques removed its cap to inspect a harmless duplicate. The tightly rolled sheet shot up and opened over his head like a startled paper crown. The receptionist took the tube before the duplicate could tumble to the floor. He put the sheet flat, his dignity intact enough to continue the meeting.

Michael interviewed only the appropriate custodians of the notices and the staff who voluntarily described what they had seen. Nobody was required to unlock a private account. A colleague recalled receiving a link from a legitimate-looking internal address; the system owner said the address alone could be copied into a forged message. The team's technical review could not establish the human sender.

The Enterprise Architect found that a form field labeled **requested by** had never been made mandatory. The template could therefore export a persuasive-looking page with an empty attribution space. That was an architecture defect worth correcting. It was not proof that someone had used the omission maliciously.

Enka wanted to tell the whole organization immediately. Michael suggested a narrower bulletin: an investigation was open; the notice remained unverified; the normal route was in force; staff should retain copies and use the controlled reporting channel. Enka heard an objection as hesitation until he recognized the difference between protecting people from an instruction and accusing a person of issuing it.

“You were right to stop the transfer,” he told Michael. “I was right to ask for speed. I was wrong to think one excluded the other.”

Michael nodded. “Speed belongs in the safety response. Certainty belongs in the evidence.”

Edliex waited through its listening phase, returned a limited feedback report on the broken authoring workflow, and entered conversational mode for a final question: if the paper could not name its issuer, could Koware name its own response? Jacques answered by producing a signed internal decision record that said what had actually been checked, who reviewed it, what was still unknown and when the next review would occur.

At dusk Enka opened the historical clock reference on an isolated screen. Its recorded date belonged to July 2003. A second display contained a Millennium Probe field pointing toward a date in 2108. Neither display was a witness who could certify today's notice; their timestamps belonged to different streams and different purposes. He shut the panes rather than letting the most astonishing date win the argument.

A note arrived from the remote mission channel: **We recognize the arrangement, but we have not authorized this instruction. Do not assign the pattern an owner yet.** The receipt was preserved as a claim from that channel, awaiting full authentication.

Now Enka understood what the paper could not give them: not only a name, but the right to pretend they knew one.

### Part 8: The Message That Stayed Unsent

On the last working day of the episode's review window, the threat was small enough to fit inside a sentence and large enough to change the way every block worked. One unsigned instruction had crossed paper, screen, shared drive and rumor. No one had obeyed the doubtful archive transfer. The ordinary visitor route remained usable. But the common source template—and its connection to a four-mark arrangement seen on the remote channel—was still unexplained.

Before the final briefing, a draft broadcast appeared on Enka's screen. It was polished, urgent and wrong in one crucial way: it named a culprit. The draft did not come from a verified authorization chain, and nothing they had established justified the attribution. Enka selected the recipient group, then stopped short of sending it.

A tape dispenser had attached itself to his jacket cuff. As he walked toward the meeting table, the dispenser skated after him like a stubborn pet on a clear ribbon. He stopped, peeled it free and set it down. Even the smallest attachments, he thought, could follow a person farther than expected.

In the Administrative block, Michael had replaced the cluttered pinboard with three clear sections: **verified guidance**, **external headlines with source and date**, and **open questions**. The Bank of Ghana's cyber directive and the UN climate talks still had places under current developments, because they mattered to the organization's work and because each carried a route to its original publisher. Unverified messages were not promoted into those columns merely because they were dramatic.

The team assembled. Joycelyn confirmed the practical controls: unobstructed passage, approved visitor guidance, a stop on unsanctioned box movement, and a safe process for staff to report conflicting signs. Vera recorded the template flaw and the independent rechecks required to repair it. Jacques summarized the decision trail. Bolt described what visitors had actually asked and how reception had answered without causing panic. Michael outlined the difference between a notice dispute and an allegation against any person.

Edliex listened until all reports had been given. At feedback stage, it displayed a three-column timeline—not one blended legend. **Production time** marked the events in Accra. **Historical reference** identified a recorded 2003 clock source and nothing more. **Millennium Probe** identified a separate experimental reading associated with 2108. The team's last reported location in Kuala Lumpur remained a distinct field, with contact and retrieval status still unconfirmed.

“Can we tell the staff what happened?” Enka asked when conversational mode opened.

“We can tell them what we verified,” Edliex replied. “The route did not change. The attempted record movement was not authorized through the channels checked. Multiple notice versions share a source design. The identity and purpose of the modification remain unresolved.”

“And the message from Kuala Lumpur?”

“Received, not fully authenticated for broader attribution. It cannot settle the matter alone.”

The reports did not make Enka heroic in the way he had imagined at the start. They made him accountable. He deleted the accusation from his draft and wrote a narrower communication: **The previously circulated four-language visitor-route notice is not an approved instruction. Continue to use the established reception guidance. Report any further copies through the controlled channel. We will update this guidance when the authorized review is complete.**

Jacques checked the wording. Joycelyn approved the safety message within her remit. Michael checked that no unverified person was being accused. The bulletin went only to the appropriate internal distribution. The larger, sensational message—with the name and the story they could not substantiate—remained unsent.

As everyone left, Enka stayed with the two screen panes. A clean copy of the four directional marks had appeared in the remote receipt. The marks were similar, not identical. In the experimental probe display, a field shifted as the screen refreshed. For one instant he saw the arrow arrangement align as if the pages were transparent layers held against the light. Then the pane cleared.

He did not call it a transmission from the future. He made a record of what the screen had shown, with its source and uncertainty, and asked for an independent capture before anyone built a story around it.

A sound came from reception: the ordinary click of Bolt taking down the last unverified notice. The wall looked bare, but no visitor would now be sent into a passage by an unsigned instruction.

The next episode would begin with a harder problem. The disputed route had been contained; the provenance had not. Somewhere between a local document template, an incomplete remote receipt and a timestamp belonging to another century, the four marks remained. They were waiting for someone to make a claim bigger than the evidence allowed—or to discover why the evidence had been arranged to invite exactly that mistake.

## The Classies Episode Challenge — The Eight Locks

Eight review locks were encountered across the parts: **language equivalence, full-frame context, provenance, authority, independent corroboration, physical orientation, accountable identity and separate time streams**. Each lock supplies one method for examining the four notices, but none alone proves a notice is authentic. Working as a team, readers may assemble a public evidence board and compare the clues without accessing private records or attempting to locate an individual.

**Challenge question:** Given the four disputed notices, the cropped image, the unsigned transfer instruction, their common template, the shifted registration line, the missing issuer and the separate Accra–historical–Probe timestamps, what *ordered eight-lock verification protocol* would allow the team to determine which claims may safely guide visitors now, which claims must remain unresolved and what additional public, independently verifiable evidence would be required before any revised notice could be issued?
