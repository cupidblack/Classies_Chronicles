# THE NOTICE IN FOUR LANGUAGES

*A Classies Chronicle · Monday, 2 November 2026*

### 05:10 — New Legon

The Koware compound was still grey and quiet when Enka opened the week’s study notes. By afternoon the forecast promised thirty-two degrees and a thirty per cent chance of rain. For now the air at the window was soft, and the generators had not yet started their Monday argument with the traffic.

The Eternal Dreamfall plan had turned a page. Week 96: the Septuagint, still in progress, the ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures, made by people who had to carry meaning from one language into another without dropping any of it on the way. The plan named the week, not a passage, so Enka read the way the week seemed to ask: slowly, and as a whole, watching how a faithful translation preserves a text, and how even a faithful one can lean. In the margin he added a reminder that traditions differ over which books in that collection they receive as scripture, and that a careful study never blurs that line.

Then the verse. Hebrews 4:12 (NIV) calls God’s word alive and active, able to examine the thoughts and attitudes of the heart. He smiled at a detail he had noticed before: the writer of Hebrews leaned largely on the Greek scriptures, so even a verse about a living word rested on a text that had been carried across. Carried, trusted, and still worth checking.

### 06:20 — A Sunday in Las Vegas

The historical reference chimed on his console: *2003-07-20. Sunday. Las Vegas.*

He let the date pull him back. Heat already building over the valley. A weekend newspaper folded on a table, with a showroom listing for a singer returning to a Las Vegas stage after more than a decade away. Lower down, a short wire item from France: two bombs outside tax offices in Nice, sixteen people hurt.

The memory thinned out the way heat lifts off a road. What stayed were the two items, and Enka copied them into the research packet as references, not as claims. The team in Kuala Lumpur would be asked to look for anything that echoed them: a return after a long absence, a public building and the safety of the people in it.

*Look. Report. Never assume.*

### 07:15 — Koware Group, New Legon

The R&D laboratory specialist had the channel up before Enka reached the console.

“Kuala Lumpur reports a quiet night,” the specialist said. “It’s late Friday afternoon on their clock, the twenty-ninth of January, 2236. They’re ready for tasking.”

“Send them the two references.” Enka paused. “And tell them what I told myself. Similarity is a reason to look. It is not a conclusion.”

The specialist nodded and typed. Nothing about the day required a dramatic order. The team was in place, the mission was observation, and the most useful thing Koware could give them was a method.

### 09:30 (New Legon) · 17:27, Friday 29 January 2236 (Kuala Lumpur)

Rain-wet light fell through the glass at the end of the corridor. The channel opened on the team lead’s face, and behind the lead, the field analyst stood by the lift, reading a notice mounted at eye level.

It was printed in four languages — Malay, English, Mandarin and Tamil — and it told residents which stairs to use in an emergency.

“Three versions say left of the lift,” the analyst reported. “One says right. Three to one. I’d go left.”

“Three copies can come from one mistake,” the team lead said, not unkindly.

Nobody moved toward either stairwell. They did the slow, dull, sensible thing. They photographed each version, noted its height on the wall, its print, its paper. The one that said *right* hung a little lower than the others, and it was the only one carrying a printed date.

Enka watched the images arrive. “Record what the signs say,” he said. “Record what the corridor is. Don’t let one stand in for the other.”

“Understood. Observation only,” the team lead answered. “We’ll walk both routes with the building’s manager while it’s calm, before anyone needs them.”

It was the oldest safety lesson there is: know two ways out, and know them before you need them.

### 14:40 — New Legon

The afternoon had turned heavy. A few fat drops of rain ticked on the roof sheets, and the compound smelled of wet dust.

“The one that disagrees is the only one with a date,” the lab specialist said, studying the photographs.

“Which doesn’t make it right,” said Enka.

“Or wrong.”

“No.” He turned back to his study notes and wrote one line beside the Septuagint heading, the translators’ oldest problem in a modern coat: *the copies that agree may not be independent.*

Two centuries away, in a corridor that smelled of rain, four versions of one instruction waited to be believed.

## TODAY’S AUDIENCE CHALLENGE — The Notice in Four Languages

The emergency notice in the Kuala Lumpur corridor appears in four languages. Three versions say the stairs are to the left of the lift. One says right. The field analyst’s verdict is “three to one, go left.”

1. Give one reason why “three out of four agree” may not be good enough evidence.
2. Describe two checks the team could complete safely, without using either stairwell in an emergency, to find out which instruction is correct.
3. Once the team knows, what should it do about the notice itself?

2. Any two of: walk both routes with the building manager and confirm which stairwell door opens and leads to the ground level; compare against the building’s filed fire-safety floor plan; ask the facility manager or authority for the approved wording; compare dates and print versions of each notice; run a calm drill.
