### 1. A QUESTION THAT REFUSED TO DISAPPEAR

The morning in New Legon began quietly.

Enka had barely settled into his morning routine when yesterday's investigation returned to his thoughts.

Four languages.

One notice.

And a building manager who had not yet provided a satisfactory explanation.

The notice itself was not particularly remarkable. Notices appeared in buildings every day. They announced maintenance, security arrangements, changes in access procedures and sometimes emergencies.

What made this one different was the unusual combination of languages and the circumstances surrounding its appearance.

Yesterday, the team had agreed not to jump to conclusions.

Today, Enka intended to establish something more fundamental:

Who had authorised the notice?

He opened his notes and reviewed the questions raised during the previous investigation.

Was the notice officially issued by the building management?

Had it been translated from one original document?

Were all four versions prepared simultaneously?

And, perhaps most importantly, had the building's occupants received the same information?

He paused.

A document could be accurate in four languages and still communicate four different things.

That possibility deserved attention.

---

### 2. THE IMPORTANCE OF ASKING THE RIGHT PERSON

Enka contacted the building manager.

Rather than beginning with accusations or suggestions about what might have happened, he explained that the team wanted to verify the notice's origin and distribution.

The building manager was asked to establish:

* Who requested the notice.
* Who prepared its original wording.
* Who authorised the translations.
* When the different versions were displayed.
* Whether the notice had been distributed through other channels.

The questions were straightforward.

The answers, however, would determine the next stage of the investigation.

The manager was initially cautious.

He explained that notices could pass through several hands before reaching their final destination. Someone might request the information, another person might prepare the document, and a different person might arrange its display.

Enka listened.

It was precisely the sort of administrative chain that could make a seemingly simple investigation complicated.

He asked whether the original instruction could be verified against the records used to prepare the notice.

The manager agreed to check.

There was no breakthrough yet.

But there was now a clear route towards one.

---

### 3. THE LESSON OF THE DAY

Later that morning, Enka returned to his Bible reading.

The Eternal Dreamfall programme was continuing through its Week 96 Septuagint study context.

He also reviewed the YouVersion Verse of the Day for 3 November.

**Psalm 119:11 (NIV)**

"I have hidden your word in my heart that I might not sin against you."

The verse brought his attention to something that had been developing throughout the investigation.

Information was not simply something to collect.

It was something to understand, retain and use responsibly.

A person could possess numerous documents without understanding their meaning.

Likewise, someone could hear an instruction repeatedly without allowing its underlying principles to influence their decisions.

Enka considered the relationship between knowledge and responsibility.

The investigation required accuracy, but accuracy alone was not enough.

There was also the responsibility to avoid misrepresenting people, drawing premature conclusions or allowing speculation to become accepted truth.

He returned to the notice.

Four translations did not necessarily mean four independent sources.

They might all have originated from a single instruction.

Until that instruction was verified, the team would continue treating the document as evidence requiring investigation rather than proof of anything extraordinary.

### 4. THE KOWARE CONNECTION

Meanwhile, the day's organisational spotlight brought another aspect of responsibility into focus.

The Koware organisational structure identified Day 12 as part of the Human & Employment Resources management cycle.

The relevant responsibilities included Health & Safety, domestic operations and supporting personnel.

For Enka, this was an appropriate reminder that organisational systems were only as reliable as the people and procedures supporting them.

A workplace notice, for example, was not merely a piece of paper.

It could influence how people moved through a building, responded to an emergency or understood their responsibilities.

The person issuing it had to consider whether the information was accurate, understandable and accessible.

A translation that changed an instruction could create a problem even when the original document was correct.

This was why the building manager's verification mattered.

It was also why the investigation should not be rushed.

### 6. THE NEXT STEP

By the end of the day, Enka had a better-defined investigation.

The team had moved from asking what the notice said to establishing who was responsible for its creation and distribution.

That was progress, even without a dramatic discovery.

The building manager's verification remained outstanding.

Once the original instruction was located, the team could compare it with the four-language notice and establish whether the versions communicated the same information.

Until then, the investigation would remain open.

Enka closed his notes.

Sometimes the most important discovery was not finding another document.

It was identifying the person who could explain the first one.

And somewhere between four languages and one unanswered question, the investigation was beginning to reveal just how much depended on the accuracy of a single message.

Enka returned to the photographs before he returned to the questions.

Four versions of an emergency notice.

Four languages.

One instruction about which stairs occupants should use.

Three copies pointed in one direction. The fourth pointed in another.

The unusual copy also carried a date, but that alone did not establish whether it was older, newer, correct or incorrect.

Enka opened his notebook and divided a fresh page into two columns.

**OBSERVED.**

**NOT YET VERIFIED.**

Under the first heading, he recorded what could actually be established: the number of notices, their languages, the conflicting instructions, the visible date and the fact that they had appeared together.

Under the second, he placed everything that still required evidence.

Who had written the original instruction?

Who had authorised it?

Had the translations been prepared from the same approved document?

Had somebody subsequently changed one of the versions?

Most importantly, which instruction could occupants safely rely upon?

He resisted the temptation to answer any of those questions.

The previous day's investigation had established the need for a safe, manager-assisted verification. That remained the appropriate next step.

A majority of matching notices was not a substitute for an authorised evacuation instruction.

Enka knew that the difference mattered.

An incorrect instruction could put people in danger, even if it had been reproduced several times and displayed with apparent authority.

### Knowledge Must Be Handled Responsibly

The morning's Eternal Dreamfall study continued the programme's Week 96 examination of the Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation corpus.

The precise daily reading allocation was not identified in the available programme record, so Enka did not assign a passage that he could not verify.

Instead, he considered the significance of transmission.

A document could pass through several languages and several people before reaching its intended audience.

At every stage, something could be preserved, misunderstood, omitted or changed.

That thought brought him back to the day's YouVersion verse.

**Psalm 119:11 (NIV)**

> I have hidden your word in my heart that I might not sin against you.

The verse offered a different perspective on the investigation.

Knowing something was not simply a matter of receiving information. It involved retaining it, understanding it and allowing its principles to guide one's decisions.

For Enka, this meant that accuracy and integrity had to work together.

A person who collected information without examining its source could unintentionally spread misinformation.

Likewise, someone who discovered an apparent contradiction had a responsibility to investigate before announcing an explanation.

He returned to the two columns.

The first would contain what the team could demonstrate.

The second would remain open until reliable evidence became available.

---

### Safety Is More Than Displaying a Notice

The day's Koware organisational spotlight fell within the Human & Employment Resources management cycle.

Jacques Ohemeng's management responsibilities included the Health & Safety function, represented by Mme. Joycelyn Ennison, the designated Health & Safety Technician.

The programme also included domestic and operational support personnel whose responsibilities contributed to the working environment.

The relevant HS1000 programme area, *Emergency Preparedness, Response, and Life-Saving Skills*, provided a useful educational connection.

Fire safety and evacuation planning are not merely administrative exercises.

They depend on instructions being understandable, accessible, properly authorised and consistent with the actual emergency arrangements.

A notice displayed in four languages is not automatically an effective safety measure.

Its translations must communicate the same approved instruction. Its location must make it accessible. Its content must agree with the responsible organisation's emergency arrangements.

And occupants must not be left to decide which conflicting instruction is correct.

Enka recognised that the investigation was therefore about more than the identity of a document's author.

It was also about the responsibility attached to issuing information that could influence someone's safety.

The distinction between a document's existence and its authority was becoming increasingly important.

### The World Beyond the Investigation

The contemporary news briefing offered several reminders that public instructions and institutional decisions had consequences beyond the organisations issuing them.

In Ghana, the Judicial Service Staff Association's indefinite strike had disrupted court operations. The association was seeking approval of revised salaries and outstanding arrears.

Meanwhile, five transport unions had announced a planned nationwide withdrawal of commercial transport services for Wednesday, 4 November, over trading activities at designated bus stops and pedestrian walkways.

Both developments illustrated how decisions made within institutions could affect the movement, work and daily responsibilities of ordinary people.

Another announcement from the Ghana Meteorological Agency raised concerns about a developing strong El Niño event and its potential effects on water resources, agriculture, electricity generation and public health.

These were different situations, but they shared an important principle: preparedness depended on reliable information and coordinated action.

Enka also noted the Auditor-General's report that more than GH¢18 billion had been recovered through audit recommendations since 2022.

The figure was significant, but the underlying lesson was even more relevant to his investigation.

Identifying a problem was only part of the process.

The evidence had to be examined, responsibility established and corrective action implemented.

Across the continent, economic developments were presenting similar challenges. Reports concerning the proposed withdrawal of South Africa and Rwanda from US duty-free trade benefits highlighted the importance of planning for changing external conditions.

Technology and international affairs were also demanding careful interpretation.

Enka made a note not to confuse a developing news report with an established conclusion.

The same discipline applied whether he was examining an emergency notice, an audit report or an international news headline.

Information deserved scrutiny before it became a decision.

---

### A Question for the Laboratory

The supplied Millennium Probe snapshot continued to identify 27 September 2233 as the destination timestamp, within Phase 5.

The laboratory's previous operational restriction remained unchanged.

The Edubeam assignment was observational.

No new field intervention, contact authorisation or technical capability had been established.

The discrepancy between the current supplied timestamp and earlier destination records also meant that the team could not responsibly be assigned a new location on the strength of the available information.

The laboratory would need to reconcile the temporal reference and establish the correct destination context before any new deployment development could be recorded.

For Enka, the principle was familiar.

A system could produce a precise-looking result without resolving every question surrounding that result.

Precision was not the same as verification.

He returned to the photograph of the fourth notice.

It remained evidence.

It was not yet an explanation.

---

### What the Record Must Prove

By the end of the day's investigation, Enka had not discovered who authorised the four-language notice.

He had not established which version contained the correct instruction.

Nor had he obtained the original approval document.

But the investigation had become more disciplined.

The team now had a clearer record of what was visible, what remained uncertain and what evidence would be required to move forward.

The next step remained the verification of the original instruction and its authorisation trail through the appropriate building-management process.

Enka closed his notebook without filling the second column.

Sometimes the most valuable contribution an investigator could make was not an immediate explanation.

It was refusing to replace missing evidence with a convincing assumption.

Four notices could repeat the same instruction.

One could contradict them.

But the truth would not be established by counting pieces of paper.

It would be established by finding the authorised record and verifying what it actually required.

# DAY 02 — THE FIFTH MESSAGE

**Tuesday, 3 November 2026**

*Some messages are dangerous because they are misunderstood. Others become dangerous because nobody knows who sent them.*

---

## THE EMPTY SPACE

The first thing Enka noticed was not the difference between the four notices.

It was the empty space beneath them.

In the photographs forwarded from the Edubeam observation team, four versions of an emergency instruction occupied the same noticeboard. Three appeared to direct occupants toward one stairwell. The fourth appeared to direct them elsewhere.

The fourth also carried a date that deserved closer inspection.

Yesterday, the team had treated the disagreement as an unresolved safety concern.

Today, Enka noticed something else.

Each notice had been placed inside a transparent holder, but the lower portion of the board contained an empty fifth holder.

It might have held an old announcement. It might have been reserved for something else. It might never have contained anything at all.

Enka enlarged the photograph.

A pale rectangular outline showed where a sheet of paper had previously rested.

He opened his notebook.

**Observation:** One empty document holder.

**Explanation:** Unknown.

He almost smiled.

A year earlier, he might have invented three explanations before breakfast.

Now he had learned that explanations were expensive things. Once people believed them, they began making decisions as though they were facts.

His phone vibrated.

A message from the field observation channel appeared.

**FIELD TEAM — STATUS UPDATE**

*No movement through the disputed routes. Photographic records preserved. Building management contact requested. Awaiting verification.*

Enka reread the message.

Good.

Nobody had mistaken curiosity for permission.

---

## THE OTHER NOTICE

Across Ghana, another kind of public notice was attracting attention.

Commercial transport unions had announced plans for a nationwide withdrawal of services on Wednesday, 4 November, raising concerns about trading activities at bus stops and pedestrian walkways.

For commuters, the announcement raised an immediate question: how would they travel if the planned action proceeded?

Enka encountered the report while reviewing the morning's developments.

The situation was unrelated to the Edubeam team's investigation. Yet both cases reminded him how much depended on the information people received.

A transport announcement might alter someone's journey to work.

An emergency notice might determine the direction someone moved during a dangerous situation.

In either case, unclear communication could have consequences far beyond the person who wrote the message.

He wrote another sentence in his notebook.

*Information becomes responsibility when someone else is expected to act on it.*

Then he returned to the photographs.

The fifth holder remained empty.

---

## WORDS THAT SURVIVE

Later, Enka opened his Eternal Dreamfall study notes.

The current working reading context continued through the Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation corpus associated with the Hebrew Scriptures.

Its history presented a familiar problem.

Words could cross languages, generations and cultures while their readers continued asking what those words originally meant.

A translation was not merely a collection of equivalent words. Meaning also depended on context, interpretation and the choices made by the people transmitting a text.

Today's YouVersion Verse of the Day was Psalm 119:11.

Its theme was the importance of keeping God's word within one's heart so that it could guide conduct.

Enka sat quietly with the thought.

He had spent considerable time trying to understand extraordinary signals, uncertain memories and the unusual experiences that had changed his understanding of reality.

But a much simpler question stood before him.

What happened when people carried instructions they did not fully understand?

He remembered the field photographs.

Four messages.

Two apparent directions.

One building.

And people who might eventually have to depend on those instructions.

The lesson was not that every disagreement concealed something extraordinary.

It was that understanding required more than receiving information.

It required care.

---

## THE SAFETY QUESTION

The day's Koware organisational review brought the Human & Employment Resources function into focus, including Health & Safety responsibilities.

Enka considered how the investigation might be explained during a safety briefing.

If two signs pointed in different directions, an observer could not establish the correct emergency route simply by counting which instruction appeared more often.

Nor would the newest-looking notice necessarily be the authorised one.

The appropriate people would need to verify the building's approved emergency arrangements, correct conflicting information and ensure occupants received clear guidance.

The field team had no authority to test an uncertain route by sending people through it.

Their assignment remained observation-only.

Enka prepared a short message.

**ENKA — FIELD OBSERVATION CHANNEL**

*Preserve the original photographs. Record the position of each notice without altering the board. Request verification from the responsible building personnel.*

He paused before adding another sentence.

*Please ask whether the empty holder has any relevance to the current notices. Do not assume that it does.*

He sent the message.

Several minutes passed.

Then a reply appeared.

*Acknowledged. No intervention undertaken.*

For the first time that morning, Enka leaned back.

Progress did not always look like a discovery.

Sometimes it looked like a team resisting the temptation to act before it understood.

---

## THE UNEXPECTED REPLY

By afternoon, the building management contact had responded through the observation channel.

The reply did not explain the contradictory instructions.

It did not identify the person who had approved the wording.

And it did not establish which notice, if any, occupants should rely upon.

It contained only a preliminary acknowledgement.

Management would review the available documentation.

Enka could have been disappointed.

Instead, he concentrated on the distinction between an acknowledgement and a verified answer.

The response established that the concern had been received.

Nothing more.

He updated his notes.

**Confirmed within today's fictional investigation:** The inquiry has been acknowledged.

**Still unresolved:** The original instruction, approval history, translation process and correct emergency arrangements.

Then he returned to the photograph of the empty holder.

There was no reason to conclude that its missing paper had been a fifth emergency notice.

Nevertheless, the empty space had changed the nature of his question.

He had begun the morning asking why four documents disagreed.

Now he wanted to understand how the documents had reached the board in the first place.

---

## THE DISTANCE BETWEEN TWO TIMES

At the laboratory monitoring desk, the Millennium Probe presented another complication.

Its latest observation belonged to a distant future date.

The probe's reported destination coordinate had changed substantially from an earlier snapshot.

Enka examined the two records without attempting to reconcile them through imagination.

The monitoring interface showed a different temporal reading.

It did not prove that the deployed team had changed location.

It did not prove that the team had experienced the passage of the intervening years.

And it certainly did not give Enka the ability to control the displacement.

He remembered the frightening uncertainty that had followed his first anomalous experiences.

There had been moments when he wanted the extraordinary to become predictable.

He still did.

But wanting certainty was not the same as possessing it.

He recorded the discrepancy for further examination.

The Edubeam team's existing observational restrictions remained unchanged.

Whatever the probe was measuring, today's investigation still concerned something painfully ordinary.

A noticeboard.

A set of instructions.

And the possibility that someone might rely upon the wrong information.

---

## WHAT THE BOARD COULD NOT SAY

Evening approached.

Enka reviewed the day's record one final time.

No emergency route had been declared correct.

No missing document had been identified.

No extraordinary explanation had been established.

The management inquiry remained open.

The photographs remained preserved.

The field team remained under observation-only instructions.

Yet something had changed.

The investigation now had a more disciplined structure.

Rather than asking the team to find a mysterious answer, Enka had asked them to establish the history of the information.

Who prepared it?

Who approved it?

Who translated it?

Who displayed it?

And what independent records could establish whether it was safe to follow?

Outside, ordinary life continued.

People hurried home. Businesses prepared for the following day. Messages travelled from one phone to another.

Some would be accurate.

Some would be incomplete.

Some would be misunderstood.

Enka closed his notebook.

The empty holder had given him no answer.

But it had reminded him that the absence of information was not permission to invent it.

He switched off the desk lamp.

On the monitoring screen, the photograph remained.

Four notices.

One empty space.

And a question that would have to survive until tomorrow.

# THE CROPPED TRUTH

**Tuesday, 3 November 2026**

*Sometimes the most dangerous part of a message is what someone cannot see.*

---

### THE PHOTOGRAPH OF A PHOTOGRAPH

The first message that troubled Enka that Tuesday was not a warning from the Millennium Probe.

It was a photograph of another photograph.

On his screen, an ordinary mobile phone filled almost the entire frame. Someone had photographed the emergency instructions displayed in the building where the Edubeam observation team was investigating the previous day's discovery.

Four languages. One building. Conflicting directions.

But the image on the phone showed only three of the four language panels.

The fourth had been cropped away.

Enka leaned closer.

“Who sent this?”

The field observer's response arrived through the established reporting channel.

“An occupant showed it to the building office. They wanted to know which staircase their family should use.”

“Was there an emergency?”

“None reported. They were asking about the instructions before one happened.”

Enka considered that.

Yesterday, the team had been concerned about what the notices said.

Today, somebody was already passing around an incomplete version.

And somewhere beyond the photograph was a family expecting a reliable answer.

---

### THREE LANGUAGES, ONE MISSING QUESTION

Enka opened the original observation images beside the cropped screenshot.

The three visible translations appeared to agree.

The fourth translation, absent from the screenshot, pointed elsewhere.

He could almost hear the argument forming.

Three against one.

Surely the majority must be correct.

But printed instructions were not votes.

“Did the occupant ask our team to choose a route?” Enka asked.

“Yes.”

“And what did you say?”

“We explained that we couldn't verify the instruction and referred the question to the responsible building personnel.”

Enka nodded.

“Good.”

There was a brief silence.

Then the observer spoke again.

“They seemed disappointed.”

“That doesn't surprise me.”

“They wanted someone to sound certain.”

Enka watched the four panels on his screen.

“Certainty is useful,” he said. “But only when it has somewhere reliable to stand.”

For a moment, neither spoke.

The observation-only restriction had prevented the team from making a decision beyond its authority.

Yet the question remained urgent.

Someone needed to establish which emergency instructions were current, authorized and safe.

And the people responsible for the building needed to know that an incomplete image was circulating.

---

### THE NOTICE BACK HOME

In Accra, a different announcement was occupying public attention.

Five commercial transport unions had announced plans to withdraw their services on Wednesday, 4 November, in a dispute involving trading at bus stops, pedestrian access and traffic enforcement.

Enka encountered the report while reviewing the day's news.

He imagined the questions commuters would be asking.

Would the action proceed?

Would alternative arrangements be necessary?

Had anything changed since the announcement?

A notice could be accurately reported and still describe something that had not yet happened.

The distinction mattered.

He returned to the building photographs.

In one situation, people were preparing for a possible disruption.

In the other, they were trying to understand instructions that might one day guide them away from danger.

Both depended on communication that was complete, current and trustworthy.

And neither would be improved by presenting uncertainty as certainty.

---

### THE WORD WITHIN

Later, Enka returned to his Eternal Dreamfall reflections.

His working study notes were concerned with the Septuagint—the ancient Greek translation tradition through which scriptural writings had reached different readers and communities.

Languages could change.

Copies could multiply.

Interpretations could differ.

Preserving meaning demanded care.

The day's YouVersion reading, Psalm 119:11, concerned keeping God's word in one's heart so that it could guide conduct.

Enka sat with the distinction.

The psalmist was speaking about moral and spiritual formation, not technical instructions.

Nevertheless, the reading encouraged a practical question:

What responsibility comes with receiving words that other people may trust?

Knowing a statement was not the same as understanding it.

And repeating a statement did not make it reliable.

He thought about the person holding the cropped photograph.

Perhaps they had shared it because they wanted to help their family.

Good intentions, however, could not restore information missing from an image.

Enka closed his study notes and returned to work.

---

### THE RESPONSIBILITY TO ASK

The day's Koware organizational schedule brought its Human & Employment Resources and Health & Safety functions into focus.

Enka considered the problem through the lens of emergency preparedness.

A safety notice was not merely a document to be displayed.

It was information people might need to understand under pressure.

The responsible personnel would need to compare the conflicting notices with the building's current emergency arrangements, establish their approval history and ensure that occupants received reliable guidance.

An observation team could document the problem.

It could not authorize an evacuation route.

Enka sent a short request through the field channel.

“Preserve the complete observation images. Record how the cropped version differs. Ask building management to refer the conflicting instructions to the personnel responsible for the building's emergency procedures.”

A reply followed.

“Understood. No route selection or physical intervention.”

“Thank you.”

The observer hesitated.

“There is something else.”

Enka waited.

“The occupant said they had shared the picture because their family could not understand all the notices.”

For the first time that afternoon, Enka stopped looking at the screen.

The problem was no longer simply four translations disagreeing.

It was that people were trying to make decisions from information they could only partially understand.

He wrote a new question beneath his investigation notes:

*Who has been left without a dependable explanation?*

---

### THE MESSAGE THAT RETURNED

The building management office eventually acknowledged the additional concern.

The conflicting instructions would be referred for verification.

The incomplete screenshot had also been brought to its attention.

There was still no confirmed answer about the correct emergency route.

No authorization record had been produced.

No explanation had established why the translations differed.

But the inquiry had advanced.

It was no longer concerned only with the origin of the notice.

It now concerned the people expected to rely upon it.

Enka reviewed the day's observations.

The full photograph showed disagreement.

The cropped photograph concealed it.

Neither photograph, by itself, established the approved emergency arrangements.

He was about to close the file when another message appeared.

The field observer had added one final observation:

“The occupant says the cropped picture has already been forwarded to several other people.”

Enka remained still.

The incomplete message was traveling.

The verification had not caught up.

He looked again at the fourth language panel—the one missing from the circulating photograph.

Yesterday, that panel had been part of the mystery.

Today, its absence had become part of the danger.

Enka opened a fresh page in his notebook.

At the top, he wrote:

**A MESSAGE IS NOT COMPLETE SIMPLY BECAUSE IT HAS BEEN SHARED.**

Below it, he left space for the building's eventual response.

The investigation would continue.

But for tonight, the most important discovery was not a new technology, a hidden code or another journey through time.

It was the recognition that a person could act on an incomplete truth without ever knowing what was missing.

# THE CROPPED TRUTH

**Tuesday, 3 November 2026**

*Sometimes the most dangerous part of a message is what someone cannot see.*

---

### THE PHOTOGRAPH OF A PHOTOGRAPH

The first message that troubled Enka that Tuesday was not a warning from the Millennium Probe.

It was a photograph of another photograph.

On his screen, an ordinary mobile phone filled almost the entire frame. Someone had photographed the emergency instructions displayed in the building where the Edubeam observation team was investigating the previous day's discovery.

Four languages. One building. Conflicting directions.

But the image on the phone showed only three of the four language panels.

The fourth had been cropped away.

Enka leaned closer.

“Who sent this?”

The field observer's response arrived through the established reporting channel.

“An occupant showed it to the building office. They wanted to know which staircase their family should use.”

“Was there an emergency?”

“None reported. They were asking about the instructions before one happened.”

Enka considered that.

Yesterday, the team had been concerned about what the notices said.

Today, somebody was already passing around an incomplete version.

And somewhere beyond the photograph was a family expecting a reliable answer.

---

### THREE LANGUAGES, ONE MISSING QUESTION

Enka opened the original observation images beside the cropped screenshot.

The three visible translations appeared to agree.

The fourth translation, absent from the screenshot, pointed elsewhere.

He could almost hear the argument forming.

Three against one.

Surely the majority must be correct.

But printed instructions were not votes.

“Did the occupant ask our team to choose a route?” Enka asked.

“Yes.”

“And what did you say?”

“We explained that we couldn't verify the instruction and referred the question to the responsible building personnel.”

Enka nodded.

“Good.”

There was a brief silence.

Then the observer spoke again.

“They seemed disappointed.”

“That doesn't surprise me.”

“They wanted someone to sound certain.”

Enka watched the four panels on his screen.

“Certainty is useful,” he said. “But only when it has somewhere reliable to stand.”

For a moment, neither spoke.

The observation-only restriction had prevented the team from making a decision beyond its authority.

Yet the question remained urgent.

Someone needed to establish which emergency instructions were current, authorized and safe.

And the people responsible for the building needed to know that an incomplete image was circulating.

---

### THE NOTICE BACK HOME

In Accra, a different announcement was occupying public attention.

Five commercial transport unions had announced plans to withdraw their services on Wednesday, 4 November, in a dispute involving trading at bus stops, pedestrian access and traffic enforcement.

Enka encountered the report while reviewing the day's news.

He imagined the questions commuters would be asking.

Would the action proceed?

Would alternative arrangements be necessary?

Had anything changed since the announcement?

A notice could be accurately reported and still describe something that had not yet happened.

The distinction mattered.

He returned to the building photographs.

In one situation, people were preparing for a possible disruption.

In the other, they were trying to understand instructions that might one day guide them away from danger.

Both depended on communication that was complete, current and trustworthy.

And neither would be improved by presenting uncertainty as certainty.

---

### THE WORD WITHIN

Later, Enka returned to his Eternal Dreamfall reflections.

His working study notes were concerned with the Septuagint—the ancient Greek translation tradition through which scriptural writings had reached different readers and communities.

Languages could change.

Copies could multiply.

Interpretations could differ.

Preserving meaning demanded care.

The day's YouVersion reading, Psalm 119:11, concerned keeping God's word in one's heart so that it could guide conduct.

Enka sat with the distinction.

The psalmist was speaking about moral and spiritual formation, not technical instructions.

Nevertheless, the reading encouraged a practical question:

What responsibility comes with receiving words that other people may trust?

Knowing a statement was not the same as understanding it.

And repeating a statement did not make it reliable.

He thought about the person holding the cropped photograph.

Perhaps they had shared it because they wanted to help their family.

Good intentions, however, could not restore information missing from an image.

Enka closed his study notes and returned to work.

---

### THE RESPONSIBILITY TO ASK

The day's Koware organizational schedule brought its Human & Employment Resources and Health & Safety functions into focus.

Enka considered the problem through the lens of emergency preparedness.

A safety notice was not merely a document to be displayed.

It was information people might need to understand under pressure.

The responsible personnel would need to compare the conflicting notices with the building's current emergency arrangements, establish their approval history and ensure that occupants received reliable guidance.

An observation team could document the problem.

It could not authorize an evacuation route.

Enka sent a short request through the field channel.

“Preserve the complete observation images. Record how the cropped version differs. Ask building management to refer the conflicting instructions to the personnel responsible for the building's emergency procedures.”

A reply followed.

“Understood. No route selection or physical intervention.”

“Thank you.”

The observer hesitated.

“There is something else.”

Enka waited.

“The occupant said they had shared the picture because their family could not understand all the notices.”

For the first time that afternoon, Enka stopped looking at the screen.

The problem was no longer simply four translations disagreeing.

It was that people were trying to make decisions from information they could only partially understand.

He wrote a new question beneath his investigation notes:

*Who has been left without a dependable explanation?*

### THE MESSAGE THAT RETURNED

The building management office eventually acknowledged the additional concern.

The conflicting instructions would be referred for verification.

The incomplete screenshot had also been brought to its attention.

There was still no confirmed answer about the correct emergency route.

No authorization record had been produced.

No explanation had established why the translations differed.

But the inquiry had advanced.

It was no longer concerned only with the origin of the notice.

It now concerned the people expected to rely upon it.

Enka reviewed the day's observations.

The full photograph showed disagreement.

The cropped photograph concealed it.

Neither photograph, by itself, established the approved emergency arrangements.

He was about to close the file when another message appeared.

The field observer had added one final observation:

“The occupant says the cropped picture has already been forwarded to several other people.”

Enka remained still.

The incomplete message was traveling.

The verification had not caught up.

He looked again at the fourth language panel—the one missing from the circulating photograph.

Yesterday, that panel had been part of the mystery.

Today, its absence had become part of the danger.

Enka opened a fresh page in his notebook.

At the top, he wrote:

**A MESSAGE IS NOT COMPLETE SIMPLY BECAUSE IT HAS BEEN SHARED.**

Below it, he left space for the building's eventual response.

The investigation would continue.

But for tonight, the most important discovery was not a new technology, a hidden code or another journey through time.

It was the recognition that a person could act on an incomplete truth without ever knowing what was missing.

The date was the easiest thing to read on the photograph.

**WHAT THE RECORDS MAY SHOW.**  
**WHAT WE STILL DO NOT KNOW.**

Under the first, he listed the plain observations: four versions, four languages, two different route instructions, one visible date.

He stopped before filling the second column.

A date could show when a notice was printed. It could not, by itself, show who approved it, whether it replaced an earlier instruction, or whether the other copies had been translated from the same authorized source.

The third column was already crowded.

Enka opened the field channel. The team’s earlier report had proposed a careful next step: ask building management to clarify the notices and, if appropriate, accompany a safe inspection. No one was to choose a route by counting copies or treat the investigation as permission to test an evacuation path.

He dictated a short request: the approved emergency instruction, its revision or effective date, the authority responsible for approving it, and any record showing when the different language versions were prepared and displayed.

Then he added one more condition.

“If there’s a conflict in the instructions, building management needs to refer it to the people responsible for the site’s safety procedures. We document what we receive. We don’t substitute our judgment for theirs.”

The reply came later. The manager had located a folder of building records and would check whether it contained the relevant approval and distribution documents. The folder had not yet been reviewed against the photographs. No conclusion was ready.

It was not the answer Enka had hoped for. It was, he decided, a better answer than a confident one without evidence.

He returned to his notebook and wrote beneath the date in the photograph:

*Visible date: observed. Meaning and authority: unverified.*

The morning’s Eternal Dreamfall study remained in the programme’s Week 96 Septuagint context. Enka thought about how a message could travel through languages and copies—and how each transfer made careful comparison more important. He did not assign a daily passage he could not confirm.

The principle applied beyond old texts. A notice might be reproduced accurately and still lack authorization. A translation might preserve the wording and still inherit a mistake from its source. And an official-looking stamp might answer who handled a document without answering who had the authority to issue it.

At the end of the day, the original approval record had not been found. The four versions had not been reconciled. The building manager’s check remained in progress.

Enka closed the file without changing the status from **OPEN**.

One date had helped them ask a sharper question. It had not answered it.

### 05:25 — New Legon, Accra

Dawn over New Legon was slow in arriving, muffled by heavy low cloud that threatened rain before midday. Through the open louvres of the Koware residential compound, the first stirrings of traffic from the Haatso–Atomic road sounded like distant surf. 

Enka Yamoah sat at his desk, his tea cooling beside a double-spread notebook. On the left page, he had taped two high-resolution stills transmitted from Kuala Lumpur late the previous afternoon. They showed a printed building notice affixed to the polished travertine wall beside the 14th-floor lift lobby. On the right page, he had drawn a clean vertical line in blue ink, dividing the sheet into two equal columns:

**OBSERVED**  
**NOT YET VERIFIED**

He did not write hastily. In an investigation, early assumptions were like dry kindling: once lit, they burned away the patience required to look at what was actually there.

Under **OBSERVED**, he penned five brief entries:
1. *Single A4-dimension laminated plaque, mounted 148 cm from floor level.*
2. *Four parallel language columns: Malay (top), English (upper-middle), Mandarin (lower-middle), Tamil (bottom).*
3. *Content: Emergency evacuation instructions identifying designated stairwells.*
4. *Divergence: Malay, English, and Mandarin specify 'Stairwell B (North Corridor – Left of Lift Bank)'. Tamil specifies 'Stairwell A (South Service Corridor – Right of Lift Bank)'.*
5. *Physical discrepancy: Tamil text block is printed on an overlaid label strip, carrying a small stamp date: 'REV 12/04'.*

Under **NOT YET VERIFIED**, the list was longer:
1. *Who authorized the original building safety instruction?*
2. *Who prepared the translations, and were they commissioned from a single source text?*
3. *Why does the Tamil version exist as an overlay rather than an integrated print?*
4. *Does 'REV 12/04' represent an update, a correction, or an unapproved alteration?*
5. *Which stairwell is currently clear, unobstructed, and designated in the municipal fire plan?*

He capped his pen. 

Three versions said left. One said right.

Yesterday, the field analyst in Kuala Lumpur had looked at the plaque and remarked, *"Three to one. Majority rules in a hurry."* 

Enka had answered through the link before the words were fully cold: *"A majority of signs only proves that an error can be repeated three times."*

### 06:15 — The Weight of Transmission

The early hour invited reading before the compound awoke. Enka turned to the week's study. The Eternal Dreamfall curriculum was moving through Week 96: the Septuagint—the *Versio Septuaginta Interpretum*—the monumental Hellenistic translation of the Hebrew scriptures produced in Alexandria across the third and second centuries BC.

The study guide assigned no isolated daily verse, asking the reader instead to consider the mechanics of textual transmission. Seventy or seventy-two scholars, according to legend, had been summoned to render the Torah into the lingua franca of the Mediterranean world. Whether seventy translators worked in isolated cells or in collaborative consensus mattered less than the stubborn historical reality: every translation is both an act of preservation and an act of interpretation. Words carried across linguistic frontiers can gain nuance, drop precision, or calcify into traditions that subsequent generations treat as primary authority.

Beside the study guide lay the daily YouVersion Verse of the Day for Tuesday, 3 November 2026:

> *"I have hidden your word in my heart that I might not sin against you."*  
> — **Psalm 119:11 (NIV)**

The psalmist was not speaking of displaying a scroll in an entrance hall. He was speaking of internalizing truth so deeply that it governs action when pressure mounts. A sign displayed on a wall could be photographed, admired, or glanced at in passing. But unless its instruction was verified, understood, and integrated with the reality of the structure, it remained merely ink on paper. Accuracy was not an academic preference; it was an ethical duty.

### 06:50 — A Memory of Las Vegas

A low hum from the console indicated a time-reference lock. The historical-reference monitor flashed its coordinates: *2003-07-20 06:51:45 UTC. Sunday.*

The date opened a vivid window of memory. July 2003. Las Vegas, Nevada. The morning heat was already radiating from asphalt parking lots before seven o'clock, the glare of the desert sun turning the neon signs of the Strip into faded skeletons. He remembered sitting at a laminate diner counter off Flamingo Road, drinking iced water, reading the Sunday edition of the *Las Vegas Review-Journal*. 

One small wire report had stuck in his memory: across the Atlantic in Nice, France, two small explosive devices had detonated in the early hours outside two regional treasury offices, injuring sixteen people. The report emphasized that while the damage was structural, the casualties had occurred because people in the adjacent municipal offices had moved toward an unverified rear exit corridor that was choked with debris. 

*Emergency wayfinding fails at the exits, not in the rooms.*

He jotted the memory into the margin of his notebook. It was not a mystical premonition; it was a precedent. Whether in 2003, 2026, or two centuries into an uncharted future, when alarms sound, human beings follow whatever instruction looks official. If the official notice is wrong, authority becomes a trap.

"Good timing, Joycelyn," Enka said, stepping alongside them. "Suppose you discovered that our fire evacuation notices listed the east stairwell in English, but the French and Twi versions directed people toward the courtyard storage gate. What would you do?"

"I would flag the notices as invalid immediately, issue an interim verbal briefing to all department heads, and pull the original building compliance certificate from our archives. You never allow people to guess which sign is correct. A notice without an audit trail is a hazard."

Jacques Ohemeng nodded in agreement. "And you trace who put the conflicting notices up. A safety instruction isn't just graphic design. It requires departmental authorization."

Enka thanked them and walked through the security airlock into the R&D telemetry room. The conversation was grounded in the reality of New Legon, but its logic was universal.

"Did they provide it?" Enka asked.

"Not immediately," the team lead replied. "The duty officer on the desk is a junior clerk. He told us that signage in the residential towers is subcontracted to an external facilities management vendor. He pulled an older maintenance log, but there's a problem: the building management system underwent a full digital migration four months ago. The paper records for Tower C were boxed for digitization."

"What about the physical stairwells?"

"We walked the perimeter corridors from the exterior lobby with the clerk accompanying us," the field analyst reported, holding up a handheld scanner feed. "Stairwell B—the north stairwell, the one three signs point to—is wide, pressurized, and leads directly to the exterior assembly courtyard. Stairwell A—the south stairwell, the one the Tamil label points to—terminates at the basement maintenance dock, which has an automated magnetic roll-down gate."

Enka leaned forward. "If the power drops or the magnetic latch locks down in an emergency..."

"...Stairwell A becomes a dead end," the team lead finished quietly. "A crowd funneling into Stairwell A would be trapped at the basement threshold unless the manual emergency override is activated."

"And the label strip on the Tamil notice?" Enka asked.

"We looked closer with optical magnification," the field analyst answered. "The adhesive is fresh. It wasn't applied by the building contractor. The typeface doesn't match the original plate. Someone placed that label over the original Tamil text."

Enka stood up from the console. The shape of the problem had transformed completely.

This was no longer a question of translation error.

Someone had deliberately altered a life-safety route in one language, redirecting anyone who read that specific notice into a cul-de-sac.

"Maintain strictly observation-only status," Enka commanded steadily. "Do not peel the label. Do not touch the plaque. We establish the chain of custody."

"Understood, HQ," the team lead acknowledged. "Standing by for supervisory arrival."

Enka closed his notebook. The two columns were no longer empty. The observed facts were terrifying in their simplicity. But the origin of the label, the identity of its author, and the intent behind it remained waiting in the dark.

Outside the New Legon office the sky hung low and grey over Accra, with rain forecast and the heat already climbing toward thirty-two degrees. Enka took his Eternal Dreamfall reading first, as he did most mornings. Week ninety-six: the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures, made in Alexandria by many hands over many years. The plan named no passage for the day, so he borrowed only the idea. A translation is a copy that somebody once judged faithful, and someone always has to be willing to put a name to that judgement.
The day’s verse was Psalm 119:11, about hiding the word in the heart. Enka smiled at the irony. The notices in Kuala Lumpur were hardly hidden. They were pinned up where everyone could read them. What nobody could see was where they had come from.
The Health & Safety Technician was at the long table outside the kitchen, checking the extinguisher log with the suspicion of a person who had been handed logs before. Enka laid the four photographs down in front of them.
“Don’t tell me which stair,” he said. “Tell me how this happens.”
The Technician studied the prints for a long moment. “I won’t pick a stair from a photograph. Nobody honest could.” They held up a hand and counted. “Before I trust a notice, I want four things. Who issued it. When it took effect. Which version it is. And what happened to the old copies.”
“The old copies.”
“Everyone remembers to hang the new sheet. Almost nobody remembers to take the old one down.”
They tested the idea on Koware’s own board by the stairs. The Janitor, who knew that board better than anyone in the building, was already there with a stepladder. At the Technician’s nod, the Janitor lifted one corner of the current evacuation sheet. Behind it, curling at the edges, was an older sheet with a different assembly point.
“That’s been there since before the repaint,” the Janitor said, without defensiveness, only accuracy. “Nobody told me it was supposed to come down.”
Nobody ever had. The old sheet went into the Technician’s folder with the date it was withdrawn written beside it, and Enka stood looking at the bare pins a little longer than the moment needed.
Back at his desk he wrote to the field team in Kuala Lumpur. The assignment was still observation only, so the message said so first: do not touch the board, do not test either stairwell. Then it asked, through the building manager, for five things: the revision number and effective date of the current notice, who had approved it, who had received it, what had been done with earlier copies, and who in the building owned the evacuation plan.
At the laboratory, the probe log still held two timestamps that would not agree. Enka marked both unreconciled and wrote nothing between them. A reading was not a location. A log was not a decision.
Noon brought the rain, and with it the news that commercial transport unions had announced a withdrawal of services for Wednesday, 4 November. It was an announcement, nothing more, but staff would plan around it anyway. Enka and the Technician wrote a notice for Koware’s own people in three lines. It named who issued it and the date, and it was marked Version 1. It said what was known: the unions had announced a stoppage. It said what was not: whether services would actually stop. Any change would arrive as Version 2, with a new time on it.
“Dull,” the Technician said, reading it back.
“Good,” said Enka. “Dull is what people follow at six in the morning.”
The reply from Kuala Lumpur came late in the afternoon: a photograph of one page from the building manager’s binder. A revision log, two entries. Revision A carried the same date as the odd notice, and beside it, a name. Revision B was newer. Beside it were only initials.
The manager had added a line of their own. The translations, they wrote, were printed separately, in-house, and the question would go to the person responsible for the site’s safety procedures. The manager had not said which stairwell the plan named, and the team had not asked.
Enka read the page three times.
It did not answer the question. It changed it. The odd notice was probably the older one, but older did not mean wrong. Revision B might be the careful correction. It might equally be a mistake, printed three times and pinned up in several languages, and repetition did nothing to tell the two apart. Until someone put a name beside those initials, the board was an argument, not an instruction.
He sent the team one line: hold, observe, record.
Evening came with the rain still falling. Enka squared the photographs on his desk, three arrows in agreement and one against, and found he no longer thought of them as a vote. A sheet in a frame could look exactly like authority. Only the name beside it could prove it.
