## The Date That Would Not Stay Still

The Probe display had changed again.

Enka stood beside the laboratory console at Koware’s headquarters in New Legon, Accra. On the screen, the destination read **26 June 2221**. Below it sat a second timestamp: the current date, **5 November 2026**.

He read both twice.

“Another different year,” he said.

The R&D specialist did not look surprised. “Different from the earlier readings, yes. But that doesn’t automatically mean the instrument has failed.”

Enka turned from the screen. “Then tell me what it means.”

The specialist opened the run record. Historical reference. Current date. Phase boundaries. Oscillation index. Amplitude. Probe value. Each field occupied its own line, with its source noted beside it.

“The dates are changing intentionally,” the specialist said. “The Millennium Probe recalculates its output in relation to the historical data and the current date. It isn’t a fixed calendar appointment. Each reading is the result of several linked calculations.”

“So we shouldn’t call a changed date a changed destination until we know what the reading represents.”

“Exactly. First we need the run inputs and the calculation record. Then we can say what changed.”

Enka looked again at the values. The timestamp was precise to the second; the explanation was not yet complete. Precision could describe a result without answering every question about it.

“Keep the raw response,” he said. “Keep the earlier readings too. Don’t replace one with another.”

The specialist added the new response to the run record. “And the team?”

“Their supplied physical position is Kuala Lumpur,” Enka said. “That tells us where they are, not what this new timestamp means for their temporal position. We shouldn’t collapse those into one field.”

The specialist nodded and wrote the distinction into the notes.

For a moment, the room settled into the quiet hum of equipment. Then Enka noticed an older design sheet on the console—the one with his own handwriting across the margin.

The specialist followed his gaze. “You designed the Probe?”

“I designed its original scheme,” Enka said. “And the cloned Edubeam was first operated solely by the Micro Computer Technology Institute of London. That history matters. If we’re going to understand what the instrument does, we have to preserve how it was built, not just what it displays.”

The specialist studied the sheet. “Then the design record belongs beside the readings.”

“Beside them, but not in place of them.”

Enka opened a new page in his notebook and drew three headings:

**WHAT THE SOURCE RETURNED**  
**WHAT THE CALCULATION MAY MEAN**  
**WHAT WE HAVE NOT VERIFIED**

Under the first, he recorded the displayed Probe date and the raw values. Under the second, he wrote that the system’s changing output was intentional according to the specialist’s explanation. Under the third, he left room for the exact calculation pathway and the relationship between the Probe timestamp and the deployed team’s temporal position.

The four-language notice investigation remained unresolved as well. Earlier drafts had explored different records and explanations, but none had been accepted as the settled account. Enka would not allow an attractive theory about the Probe to turn into a shortcut for the notice mystery—or the other way around.

“Two open questions,” he said. “One about who authorised the notice. One about how to interpret the Probe.”

“And the same rule for both?” asked the specialist.

“Record what we know. Label what we infer. Keep looking for the evidence that would settle it.”

The specialist saved the new entry without overwriting the earlier ones.

Across the screen, the dates remained different. The difference no longer looked like a contradiction by itself. It was a result that needed its working preserved.

Enka closed his notebook.

A changing reading could be intentional. It could also be misunderstood. The difference between those possibilities would not be settled by staring harder at the latest number.

It would be settled by keeping the record—and asking the right question of it.

# THE DATE THAT WAS NEVER A DEPARTURE

The first thing Enka noticed was the date.

It had changed again.

On the laboratory's central monitor, the Millennium Probe now displayed **26 June 2221**. Yesterday's archived reading had pointed considerably further into the future.

At Koware headquarters in New Legon, Accra, the morning's work was already underway. Four photographs of conflicting emergency notices remained open on the adjacent screen. Three directed occupants toward one stairwell. The fourth pointed elsewhere.

The previous day's investigation had produced a distribution register. It showed that four language versions had circulated, but it had not established who authorized the instructions.

Now, with the Probe apparently travelling backwards through its own projected calendar, a second uncertainty threatened to distract everyone from the first.

“Have we lost our position?” Enka asked.

The R&D Lab specialist studied the telemetry and shook his head.

“No. We haven't lost anything.”

“Then why does the date keep changing?”

For the first time that morning, the specialist smiled.

“Because you designed it to.”

Enka looked up.

“Explain.”

The specialist brought the calculation interface onto the main display.

“The Millennium Probe was never intended to behave like an ordinary clock. It doesn't simply add a fixed number of years to today's date. It calculates candidate temporal references from several inputs: our historical reference, our present date, the configured amplitude, and the oscillation parameters.”

He pointed to the latest reading.

“Look carefully. The production clock is still 5 November 2026. Our historical reference points to 21 July 2003. But the calculated probe date is 26 June 2221. The calculation changes as its inputs and sampling conditions change. That variation is intentional.”

“So a different date doesn't necessarily indicate a malfunction?”

“Exactly. And it certainly doesn't mean the team in Kuala Lumpur has physically moved to another year.”

The distinction mattered.

The Edubeam's deployed observation team remained assigned to the unresolved investigation in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia. Their actual mission position was a separate matter from the Probe's latest calculated temporal target.

A number on a monitor was not proof of a journey.

---

Enka leaned against the desk.

He remembered why he had built the system that way.

Years earlier, the Edubeam had been operated solely by the Micro Computer Technology Institute of London. Enka had subsequently established a cloned version, adapting its experimental capabilities for the research ambitions that would eventually become part of Blue Crown's work.

The Millennium Probe had been his own design.

He had wanted more than a machine that could identify a future date. He wanted an instrument capable of examining relationships between historical conditions, present decisions and possible future outcomes.

A fixed destination could encourage researchers to mistake one calculation for the only possible result.

A changing probe demanded something more difficult.

Interpretation.

The specialist interrupted his thoughts.

“There's another important distinction, sir. These values describe the model's calculation. They don't independently prove that an event happened, that a future condition exists, or that a person has arrived somewhere.”

Enka nodded slowly.

“Then we should teach that distinction properly.”

The timing was fortunate.

The Director of Academic Programs had arrived for a scheduled discussion.

---

The director entered carrying a slim training folder.

“I've been reviewing the Kuala Lumpur documentation,” the director said. “The trouble isn't simply that the notices disagree. It's that someone could learn the wrong lesson from them.”

Enka invited the director to continue.

“If we use these materials in a training exercise, learners might assume the most frequently repeated instruction must be correct. Others might trust whichever notice looks newest.”

“And neither assumption is sufficient,” Enka replied.

“Precisely.”

The director placed a sheet on the table.

It contained five headings:

**Origin. Authorization. Translation. Distribution. Effective status.**

“These are different questions,” the director explained. “A distribution register answers one of them. It doesn't automatically answer the other four.”

The Academic Registrar's function would be important in any future training programme: preserving reliable versions of learning materials and recording which edition learners had actually received. Academic outreach would likewise require clear, accessible explanations rather than merely producing more copies.

The director proposed a practical exercise. Learners would receive the four notices and the existing register, then identify what could be established from the evidence and what remained uncertain.

“But we cannot circulate the exercise as real emergency guidance,” the director added. “It must be unmistakably identified as a training case.”

Enka approved the idea as a draft for further review.

He remembered his morning reading from Second Timothy: the responsibility to handle truth carefully rather than merely repeat words confidently.

The principle seemed unusually relevant.

A message could be beautifully translated and dangerously misunderstood.

---

At midday, the laboratory received another communication from Kuala Lumpur.

The field analyst's voice emerged through a brief burst of static.

“The building manager has acknowledged our request. The original approval record has not been supplied.”

“Has anyone tested the routes?” Enka asked.

“No. We remain in observation mode.”

“Good.”

“The manager is asking whether our team can confirm which stairwell should be used.”

Enka glanced at the director.

There it was again: the temptation to turn incomplete research into immediate authority.

The director spoke first.

“We cannot certify a physical escape route from photographs and an incomplete administrative record.”

The R&D specialist agreed.

“Nor should the Edubeam team perform an unauthorized inspection.”

Enka returned to the communications channel.

“Please explain that the responsible building safety personnel must assess the routes and confirm the current emergency arrangements. Our team must not alter the notices or test either disputed stairwell.”

The field analyst acknowledged the instruction.

The director opened the Buzzjuice Health & Safety training material on hazard identification and risk management.

“This is why risk assessment comes before control selection,” the director said. “You identify the hazard, establish what is known, and involve the people responsible for choosing and verifying the appropriate safeguards.”

A contradictory safety notice was itself a problem requiring attention.

But an unqualified observer choosing between two uncertain routes could create another.

---

Later that afternoon, Enka returned to the historical reference.

**21 July 2003. Las Vegas, Nevada.**

The research file contained a contemporary report about Clark County schools waiting for legislative budget approval before proceeding confidently with teacher recruitment.

He found the parallel striking.

An institution might possess candidate lists, plans and urgent needs. None of those things removed the importance of the authorization on which the next decision depended.

Twenty-three years later, in Ghana, the day's news offered another example. The Bank of Ghana had warned against digital lending applications operating without the necessary regulatory approval.

Something could be widely available without being properly authorized.

The lesson crossed industries, continents and centuries.

The director also drew attention to reports about Ghanaian students pursuing education across Europe.

“Education opens doors,” the director observed. “But the responsibility of an educational institution doesn't end when information crosses a border. Its records, claims and instructions must remain trustworthy.”

Enka looked again at the four notices.

Different languages. Different readers. One building.

And still no verified source instruction.

---

As the day approached its end, the laboratory specialist returned to the Probe.

“There's something else worth recording,” the specialist said.

The display showed the oscillation index, amplitude and calculated probe value beside the historical and production timestamps.

“The calculated future reference is a result of the model, not an instruction to deploy. If the system produces another date tomorrow, that alone won't establish any movement in the field.”

Enka considered the phrase.

“Then the important question isn't simply where the Probe points.”

“No.”

“It's what the calculation actually tells us.”

The specialist nodded.

“Exactly.”

At that moment, a new message arrived from Kuala Lumpur.

The building manager's records office had identified a second register associated with the notices.

Its heading concerned **authorizations and effective dates**.

But the manager had not yet obtained the document itself. Access required a request through the responsible safety office.

Enka studied the message.

Yesterday, they had found proof of distribution.

Today, they had learned where another piece of the authorization trail might be kept.

They still did not know what it would reveal.

“Record the lead,” he said. “Not the conclusion.”

The director closed the training folder.

“And tomorrow?”

“We follow the evidence. We don't teach certainty before we've earned it.”

Outside, the afternoon light settled over New Legon.

On one laboratory screen, the Millennium Probe continued its careful calculations across centuries.

On the other, four notices remained suspended in uncertainty.

For the first time that day, Enka saw the connection clearly.

**A changing date was not necessarily a failed calculation.**

**A distributed instruction was not necessarily an authorized instruction.**

Both demanded that the person interpreting the evidence understand what the evidence could—and could not—prove.

The laboratory fell quiet.

Then the specialist opened a new record.

**AUTHORIZATION REGISTER — REQUEST PENDING.**

The answer was not there yet.

But the next question had become clearer.

What additional evidence should the investigators seek before treating any notice as authoritative, and how should they handle the uncertainty in the meantime?**

At Koware headquarters in New Legon, Accra, the R&D Lab confirmed that the Millennium Probe’s shifting dates are not an error. The probe is an intentional calculation system built from historical reference data and the current date, producing a deliberately oscillating “probe time” rather than a single fixed timestamp. 

The live probe response placed its internal probe date at **2235-11-03T16:53:36Z**, while reporting the actual current time as **2026-11-02T10:36:53Z**. Its active phase runs from **16 October to 20 December 2026**, with an oscillation index of approximately **0.0337** and an amplitude of roughly **76,644 days**. 

## Enka’s Original Design

The R&D Lab specialist explained that the probe was originally designed by **Enka**, Koware’s corporate manager and the creator of the cloned Edubeam system. That earlier Edubeam version had been operated solely by the Micro Computer Technology Institute of London, tying the probe’s lineage directly to the archive’s foundation-era technology. 

According to the specialist, the changing dates are the point: the probe compares a historical anchor—**20 July 2003, 17:24:37 UTC**—against present conditions, then projects a calculated destination date. The result is not a prediction of the future in the ordinary sense, but a measurement of drift between recorded history, the present, and the probe’s calibrated target. 

## Destination: Kuala Lumpur

The probe’s destination remains **Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia**, where the deployed team is already positioned. Their task is to observe whether the probe’s calculated convergence point produces a verifiable signal, rather than merely another shifting number on a screen. 

Back in Accra, the Koware team treated the latest oscillation index as unusually low. A value near zero suggests the probe is close to one of its calculated phase boundaries, making the Kuala Lumpur deployment more important than routine monitoring. 

## The Unsigned Fourth Notice

The chronicle connects today’s probe activity to the earlier **Unsigned Fourth Notice** incident, in which Enka’s historical location was recorded as Las Vegas, Nevada. The notice’s unresolved authorship now takes on new weight: if Enka designed both the probe and the cloned Edubeam, the notice may have been less a warning than an instruction left in plain sight. 

The R&D Lab’s position was cautious: the probe proves that dates can be calculated, not that the notice’s sender has been identified. Still, the team agreed that the Kuala Lumpur team should preserve all raw probe readings before the phase closes on **20 December 2026**.
