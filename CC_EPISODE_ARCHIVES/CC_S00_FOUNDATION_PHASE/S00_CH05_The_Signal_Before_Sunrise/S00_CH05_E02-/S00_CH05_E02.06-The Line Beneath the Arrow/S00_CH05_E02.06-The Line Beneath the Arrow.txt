# CLASSIES CHRONICLES
## The Line Beneath the Arrow

**Monday, 9 November 2026**  
*Provisional daily chronicle · Formal episode/day assignment pending editorial calendar confirmation*

Four emergency notices occupied the long table at Koware's fictional New Legon headquarters. Three pointed toward an east stairwell. The fourth pointed toward a courtyard corridor. They looked almost identical until someone followed the arrows.

Johnson Ortsin unfolded a site plan. It was 9:07 on Monday morning, and the Programme Manager had already arranged the day's tasks into a neat sequence. His finger stopped at the point where the printed plans disagreed.

“If three copies say east, we have a majority,” he said.

“A majority of paper,” Derek Shiraska replied. “Not a verified exit.”

Johnson looked up. He was accustomed to solving delays by scheduling the next action. But Derek, the Production Coordinator, had drawn two columns on his cue sheet: *what we can rehearse* and *what must not be tested*. The disputed route was in the second.

Matthias Agbenorku arrived carrying a chipped cup of tea and the weekend's unfinished operations notes. In the proposed field report from Kuala Lumpur, the building manager had denied issuing the notices. A distribution work order, marked **44-E**, appeared to account for their installation, but not for who had authorized the instruction inside them.

“So they were put up properly,” Matthias said, “and we still don't know whether they tell anyone the right thing to do.”

Enka, listening at the end of the table, thought of an old account in Joshua: worn sandals and stale bread had looked convincing enough to settle an agreement before anyone asked the question that mattered. He had also lingered that morning over Psalm 119:105, with its image of a lamp illuminating a path. A lamp was useful because it helped someone see where to place the next foot—not because it chose a destination for them.

“Let's not argue about which arrow wins,” Enka said. “Find out who had the right to draw it.”

The team drafted a narrow request to the Kuala Lumpur facility's responsible safety officer. Until a verified procedure could be established locally, nobody in the Classies field team would conduct a walk-through based on the disputed notices. Qualified site personnel—not an Accra meeting or a fictional machine—would decide what interim emergency guidance the building could safely use.

By lunchtime Johnson had changed his five-step plan. Its first item was no longer *rehearse*. It was *establish authority and verify the current site plan*. Derek's rehearsal would happen on paper only. Matthias was relieved, though he did not say so. He had been carrying too many tasks, and the cup in his hand had gone cold.

A news alert made the problem feel closer to home. Electricity-company maintenance notices listed different Accra communities on different days: parts of Adenta New Site and other areas on Monday, and parts of New Legon on Thursday. The headquarters' exact supply status was not confirmed by the list. Matthias circled *Thursday* and asked for an equipment-readiness check rather than declaring that the power would fail at their building.

“Even a genuine notice has an edge,” he told Johnson. “You have to read all the way to it.”

Johnson quietly took two of Matthias's pending coordination tasks. Derek kept both columns on his cue sheet. The change was small; the meeting no longer sounded like a contest to be the first person with an answer.

At **14:10 in Accra—22:10 in Kuala Lumpur**—a fictional field update reached the group: the regional records office could trace Work Order 44-E to an older administrative file transfer. A box matching the transfer log had been located in storage. It had not been catalogued since the reorganization. It had not been opened.

Johnson put down his pen. “We found the box.”

“We found where the box is,” Derek corrected, gently this time.

Then another complication emerged. Even if the document inside authorized one route, the differently directed notice might still be a design error rather than a translation error. Someone would have to compare the original instruction with the building's current layout and each language version. Finding an author would not, by itself, make a corridor safe.

At **21:43 in Accra**, a stored Millennium snapshot entered the evening review. It recorded a present-reference instant of **21:29:08 UTC, 9 November 2026**, and a Probe coordinate of **22:18:02 UTC, 20 February 2174**—**06:18 on 21 February** in Kuala Lumpur. The supplied historical reference remained **21 July 2003, 17:34:47 UTC**, or **10:34 in Las Vegas** on that July morning.

The three readings sat in different columns. None placed Enka in Las Vegas that evening; none proved that the Kuala Lumpur team had travelled to 2174.

Enka opened a July 2003 Las Vegas newspaper brief. Police had described a fatal road collision and said a driver had caused it; the same report said the driver had not been charged at publication. The distinction was solemn, not a puzzle to be solved. A report could record an allegation without becoming a verdict.

On the conference screen, the projected future looked impossibly precise. On the table, four ordinary sheets of paper still disagreed about a stairwell.

A final message arrived from Kuala Lumpur, where Tuesday morning had already begun: the storage custodian would not release the sealed box without a documented request from the responsible office.

Johnson reached for the pen he had set aside. This time he didn't draw another arrow. He wrote a name beside the request—a role, not an accusation—and left the disputed directions unendorsed.

Beyond the window, Accra's late traffic moved under streetlights. The next step was visible. The destination was not.

**Working title: The Space Between Two Reports**

At New Legon, the operations room had one rule about the Millennium display: a number could begin a question, but it could not finish one.

Enka read the three times on the monitor again.

The historical reference pointed to **21 July 2003**. The production snapshot was dated **9 November 2026**. The Probe’s destination reading pointed to **20 February 2174**.

On the map, the Probe destination was Kuala Lumpur. The deployed team’s position was listed there too—but the screen did not show who was with them, what they had seen, or whether the reading had been checked against a second source.

Enka set his pen beside the keyboard. “Then we don’t turn a coordinate into a conclusion.”

A colleague had brought a draft incident note. Its first sentence read: *The team has confirmed a signal from the future.*

Enka crossed out *confirmed*.

“What do we know?” she asked.

“We have a supplied snapshot,” he said. “It gives us a date and a destination. We don’t have a verified live response, a team report, or an approved episode record to tell us what happened next.”

The words sounded cautious. They also left the room with a problem: the team might be waiting for guidance, and silence could be mistaken for permission to proceed.

Enka replaced the sentence with three lines:

*The supplied Probe snapshot indicates a future-dated reading.*  
*The deployed team’s reported position is Kuala Lumpur.*  
*No verified team observation or live endpoint response is available in this run.*

“Send that,” he said. “Ask them to confirm their condition and location before anyone changes the plan.”

The colleague hesitated over the blank field marked **NEXT ACTION**. A confident instruction would look more useful. It might also send people toward a signal whose meaning nobody had established.

Enka tapped the field. “Write what we can safely ask—not what we wish we knew.”

She entered the check-in request, marked the reading **unverified**, and left the mission state unchanged. No one in the room called the future timestamp a warning, a promise, or a rescue.

For a moment, the three dates remained side by side: a past that could be remembered, a present that needed checking, and a future that could only be approached as a possibility.

Enka closed the draft note, not the case.

**What safeguard did the team choose before acting on the future-dated reading?**

**THE REHEARSAL WITHOUT A STAIRWELL**
*Classies Chronicles · Season 00, Chapter 05, Episode 02 · Chronicle 06 (provisional) · Monday, 9 November 2026*

**04:10 — New Legon**

Enka had his notebook open before the compound generators found their voices. The week's reading had crossed from the Hebrew to the Greek: the Septuagint's Deuteronomy, then Joshua. Two passages stayed with him. In Deuteronomy, no one is judged on a single witness; a matter stands on two or three. In Joshua, travellers arrive at the camp in patched sandals with dry, crumbling bread, and the Israelites, persuaded by how worn it all looks, sample the provisions and make a treaty without seeking counsel.

The day's verse was Proverbs 20:3: stepping back from a quarrel is honourable, while fools rush in.

He wrote one line in the margin: *Evidence that looks old is not evidence that is true.*

**09:00 — Koware headquarters**

Week two began, as Mondays did, around the long table. Johnson Ortsin, the Programme Manager, unfolded a site map and folded it into a perfect square, which was how people knew he was thinking. A steel-edged field notebook lay at his elbow. Matthias Agbenorku, Director of Operations, set a scarred leather binder beside a chipped enamel cup of tea. Derek Shiraska, the Production Coordinator, tapped the conference microphone's stand to check it by touch, and a roll of red-and-white stage tape sat beside him like a small flag.

"Four notices," Johnson said. "Four languages. Three send people left of the lift, one sends them right, and only that one carries a date. Nobody has told us who put any of them there." He drew five boxes and joined them with arrows. "Gather, compare, test, decide, report. Decision by Wednesday."

Matthias studied the boxes for a long minute. "Five steps. But one question in here is the one the others wait on, and we haven't asked it."

"That sounds like a delay."

"It sounds like a dependency."

Derek was looking at the third box. "Test. Test how? Nobody rehearsed these notices. Not the printer, not the translators, not the residents. We're about to ask a team in another country to behave as though one of them were true."

"We're not testing the stairs," Johnson said.

"Good. Then rehearse the checking. Paper, voices, a floor plan if the building has one. A tabletop. Nobody walks anywhere."

"That's two days."

"It's the part of the schedule that finds the mistake before an audience does." Derek's voice had an edge Johnson recognised from every production meeting about every deadline.

Johnson had a word ready for that edge, the word he used for people who hesitated: *variance*. He heard himself about to say it. He set it down instead, along with the map.

Enka, who had come in quietly and taken the last chair, cleared his throat. "Label every box. Known, assumed, unverified. A notice that won't say who wrote it is only a very confident piece of paper."

Johnson wrote the three words in the margin. For the first time that morning, nobody was quarrelling.

**11:34 — Headquarters (time of receipt)**

The Kuala Lumpur report arrived with a receipt stamp. The team lead sounded calm. The building manager had agreed to meet them. He had said the notices did not come from his office and that he could not say whose they were. The front desk could say roughly when they went up, not who ordered them. All four were photographed where they hung. None had been moved.

"Should we walk the left-hand route while it's quiet?" the lead asked.

"Not yet," Johnson said. "Keep all four. Act on none of them."

Across the table, Matthias wrote something on an index card, slid it into his binder, and did not read it aloud.

**13:15 — Headquarters**

On his tablet Matthias had the Electricity Company of Ghana's outage notice for the week, a notice that did most things right. It named the utility, the days and the hours, and for Monday it listed communities: Alajo, Abelemkpe, parts of Adenta New Site, Pantang Hospital, Kente Junction. Then came three words that did a great deal of work: *and surrounding areas*. New Legon was not on the list. He read the phrase twice anyway. A good notice still leaves a question at its edges.

The national forecast mentioned thunderstorms. He added a generator check to his binder, then looked at the cup, which had been refilled three times, and at the weekend's worth of vendor calls he had been carrying alone.

"Give me two of those," Johnson said.

Matthias hesitated, then slid over the vendor calls and the generator check. It was the first thing he had handed off in the chapter without being asked twice.

Derek was already ruling a cue sheet with two columns. "Call time and show time. Never one column for both." Johnson opened his mouth to merge them. "Keep both," he said instead, and Derek, startled, nearly smiled.

Nobody can locate them."

The room was quiet for a moment.

Gloria sat down slowly. "So the notices exist. The distribution records exist. The authorization record references a source. But the source references a set of files that were moved — and the moved files haven't been found."

"That is an accurate summary," Moses said.

"We have a complete chain of custody," James said, "except for the first link."

He walked to the whiteboard and added five words to Gloria's third column.

*The original instruction itself.*

***

In the R&D laboratory on the building's lower floor, Obed Ali was running the morning calibration sequence on the Millennium Probe's telemetry display when the new reading resolved.

The phosphorescent readout had shifted again.

```
PROBE_ISO:         2174-02-20T22:18:02Z
NOW_ISO:           2026-11-09T22:09:20Z
PHASE_INDEX:       5
PHASE_LABEL:       Oct 16 – Dec 20
PHASE_START_ISO:   2026-10-16T00:00:00Z
PHASE_END_ISO:     2026-12-20T23:59:59Z
OSCILLATION_INDEX: 0.04054640
AMPLITUDE_DAYS:    77,150.2723
PROBE_VALUE_DAYS:  53,794.0340
```

Last Friday, the harmonic calculation had placed the Probe's displacement coordinate in August 2215. This morning, it rested in **February 2174** — forty-one years earlier in the Probe's own calculated future.

Obed noted the shift without alarm. He added the new snapshot to the running log, labeled with the retrieval timestamp and the live `now_iso` value, and archived it alongside the Friday and Thursday records. The curve was oscillating as designed. What had changed was the position on that curve, not the integrity of the instrument.

He was writing up the calibration note when Robert Amegashie knocked on the laboratory door and stepped inside.

"James would like you at the conference room," Robert said. "Something about a register with a gap in it."

Obed set down his stylus. "Every register has a gap. The question is whether anyone was supposed to fill it."

He picked up his blue countdown timer, out of habit, and followed Robert upstairs.

***

Enka was participating in the morning session remotely.

His voice came through the conference room speaker in the measured, unhurried way that had become familiar across years of cross-continental calls. He had received the same overnight transmission as Gloria and had been reading it during his own early morning in Las Vegas, where the historical reference had placed an independent anchor: **21 July 2003, 17:41 UTC.**

That date had nothing to do with the current investigation. It was the fixed historical origin point from which the Probe's displacement mathematics were calculated — the anchor around which the harmonic amplitude worked its long swing. The Probe's telemetry was not a travel record. It did not confirm anyone's physical location. It confirmed only the current result of a mathematical relationship between a known past date and the present moment.

The field team in Kuala Lumpur had not moved. They were still at their observation position, awaiting instructions.

"Before we go further," Enka said through the speaker, "I want to be clear about one thing. The question of where the original notice came from is real and important. But it's a separate question from whether anyone is physically in danger right now. Has the safety coordinator confirmed that the notices are being suspended while the source is investigated?"

Moses checked his clipboard. "Confirmed. The building management was informed on Friday. The notices are marked under review. No new guidance will be issued until the source is verified."

"Good," Enka said. "Then the immediate safety concern is managed, even if the documentary question isn't. We're not operating against a clock."

James appreciated the clarification. In his experience, investigations rarely collapsed because of insufficient urgency. They usually collapsed because urgency was manufactured where patience was needed.

"The Eternal Dreamfall reading this week," he said, "is from the Wisdom literature. Job's friends were certain. Job was uncertain. The text is interested in which one was asking the right questions."

Gloria smiled without looking up from her notes. "You've been reading ahead."

"I read the passage this morning," James said. "The week's provisional selection according to the study plan." He glanced briefly at the door. "It seemed relevant."

Obed, who had arrived quietly and taken a seat near the window, tilted his head slightly. "In the laboratory we call that pre-experiment anxiety. When you already suspect what the data will say and you're not sure whether to be relieved or alarmed."

***

The morning's Health and Safety session was led by Moses, continuing the module on documentation controls in safety-critical environments.

The lesson addressed the lifecycle of a safety document: creation, review, authorization, distribution, revision control, and the specific hazard created when any one of those stages was completed without a verifiable record.

Moses drew a simplified lifecycle diagram on the whiteboard.

"Every stage," he said, "depends on the one before it being completed and recorded. If authorization was performed but not documented, you have a distribution without proof of authority. If the source was authoritative but the record was lost in a transfer, you have authority without proof."

Efua Nara, one of the National Service Scheme personnel observing the session, raised her hand. "What happens when the record that proves authority no longer exists?"

Moses paused. It was a better question than most he received.

"That depends on what the record was for," he said. "If it was a simple procedural record — who reviewed a form and when — you may be able to reconstruct a reasonable account. But if it was the only document that linked the instruction to an authorizing body, then its absence is not a minor gap. It's a break in the chain that determines whether the instruction should be followed at all."

Efua wrote that down.

"In the present situation," Moses continued, "we don't yet know which kind of gap we have. The missing files may contain nothing more than a routine sign-off. Or they may contain the only verification that the instruction in the notice is correct. Until we know, we treat the gap as material."

From across the room, Robert Amegashie added quietly: "Which is exactly what you would hope an organization would do."

The comment was directed at no one in particular. Moses accepted it with a brief nod.

***

By midday, the investigation had a new shape.

James summarized the situation on the whiteboard.

The notices: four versions, physically distributed, with a work order confirming their installation.

The authorization: a reference to a regional authority, which referred to a previous administration.

The previous administration's files: transferred to a new location, arrival confirmed, current location unknown.

The immediate safety concern: managed, with notices suspended pending review.

The open question: whether the original instruction was accurate, and who, if anyone, could now vouch for it.

"We have one more step before we escalate this formally," James said. "We need to know whether the files are missing because they weren't transferred properly, or missing because they were transferred and then lost, or missing because they don't exist."

"Three very different situations," Gloria agreed.

"The field team has a contact at the regional records office," Moses said. "We can request a targeted search. Not a general inquiry — a specific request for Work Order 44-E's authorization source, by reference number, from a named administrator."

James considered this. "That's the right approach. Narrow, documented, with a specific person accountable for the response."

He turned to Robert. "Can you draft the request letter? Use the work order number and the name of the regional authority as cited in the building's register. Keep it factual. Don't suggest that the notice content is wrong — only that the authorization chain is incomplete and we need to verify the source document."

Robert agreed.

Obed looked up from his notebook. "And if the source document doesn't exist?"

James looked at the whiteboard — at the four folder tabs, blue and red, still arranged on the side table.

"Then we'll have a different problem," he said. "A better-defined one."

***

In the afternoon, Gloria set aside the documentary investigation briefly to work with the three National Service Scheme students on an educational exercise: tracing the lifecycle of a published instructional document from concept to distribution, identifying the points at which errors could be introduced and the mechanisms that existed to catch them.

She used an anonymized version of the Kuala Lumpur situation as her case study, without identifying the facility or the specific notices.

The exercise produced an unexpected result.

Sena Teki, the Liaison Intern, noted that the translation problem could occur even in a well-authorized process — if the translation stage was treated as a purely mechanical task rather than a subject-matter review. A translator could render each word correctly and still produce an instruction that directed evacuees to the wrong location, if the translator did not have access to the facility's physical layout.

"That's a design failure," Sena said, "not a language failure."

Gloria wrote that down.

It did not resolve the open question about the missing authorization record. But it suggested that even if the authorization chain were reconstructed, there was a second layer of review — the accuracy of the instruction itself — that would need independent verification.

Two separate problems, not one.

James, passing through the corridor outside the room and catching the tail of the conversation, paused at the door. He said nothing. He simply noted the observation on his folio with three words.

*Separate the problems.*

***

By late afternoon, the first response from the regional records office had arrived via the field team's transmission channel.

The records coordinator had confirmed receiving the targeted inquiry. They had located the transfer log reference. They had identified a box number.

The box was in a storage facility in the administrative district.

It had not been catalogued since the reorganization.

No one had opened it.

Moses received the message and read it twice. He brought it to James in the conference room.

"We know where it is," Moses said. "We don't know what's in it."

James read the message. The Millennium Probe display on the adjacent monitor showed its afternoon reading: **20 February 2174, 22:18 UTC.** The display had not changed since the morning calibration.

It offered no particular wisdom about an uncatalogued box in an administrative storage facility. It never did.

"Good," James said. "Now we have a location. Next step: someone with authority has to open the box."

He filed the message and began drafting the next communication.

The investigation had not concluded. But it had found its next step.

Outside, Accra moved through its Monday in the ordinary way — traffic, market calls, the particular quality of November light that made the city look like it was being considered carefully before it was painted.

In Kuala Lumpur, the field team awaited their next instruction.

The notices remained suspended, their authority still unverified.

The register still had no confirmed author.

And in an uncatalogued box in an administrative district, something waited that might answer the question or, just as plausibly, open three more.

During a safety documentation review, investigators discover that an official notice was distributed through verified channels, with a complete work order record — but the original authorizing document cannot be located. C. Request a specific, documented search for the authorization record from the named office that should hold it, while maintaining the current suspension of the notice.

D. Treat the missing record as confirmation that no authorization was ever granted, and escalate to the highest available authority.

# THE REGISTER WITH NO AUTHOR

*Monday, 9 November 2026 — New Legon, Accra*

By the time James Evans reached the conference room on Monday morning, the weekend had changed nothing.

The four notice versions were still there, preserved in labeled folders on the side table, exactly as Moses Larbi had arranged them on Friday afternoon. Three folders with blue tabs. One with a red tab. The blue folders directed evacuees toward the east stairwell. The red folder pointed to the central courtyard corridor.

The documents had not moved.

Neither had the problem inside them.

James placed his folio on the table, looked at the four folders for a moment, and then turned to the whiteboard still displaying Moses's two-column framework from Friday.

**WHAT WE KNOW.**
**WHAT WE ASSUME.**

Someone — James suspected it was Gloria — had added a third column overnight, written in a different shade of marker.

**WHAT WE NEED TO FIND.**

He was still reading it when Gloria Mica arrived, her purple teaching-plan folder under one arm and a printed transmission sheet in her hand.

"The field team sent this overnight," she said, setting the paper on the table. "They located the building's Safety Authorization Register — Sector Seven, Block C. The one that should contain the sign-off record for the notice distribution."

James picked it up. He read to the bottom. He read it again.

"There's nothing here," he said.

"There is something," Gloria replied. "There is a work order entry. Work Order 44-E. It records that notices were printed and posted. It records the dates, the languages, the floor locations." She paused. "It does not record who authorized the content."

Moses arrived carrying his compliance clipboard and a fresh printed copy of the building's administrative register. He had been on a call with the field team since eight o'clock.

"I spoke with the facility's safety coordinator," he said, setting down his clipboard. "They confirmed the notices are genuine and were distributed through official channels. They cannot confirm the original source document from which the translations were made."

James looked at him. "They issued notices without a verifiable source."

"They say the original instruction came from the regional authority. The regional authority's records office says the instruction came from a previous administration." Moses laid out the printed register. "The previous administration's records were transferred during a reorganization. The transfer log shows the files arrived at the new office. Nobody can locate them."

The room was quiet for a moment.

Gloria sat down slowly. "So the notices exist. The distribution records exist. The authorization record references a source. But the source references a set of files that were moved — and the moved files haven't been found."

"That is an accurate summary," Moses said.

"We have a complete chain of custody," James said, "except for the first link."

He walked to the whiteboard and added five words to Gloria's third column.

*The original instruction itself.*

***

In the R&D laboratory on the building's lower floor, Obed Ali was running the morning calibration sequence on the Millennium Probe's telemetry display when the new reading resolved.

The phosphorescent readout had shifted again.

```
PROBE_ISO:         2174-02-20T22:18:02Z
NOW_ISO:           2026-11-09T22:09:20Z
PHASE_INDEX:       5
PHASE_LABEL:       Oct 16 – Dec 20
PHASE_START_ISO:   2026-10-16T00:00:00Z
PHASE_END_ISO:     2026-12-20T23:59:59Z
OSCILLATION_INDEX: 0.04054640
AMPLITUDE_DAYS:    77,150.2723
PROBE_VALUE_DAYS:  53,794.0340
```

Last Friday, the harmonic calculation had placed the Probe's displacement coordinate in August 2215. This morning, it rested in **February 2174** — forty-one years earlier in the Probe's own calculated future.

Obed noted the shift without alarm. He added the new snapshot to the running log, labeled with the retrieval timestamp and the live `now_iso` value, and archived it alongside the Friday and Thursday records. The curve was oscillating as designed. What had changed was the position on that curve, not the integrity of the instrument.

He was writing up the calibration note when Robert Amegashie knocked on the laboratory door and stepped inside.

"James would like you at the conference room," Robert said. "Something about a register with a gap in it."

Obed set down his stylus. "Every register has a gap. The question is whether anyone was supposed to fill it."

He picked up his blue countdown timer, out of habit, and followed Robert upstairs.

***

Enka was participating in the morning session remotely.

His voice came through the conference room speaker in the measured, unhurried way that had become familiar across years of cross-continental calls. He had received the same overnight transmission as Gloria and had been reading it during his own early morning in Las Vegas, where the historical reference had placed an independent anchor: **21 July 2003, 17:41 UTC.**

That date had nothing to do with the current investigation. It was the fixed historical origin point from which the Probe's displacement mathematics were calculated — the anchor around which the harmonic amplitude worked its long swing. The Probe's telemetry was not a travel record. It did not confirm anyone's physical location. It confirmed only the current result of a mathematical relationship between a known past date and the present moment.

The field team in Kuala Lumpur had not moved. They were still at their observation position, awaiting instructions.

"Before we go further," Enka said through the speaker, "I want to be clear about one thing. The question of where the original notice came from is real and important. But it's a separate question from whether anyone is physically in danger right now. Has the safety coordinator confirmed that the notices are being suspended while the source is investigated?"

Moses checked his clipboard. "Confirmed. The building management was informed on Friday. The notices are marked under review. No new guidance will be issued until the source is verified."

"Good," Enka said. "Then the immediate safety concern is managed, even if the documentary question isn't. We're not operating against a clock."

James appreciated the clarification. In his experience, investigations rarely collapsed because of insufficient urgency. They usually collapsed because urgency was manufactured where patience was needed.

"The Eternal Dreamfall reading this week," he said, "is from the Wisdom literature. Job's friends were certain. Job was uncertain. The text is interested in which one was asking the right questions."

Gloria smiled without looking up from her notes. "You've been reading ahead."

"I read the passage this morning," James said. "The week's provisional selection according to the study plan." He glanced briefly at the door. "It seemed relevant."

Obed, who had arrived quietly and taken a seat near the window, tilted his head slightly. "In the laboratory we call that pre-experiment anxiety. When you already suspect what the data will say and you're not sure whether to be relieved or alarmed."

***

The morning's Health and Safety session was led by Moses, continuing the module on documentation controls in safety-critical environments.

The lesson addressed the lifecycle of a safety document: creation, review, authorization, distribution, revision control, and the specific hazard created when any one of those stages was completed without a verifiable record.

Moses drew a simplified lifecycle diagram on the whiteboard.

"Every stage," he said, "depends on the one before it being completed and recorded. If authorization was performed but not documented, you have a distribution without proof of authority. If the source was authoritative but the record was lost in a transfer, you have authority without proof."

Efua Nara, one of the National Service Scheme personnel observing the session, raised her hand. "What happens when the record that proves authority no longer exists?"

Moses paused. It was a better question than most he received.

"That depends on what the record was for," he said. "If it was a simple procedural record — who reviewed a form and when — you may be able to reconstruct a reasonable account. But if it was the only document that linked the instruction to an authorizing body, then its absence is not a minor gap. It's a break in the chain that determines whether the instruction should be followed at all."

Efua wrote that down.

"In the present situation," Moses continued, "we don't yet know which kind of gap we have. The missing files may contain nothing more than a routine sign-off. Or they may contain the only verification that the instruction in the notice is correct. Until we know, we treat the gap as material."

From across the room, Robert Amegashie added quietly: "Which is exactly what you would hope an organization would do."

The comment was directed at no one in particular. Moses accepted it with a brief nod.

***

By midday, the investigation had a new shape.

James summarized the situation on the whiteboard.

The notices: four versions, physically distributed, with a work order confirming their installation.

The authorization: a reference to a regional authority, which referred to a previous administration.

The previous administration's files: transferred to a new location, arrival confirmed, current location unknown.

The immediate safety concern: managed, with notices suspended pending review.

The open question: whether the original instruction was accurate, and who, if anyone, could now vouch for it.

"We have one more step before we escalate this formally," James said. "We need to know whether the files are missing because they weren't transferred properly, or missing because they were transferred and then lost, or missing because they don't exist."

"Three very different situations," Gloria agreed.

"The field team has a contact at the regional records office," Moses said. "We can request a targeted search. Not a general inquiry — a specific request for Work Order 44-E's authorization source, by reference number, from a named administrator."

James considered this. "That's the right approach. Narrow, documented, with a specific person accountable for the response."

He turned to Robert. "Can you draft the request letter? Use the work order number and the name of the regional authority as cited in the building's register. Keep it factual. Don't suggest that the notice content is wrong — only that the authorization chain is incomplete and we need to verify the source document."

Robert agreed.

Obed looked up from his notebook. "And if the source document doesn't exist?"

James looked at the whiteboard — at the four folder tabs, blue and red, still arranged on the side table.

"Then we'll have a different problem," he said. "A better-defined one."

***

In the afternoon, Gloria set aside the documentary investigation briefly to work with the three National Service Scheme students on an educational exercise: tracing the lifecycle of a published instructional document from concept to distribution, identifying the points at which errors could be introduced and the mechanisms that existed to catch them.

She used an anonymized version of the Kuala Lumpur situation as her case study, without identifying the facility or the specific notices.

The exercise produced an unexpected result.

Sena Teki, the Liaison Intern, noted that the translation problem could occur even in a well-authorized process — if the translation stage was treated as a purely mechanical task rather than a subject-matter review. A translator could render each word correctly and still produce an instruction that directed evacuees to the wrong location, if the translator did not have access to the facility's physical layout.

"That's a design failure," Sena said, "not a language failure."

Gloria wrote that down.

It did not resolve the open question about the missing authorization record. But it suggested that even if the authorization chain were reconstructed, there was a second layer of review — the accuracy of the instruction itself — that would need independent verification.

Two separate problems, not one.

James, passing through the corridor outside the room and catching the tail of the conversation, paused at the door. He said nothing. He simply noted the observation on his folio with three words.

*Separate the problems.*

***

By late afternoon, the first response from the regional records office had arrived via the field team's transmission channel.

The records coordinator had confirmed receiving the targeted inquiry. They had located the transfer log reference. They had identified a box number.

The box was in a storage facility in the administrative district.

It had not been catalogued since the reorganization.

No one had opened it.

Moses received the message and read it twice. He brought it to James in the conference room.

"We know where it is," Moses said. "We don't know what's in it."

James read the message. The Millennium Probe display on the adjacent monitor showed its afternoon reading: **20 February 2174, 22:18 UTC.** The display had not changed since the morning calibration.

It offered no particular wisdom about an uncatalogued box in an administrative storage facility. It never did.

"Good," James said. "Now we have a location. Next step: someone with authority has to open the box."

He filed the message and began drafting the next communication.

The investigation had not concluded. But it had found its next step.

Outside, Accra moved through its Monday in the ordinary way — traffic, market calls, the particular quality of November light that made the city look like it was being considered carefully before it was painted.

In Kuala Lumpur, the field team awaited their next instruction.

The notices remained suspended, their authority still unverified.

The register still had no confirmed author.

And in an uncatalogued box in an administrative district, something waited that might answer the question or, just as plausibly, open three more.
