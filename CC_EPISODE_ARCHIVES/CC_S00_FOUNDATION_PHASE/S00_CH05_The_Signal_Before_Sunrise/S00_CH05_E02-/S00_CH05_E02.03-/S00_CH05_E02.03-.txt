## The Missing Approval Line

The reply arrived without the document Enka had asked for.

At the laboratory console, a message from the building manager appeared beside the photographs of the four emergency notices. Attached was a distribution register: a short record showing that copies in four languages had been placed in the building.

Enka read it once, then again.

“Does this tell us who approved the instruction?” he asked.

The laboratory specialist looked over the page. “It tells us the copies were distributed. It doesn’t show the source they came from.”

“Or who authorised that source.”

The manager’s note did not claim otherwise. The register had been found in the building’s administrative files, but the original instruction and its approval record were still missing. The manager had asked the records team to continue searching.

Enka placed the register beside the photographs. It was useful—but only for one part of the story. Four copies had been distributed. That did not establish that the instruction behind them had been approved, or that each translation matched it.

He added another heading to his notebook:

**WHAT THIS RECORD SHOWS**

Then, beneath it:

**WHAT IT DOES NOT SHOW**

The distinction felt small, but it changed the investigation. Yesterday, the team had been waiting for the manager to verify the notice’s origin. Today, they had evidence of distribution, but no proof of authority. The new record narrowed the question without answering it.

On the distant team’s observation channel, the field analyst confirmed that no one had followed either conflicting direction. The team had kept to its observational assignment and had not tested an evacuation route or approached building occupants. Any assessment of the routes would have to wait for the responsible building staff and the current, authorised emergency arrangements.

“Good,” Enka said. “The notices are evidence. They are not permission to improvise.”

The specialist nodded. “I’ll label the register as a distribution record only.”

That afternoon, Enka returned to his notes from the Health & Safety material. One course section dealt with identifying hazards and assessing risk before choosing controls. A disagreement between instructions could itself be a warning sign—not a reason for an unbriefed person to decide which route was safe.

The lesson sat beside the four photographs. A warning sign could help people only when its message was reliable, accessible and consistent with the responsible organisation’s actual plan. Repeating an instruction in more languages could make it easier to understand; it could not make an unverified instruction authoritative.

The day’s organizational spotlight was the Technical Support & Operations Manager role. Enka thought about the quiet work behind a dependable record: keeping documents traceable, preserving their versions, and making sure a handover did not lose the detail that mattered. It was not dramatic work. But when something went wrong, the missing line on a form could matter as much as the words that remained.

Before closing the case file, he added the distribution register to the observed-evidence column. He did not write that the notice was genuine. He did not write that it was false. The authorisation question remained open, and the translations had not yet been compared against an approved original.

The next request to the building manager was precise: locate the source instruction and any record showing who approved it, when it took effect, and which versions were issued. Until those records could be checked, the team would not turn a count of notices—or a distribution log—into a safety decision.

Four languages had carried the message across the building.

The missing line was the one that would show who had the authority to send it.

The Line Without a Signature
Wednesday, 4 November 2026
By the time Enka reached the Koware headquarters in New Legon, a single announcement had already rearranged the morning.
Five transport unions had declared that no commercial vehicle would move that Wednesday. Messages from colleagues arrived in clusters. Should I come in? Is it really happening? Does anyone have a lift? The second question was the one nobody could answer. An announcement, Enka thought, is only a promise about the day. The day still has to agree with it.
Beside the front door, the technical support and operations manager was smoothing a sheet of paper onto the wall. Enka read it the way he read everything now: not for what it said, but for how it was made. A heading. A date. The hours the building would be staffed. A number to call for anyone stranded. And in the bottom corner, small but complete: Revision 2 — 06:40.
“Revision 2?”
“Revision 1 told everyone to report as normal,” the manager said. “Then the news changed, so I replaced it. And I took Revision 1 down. Nobody should follow the old sheet by mistake.”
It was an ordinary sentence. Enka kept it anyway.
At the laboratory desk, the field team’s overnight report was waiting.
In Kuala Lumpur, the building manager had returned from the records room with a folder, and the team had photographed a single page without touching anything else: a revision sheet for the building’s emergency instruction. Enka laid the image beside the four photographs of the noticeboard and read it line by line.
Revision 1 was complete. It had a date, a signature, an issuing office and an effective date. Its stairwell matched the three notices that agreed with one another.
Revision 2 was later. Its date matched the single date visible on the odd copy. Its approver line carried a job title, neatly typed.
The signature line beneath it was empty. So was the box marked effective from.
Enka sat back.
For two days the question had been three notices against one. Now it was a different question. The odd copy might be newer. It might even be right. But newer is not in force, and a revision that nobody has signed is still a proposal, however neatly it has been printed and translated.
He thought of the sheet by the door in New Legon. Revision 2 there had a time, an author and a number, and Revision 1 was gone from the wall. In Kuala Lumpur, Revision 2 had none of those things, and Revision 1 was still standing on the board.
Later, he returned to his Eternal Dreamfall study. The plan’s weekly reading was still the Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures. It named no particular passage for the day, so Enka claimed none. What held his attention was the translators themselves: people who carried words across a language, and whose work was trusted because someone stood behind it.
The day’s alternate verse, from Bible Gateway’s calendar, was 2 Corinthians 3:6, where Paul sets the written letter beside the Spirit who gives life. Enka was careful with it. Paul was speaking about a covenant, not a noticeboard, and a verse should not be bent to fit a problem. But he allowed himself one honest takeaway: the ink on a page is never the whole of its authority. The person behind it is.
The monitoring screen offered a second puzzle. The probe’s destination reading had been logged four times in three days, and each entry sat earlier than the one before it, the newest now showing mid-January 2231. The laboratory specialist could not say why. The log did not say whether the number described where the team was, only where the probe was pointing. Enka wrote it into the record exactly as it stood. A drifting number was not a team on the move, and he refused to let it become one.
He sent the field team three lines.
Preserve the photographs. Do not test either stairwell. Ask the building manager to place the revision sheet before the building’s own safety authority.
The reply came near evening. The manager had agreed. The safety officer would review both revisions and, if satisfied, walk the stairwells with the sheet in hand. That would be tomorrow, not tonight. The team’s orders were unchanged: watch, record, wait.
Back in New Legon, as the light thinned, Enka paused at the front door again. Revision 2 was still on the wall, its small corner line intact. Whatever the roads had done all day, this sheet had a name, a time and a number that someone could be asked about.
A signature, he decided, was not decoration. It was a person you could call.

## The Line Without a Signature
*Wednesday, 4 November 2026*

By the time Enka reached the Koware headquarters compound in New Legon, an early-morning radio bulletin had already unsettled the city's rhythm.

Five commercial transport unions had announced a nationwide commercial vehicle shutdown for Wednesday. The phone in Enka’s pocket had buzzed intermittently on the drive from Adenta: messages from staff and interns asking whether vehicles were running along the Madina corridor, whether the compound would remain open, and whether reports should be delivered remotely. The city had not completely stopped, but the streets were hesitant, caught between the gravity of the announcement and the stubborn necessity of the morning.

Near the administrative entrance, the compound’s Technical Support & Operations Manager was smoothing a fresh, crisp sheet of paper against the noticeboard beside the security desk. 

Enka slowed his pace. Over the past three days, notices had become an occupational obsession. He inspected the paper the way an analyst inspects telemetry—not merely for what it asserted, but for the discipline of its manufacture. 

Across the top ran a clear corporate header and the date. Below that, bulleted lines outlined compound operating hours, shuttle arrangements for stranded personnel, and emergency contact numbers. In the bottom right corner, framed in neat black type, sat two lines:

```
Revision: 02  ·  Issued: 06:40 UTC
Supercedes: Notice 2026-11-04-01 (Withdrawn)
Auth: Ops / Technical Support Desk
```

“Revision two?” Enka asked quietly.

The operations manager turned, holding the discarded sheet rolled in his hand. “Revision one went up at quarter to six, before the union spokesmen confirmed their position on the radio. It told staff to report as normal. Once the transport reality changed, keeping revision one on the board would have meant directing people into stranded junctions. So I pulled revision one down, logged the change, stamped revision two, and posted it. If you leave the old sheet up beside the new one, people choose whichever instruction suits their convenience.”

“And if someone asks who told them to stay home?”

The manager tapped the bottom corner of the new sheet. “My office code and timestamp are on the paper. A notice without an issuing line isn't an instruction, Mr. Yamoah. It's just paper on a wall.”

Enka nodded slowly. “Keep revision one in the physical archive. Don't discard it.”

He walked through to the research and development wing. Inside the quiet console room, the laboratory specialist had already established the data bridge to the field team in Kuala Lumpur.

On the local monitoring array, the three clocks ran their silent courses. Production time in New Legon stood at Wednesday, 4 November 2026. The historical baseline clock hummed at Sunday, 20 July 2003, reflecting Enka’s Las Vegas reference coordinates. And on the forward telemetry channel, the Millennium Probe registered its latest temporal arrival: Friday, 14 January 2231, 12:01:53 UTC.

Enka glanced at the probe readout, his brow furrowing. “The destination timestamp shifted again.”

“Yes,” the specialist replied, bringing up the drift log. “Two days ago the probe indicated late January 2236. Yesterday morning's reading was September 2233. Today's live packet returns mid-January 2231. The oscillation index has climbed to 0.03768, and the calculated probe days have dropped accordingly.”

“Has the field team changed physical location?”

“Negative,” the specialist confirmed. “They remain inside the designated residential high-rise in Kuala Lumpur, maintaining static observation from the fifth-floor corridor. The telemetry drift appears to be an instrument calibration differential, not physical translation of the team. We have logged it as an open variance.”

“Good. A drifting number is a measurement puzzle, not an operational redeployment,” Enka said. “Keep the team fixed. What did the building manager find?”

The specialist switched the main display to the encrypted field feed. 

In Kuala Lumpur, the field team had made contact with the residential building manager early that afternoon. The manager had not found the primary policy binder, but in an archive cabinet in the basement facility office, he had retrieved a laminated maintenance log: the building’s emergency signage revision register.

The field analyst had photographed the register page under clean LED illumination without removing it from the manager’s counter.

Enka leaned in close to the glass.

The register recorded two distinct entries under the heading *Emergency Stairwell Egress & Wayfinding (Levels 04–12)*.

**Revision 01** was dated three years prior. Its entry was immaculate. It specified that in the event of primary lift failure, occupants between floors four and twelve were directed to Stairwell A, situated to the left of the central lift lobby. The issuing authority was listed as the City Fire Safety Directorate, countersigned by the building's licensed Fire Warden, with a completed box marked *Effective Date: 12 March 2228*.

**Revision 02** occupied the line directly beneath it. The date matched the small, printed date observed on the odd fourth notice currently hanging on the fifth-floor wall—the Tamil version that directed occupants to the right, toward Stairwell B. 

The typed approver field on Revision 02 carried an impressive title: *Assistant Director of Building Compliance & Structural Works*. The description noted that scheduled maintenance on Stairwell A required temporary diversion of egress to Stairwell B.

But the signature box beside the title was blank. 

And the box marked *Effective Date / Certification of Clearance* contained only an empty grey dash.

Enka stared at the high-resolution photograph. 

For forty-eight hours, the puzzle had presented itself as a statistical riddle: three notices pointing left, one notice pointing right. Three languages in agreement, one language standing alone. In any superficial assessment, three votes would overwhelm one.

Now the underlying truth lay bare. 

The Tamil notice on the wall was not an accidental mistranslation. It was the physical manifestation of Revision 02. Someone had drafted the temporary diversion, printed the translated sheets, and hung the Tamil copy on the fifth-floor wall. But Revision 02 had never been signed. The structural inspection had never been certified. The effective date had never arrived. 

The older instruction was valid and signed, but potentially compromised by uninspected maintenance. The newer instruction addressed the maintenance, but lacked the legal authority and safety certification to take effect. 

“It's a proposed change,” Enka murmured. “Printed before it was authorized.”

The laboratory specialist shook his head. “And whoever posted it put up the draft without taking down the active copies, or took down three copies of the draft and left one behind.”

“Which leaves the building occupants in greater danger than having no notice at all,” Enka observed. “If an alarm sounds, half the floor runs to a stairwell that may be obstructed by scaffolding, and the other half runs toward an exit that has never been certified as clear.”

He thought back to his morning study from the Eternal Dreamfall programme. The reading plan had remained anchored in Week 96, focused on the Septuagint—the monumental effort to translate the sacred Hebrew texts into Greek for the scattered communities of the ancient Mediterranean. The Septuagint’s translators had carried profound responsibility; every term chosen had to reflect the sovereign intent of the original author. A translation detached from its author’s authority became a source of doctrinal drift.

The companion verse from Paul’s second letter to Corinth, chapter three, verse six, came to mind: *He has made us competent as ministers of a new covenant—not of the letter but of the Spirit; for the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life.* 

Paul had not been writing about municipal fire regulations, but the principle cut through the morning's dilemma with razor precision. The physical letters on a noticeboard—ink, paper, neat typography in four ancient tongues—possessed no intrinsic power to save anyone from a corridor fire. The letters were merely vessels. The true protection lay in the authority and living covenant behind them: the certified inspection, the trained fire warden, the verified clearance of the exit route. Without the living signature of authority, the letter on the wall was an empty shell—and in an emergency, an empty shell could be lethal.

Enka keyed the transmitter to Kuala Lumpur.

“Field lead,” Enka instructed, his voice measured and firm. “Under no circumstances is any member of the team to test Stairwell A or Stairwell B. Do not touch the noticeboard. Do not attempt to take down the Tamil notice. We are observers, not building custodians.”

“Acknowledged, New Legon,” the field lead replied from across the temporal distance. “What is the directive for the building manager?”

“Have the building manager escalate the photographed revision register immediately to the city's responsible Fire Safety Officer and the licensed building surveyor. Request a formal physical inspection of both stairwells and an official determination of which revision is certified in force. Until that written clearance is delivered by the lawful safety authority, the team maintains perimeter observation only.”

“Understood. We wait for the authority.”

Enka closed the audio channel. Across the courtyard in New Legon, the mid-morning heat was beginning to rise. A few commercial mini-buses crawled past the outer gates, their drivers having decided to test the strike. 

On the compound wall, Revision 02 of the Koware operational advisory remained firmly posted, its author, timestamp, and withdrawn predecessor clearly identified. 

Enka looked from the Koware board to the digital console showing the unsigned register in Kuala Lumpur. 

A title typed on a form could look formidable. A translation could make it sound universal. But authority was not a typeface, and safety was not an assumption. 

Until someone took pen to paper and assumed personal responsibility for the path, the route remained unchosen.

# THE FIFTH VOICE

### Wednesday, 4 November 2026

*Sometimes the most important message is the question nobody thought to translate.*

---

## THE QUESTION IN THE MARGIN

Enka had examined the four emergency notices so many times that he believed he knew every mark on them.

He was wrong.

The field observer's latest message arrived while the laboratory specialist was reviewing the previous day's photographs.

“There is something beneath the fourth notice,” the observer reported.

Enka leaned towards the screen.

“What sort of something?”

“Writing. Pencil, I think. It was almost invisible in the earlier image.”

The specialist enlarged the relevant area.

Below the printed instructions, in a space that appeared to have been left blank, someone had written a question:

**WHAT IF I CANNOT USE THE STAIRS?**

For several seconds, neither man spoke.

Four languages had carried the official-looking instructions across the wall.

Yet the most important words on the page might have been written by someone with a pencil.

“Do we know who wrote it?” Enka asked.

“No.”

“When?”

“We cannot establish that either.”

“Then we mustn't invent a story about the writer.”

Enka looked again at the question.

The handwriting was uneven. The final question mark was darker than the rest.

It was not evidence that an emergency had occurred. It was not proof that anyone had been harmed.

But it raised a concern the team had not yet considered carefully enough.

The investigation had focused on whether the four notices were genuine.

Now another question had appeared.

Even if the notices were genuine, would their instructions serve everyone who might need them?

---

## TWO BUILDINGS, ONE PROBLEM

At Koware's control desk, the day's ordinary responsibilities continued alongside the unusual investigation.

A report circulating that morning described plans by five Ghanaian transport unions to withdraw commercial vehicle services on Wednesday.

The announcement had prompted discussion about how people would reach their destinations if the planned disruption went ahead.

A member of the fictional operations team was examining a sample notification intended for people travelling to an appointment.

The message looked perfectly clear on the computer.

On a smaller phone, however, the important contact details were hidden below a large image.

“You have to scroll before you see whom to call,” the colleague said.

The Technical Support & Operations Manager studied the screen.

“Then the message may be complete without being useful.”

Enka smiled faintly.

“That sounds familiar.”

The manager turned the phone sideways.

“Information has to reach the person who needs it. And the person has to be able to use it.”

Enka thought of the four notices.

Four languages improved the possibility of understanding.

But translation alone did not establish whether the instruction was authorised, current or suitable for the different people expected to follow it.

A document could be technically complete and practically inadequate.

The difference was becoming impossible to ignore.

---

## THE FOUNDATION BENEATH THE WORDS

Later, Enka opened his Eternal Dreamfall study notes.

His recent reading had returned repeatedly to the way ancient writings were preserved, translated and interpreted. The Septuagint, a Greek translation tradition associated with Jewish scriptures, reminded him that transmitting words and preserving their meaning were related but distinct responsibilities.

Beside the notes, he had written the day's biblical reference:

**Matthew 7:24.**

The verse spoke of the wise builder who heard Jesus' teaching and put it into practice.

Enka considered the distinction.

Hearing was not the same as doing.

Receiving an instruction was not the same as being able to follow it.

And possessing a document was not the same as knowing whether it could be trusted.

The laboratory specialist interrupted his thoughts.

“I've been reviewing the hazard-identification material,” she said. “We're concentrating on what the notice says. Perhaps we should also record the questions it leaves unanswered.”

She brought up the Health & Safety lesson dealing with hazards, risk assessment and the selection of suitable controls.

Enka listened.

The team had no authority to decide which staircase should be used in the distant building.

They had no verified emergency plan.

They had not established who issued the notices or whether the translations reflected an approved original.

And the handwritten question had introduced another unresolved consideration.

They could document the concern.

They could refer it to those responsible.

They could not manufacture a safe answer from incomplete evidence.

“Put it in the report,” Enka said.

“As an accessibility problem?”

“As a question requiring assessment. We haven't established the conditions well enough to call it anything more specific.”

The specialist nodded.

Enka added another sentence.

“And do not lose the original photograph.”

---

## THE MESSAGE THAT NEEDED A REPLY

The field observation channel remained open.

The distant team continued its existing assignment without testing either staircase, approaching occupants or changing the building's instructions.

The laboratory prepared a concise request for the building manager through the contact route already used during the investigation.

The request asked for two separate matters to be reviewed.

First, the manager was asked to continue tracing the original emergency instruction, its authorisation and the relationship between the translated versions.

Second, the handwritten question was to be referred to the building's responsible safety personnel, who could assess it against the building's current, authorised emergency arrangements.

Enka read the message before it was sent.

“Are we telling them which route to use?” he asked.

“No.”

“Are we claiming that the written question proves the notice is unsafe?”

“No.”

“Good. Send it.”

A reply arrived later.

The manager acknowledged the concern and said it would be passed to the responsible building staff while the search for the source instruction continued.

It was not the dramatic revelation Enka had expected when the investigation began.

Nobody had uncovered a hidden laboratory.

No mysterious machine had activated.

No temporal anomaly had explained the contradictory notices.

But someone had asked a practical question.

And, for the first time, the team had made certain that the question itself would not disappear beneath the investigation.

---

## THE VOICE THAT WAS NOT TRANSLATED

As the day drew towards its close, Enka returned to the laboratory display.

The Millennium Probe's changing readings remained under technical observation.

He knew better than to confuse a changing reference with proof that a deployed team had travelled somewhere new.

For the moment, the investigation depended on something much less extraordinary.

A photograph.

A handwritten question.

An unfinished records search.

And people willing to admit what they did not yet know.

The specialist stood beside him.

“Do you think the person who wrote that question received an answer?”

“I don't know.”

“Do you think the notice was wrong?”

“I don't know that either.”

She looked at him.

“You're getting comfortable saying that.”

Enka laughed.

“Not comfortable. Just less afraid of it.”

He studied the words beneath the fourth notice one last time.

The building had four printed languages.

But there had always been another voice in the story.

It belonged to whoever needed the instructions to work.

Enka closed the investigation file for the evening.

The original approval remained missing.

The translations still required verification.

The building's responsible staff had yet to assess the handwritten concern.

Nothing had been declared safe merely because it had been printed.

And nothing had been declared false merely because it was questioned.

The mystery remained open.

But the investigation had changed.

They were no longer asking only whether the message was correct.

They were beginning to ask whether the message could truly help the people for whom it had been written.
