## PROLOGUE — THE FOURTH DIRECTION

Kuala Lumpur had not yet surrendered the night's rain. Beneath the cold light of a lift lobby, four laminated notices caught four different reflections. They were written in Malay, English, Mandarin and Tamil. Their headings made the same promise: instructions for moving safely in an emergency.

But the arrows disagreed.

Three indicated the stairwell to the left of the lift. The fourth indicated a corridor on the other side. It hung a little lower than the others, and its bottom edge carried a date so fine that it vanished in the first photograph.

The Classies field analyst framed the wall in a camera without stepping towards either route. Beside the analyst, the field lead repeated the scope of their assignment: observe, record, preserve. They were not the building's emergency officers. They could neither conduct an evacuation test nor tell its occupants which arrow to trust.

The camera shutter clicked four times.

At Koware's imagined headquarters in New Legon, Accra, Enka watched the images arrive on a laboratory screen. On a second display, the Millennium Probe showed a future-oriented reading. On a third, an historical reference returned a time in the past. Three clocks, three kinds of information; none could certify the corridor in the field photograph.

Then the field analyst enlarged the fourth notice.

Under its arrow was a line neither the first photograph nor the other three notices had clearly captured.

Enka leaned closer.

"Get the whole page," he said. "And find the person who was allowed to put it there."

## WEEK ONE — WHAT THE PAPER SAYS

### MONDAY, 2 NOVEMBER — 01: THE NOTICE IN FOUR LANGUAGES

The morning at New Legon began with the soft clack of Enka's study notebook closing. The Eternal Dreamfall reading plan had reached its continuing discussion of the Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation tradition. In one notebook column he had written *words*. In the next, *what people think those words permit*. He had not assigned himself a verse the available plan did not actually specify.

A lab call pulled him from the desk. The Director of Research & Development had convened the R&D Lab Specialist and the Liaison Technician around the incoming photographs. In Kuala Lumpur, the field team had recorded each sign's position, mounting height, layout, visible language and directional mark. The fourth had a printed date. The others did not.

"It might be newer," the specialist said.

"Or an older form with a date," the liaison replied.

The director tapped the image. "And three copies might all descend from the same mistake. We have a disagreement, not a vote."

To illustrate, the specialist laid four magnetic arrows on the table. One clung unexpectedly to the metal sleeve of a document tray; the other three followed it in a rattling little parade, attaching themselves to Enka's cuff. He stood with a line of obedient arrows pointing up his arm. Nobody could resist a brief laugh. He removed them and put them down in four separate evidence envelopes.

On the field feed a cleaner's trolley passed in the far background. The analyst did not approach the cleaner or interrupt the building's routine. Instead, the team requested permission to preserve full-frame images and ask the responsible manager for the original emergency instruction and its revision history.

Enka divided the case board into three columns: **Observed**, **Reported**, **Authorized**. Under *Observed*, he placed the four signs and their differing arrows. Under *Reported*, the building location and the manager's contact details were recorded as unconfirmed leads where appropriate. Under *Authorized*, there was still no entry for either direction.

"Don't let the precision of a photograph smuggle certainty into the conclusion," he said.

That evening the field analyst sent an uncropped image. It showed the faint lower line, but the resolution was not sufficient to authenticate what it meant. The mystery had become sharper, not smaller.

### TUESDAY, 3 NOVEMBER — 02: THE CROPPED TRUTH

A photograph can tell the truth while hiding the fact that matters most.

The Human & Employment Resources Manager discovered that principle when the latest image arrived on a shared screen with the lower edge missing again. The Health & Safety Technician, reviewing the case as an exercise in responsible workplace communication, asked for the original file rather than the neat crop prepared for a meeting slide. No one claimed this gave Koware jurisdiction over the building in Malaysia.

The manager's office in Kuala Lumpur answered their inquiry cautiously. Notices were often created by one desk, translated by another and mounted by a third. The building manager could describe an ordinary process; the manager could not yet produce the particular approved instruction that the displayed notices supposedly reproduced.

At New Legon, the Cleaning Technician and the Janitor were dealing with a less international problem: a handwritten sign had been taped too high on the storage-room door. A rolling cleaning cart caught its loose corner and pulled the sheet down in a sudden flutter, leaving the Janitor with the sign stuck across the front of his overalls. It read **KEEP THIS AREA CLEAR**. He looked down at the pile of cleaning cloths in his hands and said, "That includes me, apparently." The staff laughed once, then shifted the cart away from the doorway.

The Health & Safety Technician used the moment for a modest point: a warning is useful only when it is legible, correctly placed and consistent with actual conditions. But the Kuala Lumpur issue was more serious than a misplaced piece of paper. Wrong guidance in a genuine emergency could injure people.

The field team continued to observe from its permitted area. They requested the uncropped original, its creation date and a copy of the facility's current approved emergency arrangements from competent local personnel. They neither selected a stairwell nor staged a test.

Before closing the day, Enka compared two versions of the photograph side by side. The cropped version looked conclusive because it removed the line beneath the fourth arrow. The full version looked troubling because it restored it.

A picture could be complete enough to circulate and incomplete enough to mislead.

### WEDNESDAY, 4 NOVEMBER — 03: THE UNSIGNED FOURTH NOTICE

A file arrived from Malaysia with an official-looking cover and no answer to the actual question.

It was a distribution register. It listed the preparation and placement of the four notices. It gave their circulation a trace. It did not establish who approved the underlying emergency instruction, whether the four language versions faithfully conveyed it or whether any of them was still applicable to the building.

Isaac Barry, in the fictional Technical Support & Operations Manager role, offered to preserve the register alongside the original photographs. His team set up a read-only comparison so that later edits to a presentation could not silently replace the evidence. Bonsu "Bolt," the Concierge Liaison, asked the simplest question in the room: "If a visitor sees four arrows, who is obliged to give them trustworthy guidance?"

"Qualified local building staff," Isaac said. "Not us from another country."

An office printer began producing the registers in reverse page order. Its last sheet slid off the tray and landed face down over Bonsu's visitor badge. For one ridiculous second the laminated nameplate appeared to announce that Bonsu was **APPENDIX FOUR**. He held up the badge, inspected his accidental new title and quietly returned it to its proper place.

The laughter faded when the distant analyst reported that a neighboring notice board had recently been rearranged. Was the fourth notice moved at the same time? Nobody knew. The crew photographed only what they could see and sought the appropriate records rather than inventing a witness or interrogating occupants.

Enka wrote two headings in thick ink: **WHAT THE REGISTER SHOWS** and **WHAT IT DOES NOT SHOW**. A count of copies belonged under the first. Approval, safety and accurate translation belonged under the second.

The case had progressed from an apparent contradiction to a traceable failure of provenance. There was now one more piece of evidence and no more permission to act. Enka asked for the source instruction, issuing office, approving officer, effective date, superseded versions and any record of changes to the building layout.

"An unsigned fourth notice might be wrong," he said. "It might also be the only one showing a later change. We cannot decide by looking at the wall."

### THURSDAY, 5 NOVEMBER — 04: THE INSTRUCTION NO ONE COULD SIGN

At the Koware laboratory, the Millennium Probe moved again.

An earlier saved reading pointed to one future date; the day's captured run pointed to another. The Academic Registrar wanted a single date for the training slide. James Evans, the fictional Director of Academic Programs, told him not to choose one merely because it was easier to print. A future-oriented calculation was a model output, not an appointment or a verified destination for the field team.

They laid out the saved inputs: historical-reference timestamp, production timestamp, phase information, amplitude and Probe value. Each reading had its own acquisition context. The difference could be investigated without pretending that a person had travelled, that a mission had changed destination or that a future headline had been received.

Matthew Ebenezer, working in academic partnerships and outreach, arrived with a binder of discussion materials. A loose label peeled off the cover, drifted through the air-conditioning and adhered neatly to James's sleeve. It declared him **FINAL REVISION**. He looked at the label, raised an eyebrow and said, "Then please don't revise me without asking." The registrar rescued the label before the meeting resumed.

Across the ocean, another attempt to identify the notice's issuing office met a dead end. Several administrative desks could acknowledge distribution, but no one could yet demonstrate authority to sign for the instruction itself. A manager's title, a distribution stamp and a clean letterhead were not substitutes for the approval trail.

A new field photograph made the discrepancy more alarming: the faint line below the fourth arrow might refer to a revision or condition, but its wording could not be validated from the image. Enka barred any attempt to reconstruct the missing words by guessing from context.

The academic team turned the problem into a source-literacy exercise. How does one teach students to distinguish data from an inference? What changes when a document is translated? Which responsibilities remain with the people who maintain a real facility?

"A machine can return a precise date," James told the room, "and a person can stamp a precise time. Neither proves the claim you wish the record would make."

By nightfall, the fourth notice was still unresolved. The paper had an arrow, but no verified chain of authority behind it.

### FRIDAY, 6 NOVEMBER — 05: THE COPIES THAT AGREED

On Friday, the laboratory table filled with printouts that looked reassuringly similar.

Gloria Mica, the fictional Faculty Coordinator, laid the four versions in parallel columns. Moses Larbi, in the Quality Assurance & Compliance Manager role, asked the question that made the arrangement uncomfortable: had they been translated independently from an authorized master, or copied from a common, unverified draft? James Evans answered that the teaching exercise could model the uncertainty but could not convert it into a finding about the Malaysian building.

The pages were clipped into a single row. A ceiling fan lifted the unfastened top corners in sequence until the sheets bowed forward like four solemn delegates at an imaginary ceremony. Moses caught them before they scattered, only to find that the blank backing sheet had bowed along with them. "Unanimous," Gloria said, "including the page with nothing on it." Even the usually severe reviewer smiled.

A simulated classroom review revealed what the field investigation had not: four agreeing copies could still share one mistaken source. The fourth, different copy could be an error, a later amendment or an entirely unrelated instruction. Without authenticated revision history and responsible local verification, the team must not treat any scenario as established.

The Legal Journalist placed a cautious card on Koware's reception pinboard: **MULTILINGUAL SAFETY NOTICES — SOURCE AND APPROVAL UNCONFIRMED**. It was clearly marked as a fictional internal case-study topic, not a public building warning. Beside it were public-news cards awaiting source checks. Staff stopped to look for different reasons: the janitor for a commuting story, the research staff for a technology report, and the Corporate Manager for a potential business implication.

In the final working session, Enka received a preliminary lead to an older distribution work order. It might connect the printed notices to an archived transfer. It did not say who approved their content.

He authorized no route test, no public conclusion and no new mission capability. Instead he asked the records liaison to pursue the document trail through the responsible offices.

A case once defined by four arrows was now defined by a missing source.

## WEEK TWO — WHO MAY SPEAK FOR THE BUILDING?

### MONDAY, 9 NOVEMBER — 06: THE LINE BENEATH THE ARROW

The weekend had not solved the case. But the paper trail had acquired a number: **44-E**.

Johnson Ortsin, the fictional Programme Manager, spread the work order beside an updated site drawing. Derek Shiraska, the Production Coordinator, marked every proposed activity that would risk becoming a real route test. Mathias Agbenorku, the Director of Operations, brought the practical question: if a drawing was old, could an authentic instruction still be unsafe today?

Johnson had originally sketched a neat five-step plan ending in a demonstration. Derek drew a hard line through that last stage. "We can rehearse the communication process on paper," he said. "We cannot rehearse an unfamiliar building's evacuation routes from this desk."

Johnson conceded the point. He was annoyed at the interruption, and Derek was annoyed that it had been necessary. The irritation remained visible as they revised the plan together.

Mathias reached for a site roll just as Johnson released the elastic band holding it shut. The roll sprang open across the table, flicked a harmless teaspoon into Mathias's empty cup and knocked the cup sideways with a terrific clatter. All three men froze. Enka lifted the cup, inspected its unspilled emptiness and said, "Fortunately, that plan contains no tea." They restored order with some difficulty.

The work order, in the proposed field account, established a path by which printed notices could have been installed. The building manager's current account did not establish that the manager had issued the underlying instruction. A records office had located a sealed storage box linked to an older transfer. The contents were unknown; the custodian had not released it.

Outside the fiction of the Kuala Lumpur inquiry, a real electricity-maintenance schedule for parts of Accra offered a second test in careful reading. Notices named different communities on different days, including parts of New Legon later in the week. Mathias could plan for resilience, but he could not declare a definite outage at Koware's specific premises without verification.

The field analyst sent a final image of the text below the fourth arrow. The line was still not legible enough to validate, and the building's current safety plan had not been authenticated. The team stood down from any unapproved inspection.

Near the end of the day, a clerk's message arrived: the box existed at the place indicated by the transfer record.

"We found it," Johnson said.

"We found *where* it is," Derek corrected.

For the first time all day, Johnson accepted a correction without arguing.

### TUESDAY, 10 NOVEMBER — 07: THE NAME THE PAPER COULD NOT GIVE

At the New Legon office, Mubarak Mohsen, the fictional Facility Manager, examined a release request with three precise elements: transfer reference, date and stamp. The fourth was blank. **Authorized requester:** no name.

Elijah Peter's, the Facility Butler, arranged the outer envelopes without reading the correspondence inside. Lovemore Saiga came from the garden carrying a plant-label tray, its writing blurred from watering. The date remained visible on a plastic strip; the plant's name had disappeared.

"Beautiful date," Lovemore observed. "No idea what it belongs to."

Elijah reached to move the tray. The slippery labels suddenly fanned out over his sleeve and one settled squarely on his shoulder like a tiny officer's epaulette. It bore the words **IDENTITY REQUIRED**. Mubarak laughed despite himself, and Elijah stood rigidly at mock attention until Lovemore retrieved it.

Enka called up a draft transmission for Kuala Lumpur. Its first sentence read: *We have found the authorizing instruction. Proceed to check the route.*

"No," Mubarak said before Enka could finish. "We have located a sealed box that may contain a relevant file. We cannot even open it without the proper request."

Enka deleted the sentence. The field team's address remained a supplied geographic position, not an authenticated roster or proof that every deployed member was present and unharmed. A message sent to a plausible endpoint would not necessarily reach the right person.

The proposed replacement was narrow: preserve the photographs; ask the proper custodian, through an authenticated channel, who may request inspection of the archive; and seek current guidance from the qualified local personnel who actually controlled the building. It did not endorse a disputed route.

Mubarak marked the release form **FOR AUTHORITY REVIEW**. His own signature could confirm that he had prepared an internal request; it could not make him the officer entitled to release Malaysian archive contents.

Then a second empty field came into view: **Does the archived instruction still match the building as it stands today?**

Even the correct signature would not answer that question.

### WEDNESDAY, 11 NOVEMBER — 08, PART ONE: THE BOARD WITH TWO TRUTHS

By Wednesday, the reception pinboard had become a map of decisions that people wanted to make before the evidence was ready.

The Legal Journalist drew a black line through the word **CONFIRMED** on a proposed internal case-study card. Beneath it, the journalist wrote **REPORTED**. Josh Lafa, the fictional Public Relations & Press Coordinator, had already noticed the same problem in a draft headline: it claimed the Malaysian notice was approved because the work order showed circulation. Terry Joan, in the Marketing Manager role, agreed to withdraw the claim rather than publish a sharper, false version.

"We can describe the mystery," Josh said. "We cannot issue the building's emergency instruction."

The public-news section of the board carried carefully sourced reporting about an upcoming Ghanaian budget presentation, local electrical maintenance plans and an anticipated small-business exhibition. Each card distinguished the publication date from the date of the underlying event. Staff were encouraged to bring their own questions, not to mistake a headline for a contract or a rumour for a decision.

A receptionist asked whether a prominent card about new investment meant Koware had received funding. Terry shook their head. "An announcement that may matter to us is not an award to us."

Enka walked the board's length. He saw his own case reflected in every card. A paper could travel from writer to printer to public display without carrying the authority that its confident typography seemed to promise.

In Kuala Lumpur, the four disputed signs were still recorded as field evidence. The team had not gained the right to evaluate the routes. The sealed archive remained sealed. The day ended with a cleanly drafted note that the office could review, and with no false claim dispatched to anyone.

### THURSDAY, 12 NOVEMBER — 08, PART TWO: THE DISTANCE BETWEEN RECEIVED AND AUTHORIZED

Thursday brought a late public-policy story. Ghana's 2027 Budget was presented to Parliament that evening, with the proposed New Economy investment programme attracting attention. Enka asked the Legal Journalist to retain the actual source and presentation time on the pinboard rather than imply that the announced intentions had already become Koware projects.

In another corner of the office, the International Relations Coordinator, Dr. Pascal Quagrainie, consulted a map with the Global Partnerships Registrar and Global Partnerships Liaison. Malaysia appeared on the map because of the still-unresolved fictional building inquiry. The country, however, was not one storyline: international technology questions, business partnerships and a facility's emergency signage required different sources, different authorities and different kinds of evidence.

An electricity-maintenance notice listed parts of New Legon among areas scheduled for work that day. Mathias asked his team to verify whether the specific headquarters supply was affected and to test the *office's own* authorized backup-readiness procedures. No one declared an actual outage at the headquarters merely because the place name appeared on a list.

Meanwhile, the records liaison reported that the sealed box might contain an older administrative file, but that the custodian still required an authorized release request. The transfer slip had a number; it did not have a valid authority decision.

The thought of the fourth arrow persisted at the edge of every meeting. If the original emerged tomorrow, it might be valid history and invalid present guidance. The responsible local safety authority, not a visiting observer, would still have to establish the building's current arrangements.

The distance between Accra and Kuala Lumpur had begun to feel less like a flight route than a chain of questions. Who sent the information? Who had the right to send it? Which version applied? And who could act on it without putting someone else at risk?

### FRIDAY, 13 NOVEMBER — 08, PART THREE: THE MESSAGE THAT STAYED UNSENT

The news arrived in waves before the morning meeting.

The budget had been presented. The Ecobank JoyBusiness SME Fair was opening at GHUD Park, Accra Mall, and was scheduled to continue through the weekend. Reporting from Malaysia described an investigation into possible AI-chip re-exports; it did **not** establish that an offence had occurred. Another Ghanaian business headline concerned the stock exchange's exploration of blockchain and other digital-market infrastructure.

The Legal Journalist pinned each report with its source, date and a carefully limited headline. Josh and Terry prepared an opportunity note about the budget without claiming that Koware would receive money. Pascal's desk kept Malaysia's chip investigation separate from the entirely fictional case of the four emergency notices.

Thomisia Daniel, the Events & Tours Coordinator, suggested a possible observational visit to the SME Fair. Ingrid Razak, the Community Outreach Officer, wanted to hear what exhibitors needed. Ceda Bertto, the Booking Agent, separated expressions of interest from confirmed arrangements. There was no claim of a booked delegation or approved spending.

Ceda tried to push an overstuffed folder under the meeting-room door while carrying loose attendance sheets. The folder jammed; the sheets sprang up; one landed over Ceda's head like a paper helmet. In large letters it announced **CONFIRMED ATTENDANCE** above someone who could no longer see the room. Thomisia lifted it off. "A very clear label," she said, "on a thoroughly unconfirmed situation." It was the only slapstick interruption in the three-day closing installment.

The laughter barely subsided before the Kuala Lumpur case returned to the main screen.

Enka placed four photographs in a row. The first three arrows agreed. The fourth did not. The date beneath the fourth arrow remained unverified. Work Order 44-E demonstrated a possible installation history, not content approval. The storage box had been traced but not lawfully opened. The manager's account, the distribution register and the transfer slip were different fragments of a history that no one had fully reconstructed.

Edliex—pronounced *Alex*—had listened silently through the first part of Koware's meeting. Enka invited its feedback. Its status light changed.

"The same distinction appears in the budget report, the proposed fair visit, the chip investigation and the notice inquiry," Edliex said. "Receipt is not endorsement. A record of distribution is not proof of approval. Interest is not a booking. An allegation is not a finding."

"Then give us a path through the paperwork," Enka said.

The light shifted to conversational mode.

Edliex proposed a six-part trace, not a command: establish the original source; establish the approving authority; verify the applicable revision and date; compare the instruction with the building's present arrangements; verify the meaning of the four translations; and establish what message had actually reached its intended audience.

"Would that let *us* announce a safe stairwell?" Derek asked.

"No," Edliex replied. "The responsible qualified local authority must make that determination. Our trace would only make the limits of our evidence visible."

A draft instruction came up on the screen, short and dangerously confident:

*Use the direction shown on the three matching notices pending further advice.*

Enka read the sentence aloud. A wrong decision made in a second could be repeated by people who would never see its missing evidence. The silence that followed belonged to the people who would have to live with that consequence.

"Delete it," he said.

The replacement message asked the appropriate custodian for permission to inspect the archive through an authenticated channel, requested confirmation of the issuing authority and revision, and sought current interim guidance from competent local building staff. It asked the Classies observers to preserve evidence within their existing authorization. It supplied no route instruction.

A staff member reached towards **SEND**. Mubarak pointed to the unresolved sender-authorization field. Isaac pointed to the field-channel authentication status. Both were incomplete.

No one pressed the button.

Enka placed the proposed message in the review queue. The paper trail had advanced. The case had not been solved. The box remained sealed, and the fourth arrow remained unexplained. The field team's last independently authenticated condition and the current emergency plan still needed proper verification.

On the historical display, the past remained the past. On the Millennium Probe, a future reading continued to change with its inputs. Neither gave permission to erase the present uncertainty.

Outside, Accra moved into Friday afternoon. Traffic pressed around the roads. Vendors called to customers. Somewhere else, in a building the team could not command, four arrows continued to disagree on a wall.

Enka looked at the unsent sentence one final time.

The hardest action of the week had not been choosing a direction. It had been refusing to invent one.

## PREVIEW — EPISODE 03: THE CUSTODIAN'S KEY

The first working morning after the weekend may bring a lawful custodian, a request with an actual authorizing name and a fresh examination of the building's current plan. Or the inquiry may meet another locked door. At New Legon, the proposed fair visit, the newly announced budget measures and the shifting Probe record will compete for attention. Edliex has a verification method, but only people with the right authority can use it to make the next real decision.

The sealed box is still waiting.

## THE CLASSIES CHALLENGE — EPISODE 02

A facility displays four translated emergency notices. Three arrows agree, but the fourth differs. A distribution record and a sealed archive box provide clues to the notices' history; neither proves which instruction is current or safe. **What evidence and authorizations should be established before anyone treats one displayed route as the facility's operative emergency instruction?**
