# OUTPUT A — CONSOLIDATED EPISODE

## THE AUTHORITY BEHIND THE ARROW

### Opening — Four languages, one unanswered question

A building could display the same instruction in four languages and still fail to tell its occupants what they needed to know.

That was the problem Enka had been trying to understand since the beginning of November.

At a facility in Kuala Lumpur, four emergency notices had appeared near a lift. They were printed in Malay, English, Mandarin and Tamil. Three directed occupants towards one stairwell. The fourth indicated another direction.

The notices looked official. They were professionally printed, properly displayed and sufficiently similar to suggest that they belonged to the same administrative process.

But similarity was not proof of authority.

The Classies team had been assigned to observe and document the circumstances surrounding the notices. They were not authorized to intervene, test disputed routes or issue replacement safety instructions. Their responsibility was to preserve evidence and establish what could legitimately be concluded from it.

At Koware headquarters in New Legon, Accra, Enka watched the investigation develop through reports, photographs, registers and correspondence.

The more information arrived, the more important one distinction became:

**A document could prove that something had happened without proving that the person who initiated it had the authority to make it happen.**

The investigation would take ten working days to expose the different layers of that problem.

## WEEK ONE — THE RECORDS BEGIN TO SPEAK

### Monday, 2 November 2026

#### Day 01 — The Notice in Four Languages

The Koware compound was still quiet when Enka opened his study notes.

The Eternal Dreamfall reading plan had reached Week 96, continuing its study of the Septuagint. The weekly plan identified the subject but did not prescribe a particular daily passage. Enka considered the challenge of carrying meaning between languages without losing the distinctions that mattered.

The question stayed with him as he reached the laboratory.

The historical-reference display showed a Sunday in July 2003 in Las Vegas. Enka treated the historical material as a reference for observation, not as evidence of anything happening in the present. The Millennium Probe maintained its separate future-oriented reading, while the deployed team's supplied geographic position remained Kuala Lumpur.

Three different temporal contexts. Three different purposes.

At the laboratory, the specialist brought up a communication from the field team.

The analyst was standing beside the lift in a Kuala Lumpur building. Behind the analyst were four emergency notices, each carrying the same general message but not the same directional instruction.

“Three versions say left of the lift,” the analyst reported. “One says right.”

The team lead considered the photographs.

“Three copies can come from one mistake.”

Nobody approached either stairwell.

Instead, the team photographed each notice, recorded its position and appearance, and documented the differences in wording and direction. The one that disagreed with the others was mounted slightly lower and was the only one carrying a printed date.

Enka studied the images.

“Record what the signs say,” he instructed. “Record what the corridor is. Don't let one stand in for the other.”

The field team confirmed that it would remain within its observational assignment.

Back in New Legon, Enka wrote three questions in his notebook:

- What instruction had actually been issued?
- Who had authority to issue it?
- Did the displayed translations accurately represent the original instruction?

Four languages had carried the message across the building.

But four languages did not necessarily mean four independent sources.

**The first day established the central mystery: the notices existed, but their authority and reliability remained unverified.**

### Tuesday, 3 November 2026

#### Day 02 — The Cropped Truth

The following morning, Enka returned to the investigation.

The four notices were still the starting point, but he wanted to move beyond their appearance.

He contacted the building manager and asked for the administrative history behind them.

Who had requested the notice?

Who had prepared the original wording?

Who had authorized the translations?

When had the different versions been displayed?

Had the same instruction been distributed through other channels?

The manager explained that notices could pass through several people before reaching their final destination. One person might request the information, another prepare the wording, and another arrange for the copies to be displayed.

The chain of responsibility was not necessarily obvious from the finished document.

Enka listened carefully.

A document could be accurate in four languages and still communicate four different things. Equally, four apparently independent copies might all have originated from one unverified source.

The building manager agreed to examine the records.

There was no breakthrough, but the investigation now had a more precise direction.

At Koware, the day's organizational responsibilities reminded Enka that workplace systems depended on people, procedures and clear accountability. A notice was not merely paper. It could influence how people moved through a building and responded to an emergency.

That made the issuing authority important.

It also made premature conclusions dangerous.

The team continued its observation-only assignment. The latest supplied Probe information was retained as a separate temporal record, not used to establish the field team's physical or temporal position.

Enka closed the day's notes with a reminder:

**The first version of a story is not necessarily the complete version.**

A cropped photograph could conceal the surrounding context. A translated notice could conceal a change in meaning. An administrative record could show the movement of a document while omitting the authority behind it.

The team needed the complete record, not the most convenient fragment.

### Wednesday, 4 November 2026

#### Day 03 — The Missing Approval Line

The reply arrived without the document Enka had requested.

The building manager supplied a distribution register. It showed that copies of the emergency notices had been placed in the building.

Enka read it twice.

“Does this tell us who approved the instruction?”

The laboratory specialist examined the register.

“It tells us the copies were distributed. It doesn't show the source they came from.”

“Or who authorized that source.”

The register was useful, but its usefulness had limits.

It established that four copies had been distributed. It did not establish that the original instruction had been approved, or that each translation faithfully represented it.

Enka added two headings to his notebook:

**WHAT THIS RECORD SHOWS**

**WHAT THIS RECORD DOES NOT SHOW**

The distinction changed the investigation. The team now had evidence of distribution, but still no proof of authority.

The deployed team confirmed that it had not followed either conflicting direction. It had not tested an evacuation route or approached building occupants. Any assessment of the routes would have to be conducted by the responsible building staff under the applicable safety arrangements.

“Good,” Enka said. “The notices are evidence. They are not permission to improvise.”

He returned to the Health & Safety material concerning hazard identification and risk assessment. A disagreement between instructions could itself be a warning sign. It was not a reason for an unbriefed observer to decide which route was safe.

The day's organizational spotlight concerned dependable technical support and operations. Enka considered the quiet importance of document traceability, version control and reliable handovers.

A missing approval line might appear insignificant until something went wrong.

Before closing the file, he placed the distribution register in the evidence column. He did not classify the notice as genuine or false.

The next request was specific: locate the original instruction and the record showing who approved it, when it took effect and which versions were issued.

Four languages had carried the message.

The missing line was the one that would establish who had the authority to send it.

### Thursday, 5 November 2026

#### Day 04 — The Date That Would Not Stay Still

The Probe display had changed again.

Enka stood beside the laboratory console at Koware headquarters. The destination date differed from earlier readings.

The specialist opened the run record.

Historical reference. Current date. Phase boundaries. Oscillation index. Amplitude. Probe value.

Each field occupied its own line.

“The dates are changing intentionally,” the specialist explained. “The Millennium Probe recalculates its output in relation to historical data and the current date.”

Enka looked at the display.

“So we shouldn't call a changed date a changed destination until we know what the reading represents.”

“Exactly. First we need the run inputs and the calculation record.”

Enka asked that the raw response and all earlier readings be preserved. None was to be replaced merely because a newer value had appeared.

The specialist agreed.

The conversation turned to the deployed team. Its supplied physical position was Kuala Lumpur. That established a geographic reference, but it did not independently establish the team's temporal position or explain the meaning of the new Probe timestamp.

Enka also examined an older design sheet bearing his handwriting.

“I designed the original Probe scheme,” he explained. “And the cloned Edubeam was initially operated solely by the Micro Computer Technology Institute of London. That history belongs beside the readings.”

“Beside them, but not in place of them,” the specialist replied.

Enka wrote three headings:

**WHAT THE SOURCE RETURNED**

**WHAT THE CALCULATION MAY MEAN**

**WHAT WE HAVE NOT VERIFIED**

The Probe investigation remained separate from the emergency-notice investigation. Neither mystery would be used to explain away the other.

Two questions were now open: who authorized the notice, and how should the changing Probe readings be interpreted?

The same rule applied to both.

Record what is known. Label what is inferred. Preserve the evidence needed to settle the question.

### Friday, 6 November 2026

#### Day 05 — The Copies That Agreed

By Friday, the investigation had accumulated several different kinds of evidence.

The notices themselves.

The distribution record.

The request for the original instruction.

The building manager's correspondence.

The field team's photographs.

The records did not all prove the same thing.

The team reviewed the differences between the four versions. The apparent agreement among three notices was not sufficient to establish that their instruction was correct. Nor did the fourth notice become correct simply because it was different.

The original source remained important.

So did the question of whether the building had changed since the instruction was first prepared.

The team also reviewed its own methods. Earlier drafts of the investigation had proposed different actions and interpretations. Those proposals were not automatically accepted as events. The team retained the restrictions that mattered: observation only, no unauthorized route testing and no invented field confirmation.

Enka considered the day's reading and the importance of examining information that was true, reliable and worthy of trust.

The distinction was not merely academic. An apparently complete record could still be misleading if its context had been removed.

A work order could confirm that a task had been carried out. It could not necessarily confirm that the underlying instruction had been authorized.

A receipt could confirm that a package had arrived. It could not confirm that the recipient had understood its contents.

A translation could be accurate relative to the document supplied to the translator while the original document itself remained wrong.

The team agreed to preserve the competing versions and their provenance.

At the end of the week, Enka reviewed the evidence.

The notices had been distributed. Their original source was still uncertain. The approval record had not been found. The team had not established which route, if either, represented the building's current authorized emergency arrangements.

The investigation had made progress, but the answer remained out of reach.

## WEEK TWO — AUTHORITY, IDENTITY AND CONSEQUENCE

### Monday, 9 November 2026

#### Day 06 — The Line Beneath the Arrow

Four emergency notices occupied the table at Koware's fictional New Legon headquarters.

Three pointed towards one stairwell. The fourth pointed towards a courtyard corridor.

Johnson Ortsin unfolded a site plan.

“If three copies say east, we have a majority,” he said.

“A majority of paper,” Derek Shiraska replied. “Not a verified exit.”

Matthias Agbenorku arrived with the unfinished operations notes. The latest proposed field report indicated that the building manager had denied issuing the notices. A distribution work order, marked **44-E**, appeared to account for their installation, but not for the authority behind the instruction.

“So they were put up properly,” Matthias said, “and we still don't know whether they tell anyone the right thing to do.”

Enka listened.

“Let's not argue about which arrow wins,” he said. “Find out who had the right to draw it.”

The team prepared a narrow request to the responsible safety officer. Until a verified procedure could be established locally, nobody in the Classies field team would conduct a walk-through based on the disputed notices.

The team also recognized that finding the original instruction would not, by itself, settle the matter. The original would have to be compared with the current building layout and the four displayed language versions.

A genuine old instruction could still be unsuitable for a building that had changed.

Later, a reference to the Accra electricity-maintenance schedule prompted an equipment-readiness discussion. The schedule listed different communities on different days, but it did not establish the supply status of Koware headquarters itself.

Matthias circled the relevant date and requested verification rather than declaring that the building would lose power.

At 14:10 in Accra, a fictional field update indicated that the regional records office could trace Work Order 44-E to an older administrative file transfer.

A matching storage box had been located.

It had not been opened.

Johnson put down his pen.

“We found the box.”

“We found where the box is,” Derek corrected.

The distinction was becoming familiar.

The team had located a possible source of the missing instruction, but the contents remained unknown and release authority was unresolved.

The Probe records were also retained separately, with their dates and limitations clearly marked.

Enka looked at the two investigations on his desk.

One concerned the meaning of a changing temporal reading.

The other concerned the authority behind a printed instruction.

Both required the same discipline: a record was only as useful as the claim it could actually support.

### Tuesday, 10 November 2026

#### Day 07 — The Name the Paper Could Not Give

The smallest space on the page was where the trouble began.

Mubarak Mohsen held the request beneath the New Legon office light.

Above the empty line were three things that looked reassuring: a transfer reference, a date and an official-looking stamp.

Below them was a question:

**WHO IS AUTHORIZED TO REQUEST RELEASE?**

There was no name.

The records indicated that a storage box had been transferred. Work Order 44-E was associated with the distribution process. A sealed archive box had been located through an older transfer record.

But the custodian would not release it without a properly authorized request.

Mubarak arranged the forms.

“They all look complete,” he said, “until you compare what each one is supposed to prove.”

On a nearby screen, Enka reviewed the proposed communication to the team in Kuala Lumpur.

The draft said:

“We have found the authorizing instruction. Proceed to check the route.”

Mubarak stopped him.

“We have found a possible container for the instruction. The box is still sealed.”

Enka deleted the sentence.

The revised request sought the issuing office's authority, preserved the conflicting notices and asked for the responsible local personnel to establish what interim safety guidance applied.

It did not authorize a route inspection or claim that the original instruction had been found.

The team remained within its observational assignment.

Meanwhile, the organizational staff considered the difference between a transfer reference and an authorized request. A document could have a date and a signature yet still fail to establish that the person signing it possessed the necessary authority.

The same issue appeared in the team's records. A supplied location could identify a geographic position without authenticating the condition or whereabouts of every individual.

Enka kept the historical-reference clock, the production clock and the Probe clock separate.

A timestamp could be precise without explaining the event associated with it.

A name could appear on a form without proving the authority to act.

Before the day ended, Mubarak prepared a draft request for review. He signed only for the office's request to be considered, not on behalf of the authority it needed.

The authorization line remained blank.

Then he noticed a second question on the form:

**DOES THE ORIGINAL INSTRUCTION STILL MATCH THE BUILDING AS IT STANDS TODAY?**

Even a valid signature would not answer that.

### Wednesday, 11 November 2026

#### Day 08 — The Message That Stayed Unsent: The Empty Space

The first thing the Legal Journalist removed from the reception pinboard was not a headline.

It was a word.

**CONFIRMED.**

The marker went through it. Beneath it, the replacement word was smaller:

**REPORTED.**

In Koware's imagined headquarters, the board had become a meeting point for information. Staff paused to read announcements about business, projects and public developments.

The Legal Journalist wanted every item to distinguish its source, publication date, verification status and relevance.

The Public Relations & Press Coordinator studied the latest draft.

“The description says the Kuala Lumpur instructions were approved and distributed.”

“Distributed is what the work order suggests,” replied the Marketing Manager. “Approved is what we keep trying to prove.”

They were discussing the unresolved fictional investigation.

Four versions of an emergency notice had appeared in the working record. Three pointed in one direction and a fourth in another. Work Order 44-E provided evidence of distribution, not authorization. A sealed archive box might contain an earlier instruction, but no properly authorized release had been established.

The Public Relations desk declined to publish the team's conclusion as an official safety notice.

The Marketing desk withdrew a dramatic headline it had drafted.

They agreed to preserve the disagreement in the internal editorial record without presenting either route as safe.

Enka looked at the day's Bible App reference, Matthew 6:11, and considered the value of reliable information for the next responsible step.

The Legal Journalist reserved a blank rectangle on the pinboard:

**AUTHORITY NOT YET VERIFIED.**

The blank space was an honest piece of reporting.

### Thursday, 12 November 2026

#### Day 09 — The Message That Stayed Unsent: The Cost of a Wrong Verb

By Thursday, the International Relations desk was working from three separate questions.

What was the instruction's original source?

Who could authorize it?

Who would be accountable for checking that it remained suitable for the building as it existed now?

The Global Partnerships Registrar arranged copies of the Kuala Lumpur paperwork by document number. The Global Partnerships Liaison created a second register, recording the language of each version, its recipient and whether delivery had been acknowledged.

“An acknowledgement is not comprehension,” the liaison said. “And comprehension is not permission.”

Nobody claimed to know the deployed team's individual condition or whereabouts. The supplied geographic position remained Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia, but no fresh authenticated field communication was available to the office.

On Enka's research display were three clocks.

The production present belonged to Accra in November 2026.

The historical-reference setting pointed to Las Vegas in July 2003.

The Millennium Probe displayed a separate fictional future reference.

None established the team's current physical position or the validity of an emergency route.

Later, the Legal Journalist queued a Ghanaian budget item for verification. The reported New Economy programme gave the Marketing desk something to analyze, but it was not a Koware contract, grant approval or authority to announce a partnership.

The International Relations team also examined a developing report about disruption to widely used communications services. It raised a practical question: if an important approval request disappeared inside an inaccessible inbox, how could anyone demonstrate what had been sent, received or authorized?

The team proposed a secondary contact channel and an offline decision record.

They did not claim that Koware itself had suffered an outage.

That evening, the registrar added an annotation beside the sealed-box reference:

*Location indicated; contents unknown; release authority unresolved.*

The line did not solve the mystery.

It prevented the mystery from being mistaken for a solution.

### Friday, 13 November 2026

#### Day 10 — The Message That Stayed Unsent: The Unsent Instruction

By Friday morning, the legal newsroom and reception pinboard were crowded.

The Legal Journalist had separated verified reporting from developing investigations. The New Economy budget, the Ghana Stock Exchange discussion of blockchain and capital-market modernization, the Ecobank JoyBusiness SME Fair and the Malaysian investigation into possible AI-chip re-exports were each recorded with their own source and verification status.

The Malaysia investigation was not confused with the fictional Kuala Lumpur emergency-notice inquiry. They concerned the same country, but they did not establish the same facts.

The Events & Tours Coordinator saw the SME fair as a possible opportunity for staff to observe Ghanaian businesses. The Community Outreach Officer wanted to understand what smaller exhibitors actually needed.

The Booking Agent had a spreadsheet of interested staff and a separate space for confirmed arrangements.

“Those names mean they would like to attend,” the Booking Agent said. “We don't have a confirmed booking.”

“Then let's not put anyone on a bus in our meeting minutes,” the coordinator replied.

The proposed visit remained a proposal.

During the morning's preparations, the Booking Agent attempted to slide an overloaded folder beneath the meeting-room door while balancing a stack of attendance forms. The folder wedged, the forms sprang upward, and one large sheet landed squarely on the agent's head like an oversized paper hat.

For a moment, the room forgot the paperwork.

Then the Legal Journalist removed another word from a draft announcement:

**CONFIRMED.**

It was not confirmed.

The day's work returned to the Kuala Lumpur file.

The archive box remained a possible source of the original instruction. Its location had been recorded. The transfer history had been traced. The request for release still required the appropriate authority.

Even if the box were opened, the document inside would still need to be checked against the current building layout and the language versions on display.

The message that Enka had considered sending remained unsent.

It had claimed too much.

It implied that the team had found an authoritative instruction when the evidence established only that a possible source had been located.

It could have encouraged action before the necessary verification was complete.

Enka looked again at the blank authorization line.

The investigation had not produced the neat conclusion everyone had wanted. It had produced something more useful: a clearer account of what was known, what remained uncertain and which decisions belonged to the responsible people.

The notices had been distributed.

The transfer record existed.

The box had been located.

The issuing authority had not been established.

The original instruction had not been authenticated.

The current building layout had not been independently reconciled with the disputed directions.

And the Classies team had not been authorized to test either route.

Enka closed the file.

There would be another request, another review and another opportunity to establish the truth. But no one would turn a plausible document into a safety instruction simply because the story needed an ending.

The message stayed unsent.

The investigation remained open.

And the blank line, for once, said exactly what it needed to say.

## Season 00 — Foundation Phase
## Chapter 05 — The Signal Before Sunrise
## Episode 02 — Four Arrows, No Signature

The first mistake looked like a direction. The second looked like proof. By the time Enka understood the difference, eight people had handled the same message, four translations had repeated it, and no one could say who had first been entitled to give it.

---

## Part 1: The Notice in Four Languages

Before sunrise, rain ticked against the louvers of the Koware Group’s New Legon Estates compound. Five single-storey, three-bedroom apartment blocks stood close enough that a light coming on in one block laid a square across the wall of the next. The shared boundaries made it easy to mistake proximity for agreement. Enka would think about that again before the morning ended.

At the research block, a photograph filled the display: a rectangular emergency notice, four language panels, and a bold directional arrow. Another image arrived. Then two more. Each had been photographed in a different part of a Kuala Lumpur building being observed by a remote Edubeam-associated team. The team’s assignment was to document anomalies and maintain contact, not to manage a building or direct its occupants.

“Four languages,” said the R&D director. “One arrow.”

“Four opportunities for the same error,” Enka answered.

At the far end of the remote observation channel, a team member paused under a corridor camera as a building staff member went past with a roll of temporary tape. No one was confronted; the team took a permitted photograph of the wall after the staff member passed. The first photograph looked ordinary. The second showed a differently positioned arrow. The third had a line under the arrow that the camera could scarcely resolve. In the fourth, a reflection ran across the text. None could settle what the building’s current authorized emergency procedure required.

An operations liaison recommended asking the building manager for the source instruction, its approval history and the current posted evacuation plan. At the conference table, Edliex listened in its customary silent phase. A narrow blue line on its console pulsed whenever someone spoke. The legal journalist entered the incident on the online pinboard, with a brief paper notice for the reception area: **UNVERIFIED INSTRUCTIONS — DO NOT TREAT PHOTOGRAPHS AS AUTHORITY**.

At the physical pinboard, someone stood on tiptoe to straighten the photograph. The stack of magnetic clips escaped its tray, marched down the metal frame and snapped onto the soles of his shoes. He took two ridiculous clanking steps before everyone noticed. It was the only moment the room allowed itself to laugh.

Edliex shifted into feedback mode. “Observed: four posted variants. Inferred: possible inconsistency. Not established: which, if any, is the controlled current instruction.” The team had learned to welcome the pauses between those categories.

Then conversational mode opened. Enka asked, “What action is justified?”

“Request an authoritative record. Preserve what was observed. Do not substitute our interpretation for the building’s responsible emergency personnel.”

Enka sent the field team a simple restriction: photograph and record from permitted positions; do not test either route or instruct occupants. If immediate danger arose, follow the responsible local emergency authorities and actual current safety arrangements, not the experimental team’s guesswork.

The team acknowledged. Behind the research block, Accra traffic began to growl awake. Across two cities and several screens, the arrows remained exactly where they had been. They had not become safer merely because people were now looking at them.

What troubled Enka was not that the notices disagreed. It was how confidently each one appeared to speak.

## Part 2: The Cropped Truth

The next morning, a single image of the notice reached Koware’s reception screen without the surrounding wall, the lower line or the panel beside it. Underneath, an impatient caption declared that investigators had already discovered which way people should go.

They had not.

The legal journalist removed the image from the public-facing draft of the pinboard and pinned the intact photograph beside it in the internal newsroom review. In the original, an indistinct line continued below the arrow. In the crop, that line vanished. The missing strip did not prove the cropped instruction false; it made the caption’s certainty indefensible.

Enka walked from the administration block to the media workroom, where the creative team was preparing a simple visual comparison. They placed a transparent border over the source photograph and showed the crop as a smaller rectangle inside it. An arrow was still visible in both. The surrounding context was not.

“People will remember the arrow before they remember our correction,” said the producer.

“Then make them remember the border,” the journalist replied.

Outside, a courier motorcycle passed the shared compound gate. At reception, a visitor asked whether the notice referred to a real public emergency. The concierge liaison checked the visitor register and gave a restrained answer: Koware was reviewing an unverified document in connection with an observation project; it was not issuing building instructions. No address or private operational detail was put on display.

The journalist clicked the spinning office chair backward to reach a cabinet, forgot that its wheels had unlocked, and glided away from the drawer in a slow, helpless retreat. He managed to keep both hands triumphantly on the untouched file as the room broke into laughter. Then he rolled back and opened it properly.

The day’s pinboard also carried a Ghana News Agency report on the government’s preference for media self-regulation. The newsroom placed that headline alongside its own question, not as evidence about the Kuala Lumpur notice but as a parallel civic lesson: editorial independence brings a duty to verify the whole context.

Edliex listened to the editorial debate, gave feedback only when invited, then answered Enka’s request for a minimal correction. “State the crop omits visible context. State there is no verified approved original. Do not manufacture a definitive route.”

The revised internal bulletin went to the team. The external post was withheld pending clearance and relevance. A colleague at a crowded Accra bus stop messaged the newsroom after seeing a forwarded screen grab. He wanted to know which route the picture proved was safe. The journalist answered only that no verified route determination had been made. The first viral caption had already been copied once; they could not make it unexist. They could, however, preserve the exact image and the exact words so the error would not become their own.

Late in the afternoon, a message came from the building manager: a distribution register might be in the records room. Enka enlarged the photograph one last time. The black border of the cropped image seemed almost too neat. Someone had removed precisely the portion that complicated the claim.

He wrote in the evidence notebook: **A truthful fragment is not necessarily a truthful instruction.**

## Part 3: The Unsigned Fourth Notice

The register arrived as a scanned attachment the following day. Its columns recorded distribution locations, copy counts and language versions. No approval signature appeared beside the originating instruction.

Enka laid the scan beside the four photographs. “This tells us copies were circulated,” he said.

The technical support and operations manager leaned over the table. “Not which source they were copied from. Not whether the source was current.”

“And not who authorized it,” Enka said.

The previous safety review had reminded them that recognizing a potential hazard and choosing controls were separate steps. The team was not qualified to improvise the building’s emergency plan from photographs. They divided the question without dividing the facts. The operations team preserved the files and their receipt times. The academic side drafted a controlled comparison procedure for the language panels, with independent linguistic verification still required. The legal journalist revised the pinboard to distinguish distribution from authorization. The field team remained observation-only and reported no attempt to follow either conflicting arrow.

At reception, the fourth printout lacked a clean space where an authorizing mark might have been expected. Nobody inferred that a mark had been deliberately erased. The manager’s email said only that the original instruction and approval record had not yet been located; the search would continue.

The concierge liaison prepared a tray for the arriving documents. A self-inking stamp, set down too close to the edge, bounced onto the floor, stamped a perfect **RECEIVED** on the side of a cardboard box, then rolled to a stop beneath a chair. It was a magnificently official declaration about the wrong object. The documents themselves were left unstamped until their provenance was recorded correctly.

A news item about Ghana’s recently launched automated road-enforcement system now occupied a corner of the pinboard. Staff debated the same distinction in a different setting: an electronic notice, however impressive, must still be authenticated through the proper channel. Nobody suggested the Ghanaian report explained the Kuala Lumpur incident.

Edliex’s feedback came in three short lines: “Distribution established by an unverified copy of a register. Approval not established. Translation fidelity not established.”

“Why say ‘unverified copy’?” asked Enka.

“Because the scan has not been independently authenticated,” Edliex replied in conversational mode. “A manager’s good-faith delivery is evidence of what was sent to us, not a substitute for the custodian’s original.”

The room went quiet. Their evidence was getting better and their right to act was not growing at the same speed.

Enka drafted a narrower records request: the source instruction, authorizer, effective date, controlled version history, language-approval records and any replacement notices. By evening the request had been sent to the building manager through the existing contact. Nothing had been posted for occupants, and no field action had changed.

Four sheets sat on the table like four witnesses who could all repeat the same sentence but none could swear who had first spoken it.

## Part 4: The Instruction No One Could Sign

A new Millennium Probe reading lit the laboratory wall. The destination timestamp had changed again. The current date had not leapt forward with it; the displayed output belonged to a different clock altogether.

The R&D specialist opened the run history. Under a transparent cover lay an early Probe schematic in Enka’s handwriting. He recognized it immediately. The original design history and the cloned Edubeam’s first operation under the Micro Computer Technology Institute of London belonged beside the readings as archival story records—not as proof that any one output had physically relocated a person. Enka insisted that the raw inputs and previous readings remain preserved rather than replaced. The changing destination display, the historical reference and the present-day production clock would each retain its own label. A number with six decimal places was not, by itself, a travel order.

“Do we tell the Kuala Lumpur team the date changed?” the specialist asked.

“We can tell them what the console displayed. We cannot tell them that they changed century, building or physical position. Those are different claims.”

The proposed destination was a temporal calculation, not a verified scene that anyone could enter merely by naming it. The human team’s reported physical context remained Kuala Lumpur. Its exact individual positions, equipment condition and temporal relation to the Probe result were not confirmed in the material available to the control room.

Across the shared boundary, the academic team was examining a separate problem. The building manager had offered to circulate a clarification, but no authorized person had supplied approved wording. An employee suggested preparing a draft with a signature line ready for the manager. The quality colleague rejected the shortcut: a blank signature line could invite someone to treat an unapproved message as official.

During a tense review, the R&D specialist extended a telescopic pointer toward a graph. The pointer sprang shut with a metallic pop, leaving him presenting the audience with a handle no longer than a teaspoon. He stared at it for a beat. Even Enka smiled. Then the graph went back on the screen.

In the meeting, Edliex listened while the human team weighed clarity against authority. Its first feedback was unwelcome: “The control room cannot manufacture a building manager’s authorization. A draft written here cannot repair the approval chain there.”

“Suppose we write the safest possible instruction,” said one colleague.

“Safe under which verified current plan?” another asked.

That ended the argument. Instead, they prepared questions for the responsible building staff, including how a real emergency would be handled during verification. The staff member who had asked for a shortcut crossed it out personally.

By dusk, Enka had preserved the Probe response and the unsigned draft in different repositories with different status labels. The two puzzles shared a discipline but not necessarily a cause. The Probe calculation still needed its method; the notice still needed its lawful origin.

The night brought a fresh remote image. It did not show a signature. It showed a folded edge of paper, beneath which a thin line of smaller text could now be seen.

## Part 5: The Copies That Agreed

The morning comparison produced an almost embarrassing discovery: the four panels agreed more closely than anyone had expected. Their directions appeared consistent when translated back into a common working language. The original approved wording was still missing.

“A choir can sing the wrong song in perfect harmony,” the quality assurance manager said.

The academic registrar looked at the side-by-side printouts. “Agreement tells us something about copying. It does not tell us whether the first instruction should have been issued.”

With that distinction, the inquiry changed pace. A linguistically consistent set of copies could still reproduce an obsolete order. A clean distribution register could still omit authorization. The team set each fact in its own column, then listed the evidence required to promote it from working hypothesis to confirmed account.

At the facility block, someone opened a window to disperse a stubborn smell from the printer. The draft comparison sheets, left beneath the fan, rose together in a white flurry and landed on the quality manager’s head like an extravagant paper crown. The team retrieved them without altering their order. The quality manager took the crown off with solemn dignity and announced, “At least these copies agree on where to land.” It was the day’s one small mercy.

The legal journalist placed a verified report about transport unions suspending a planned Accra strike on the pinboard. He emphasized the difference between an announced action, its suspension, and the remaining demands. In the discussion that followed, a cleaner worried about what uncertainty in transport plans meant for staff getting home. The corporate manager worried about operational notices reaching people in time. Different jobs, different pressures, the same need to record what had actually changed.

Edliex withheld its conversational turn until the humans had exhausted their theories. Then it asked: “If all four copies match, what independent evidence tells us they match an authorized original?”

Nobody answered. That was the point.

Enka sent the building manager the comparison summary with the limitations on its first page. The remote team received only the observation policy and confirmation that building personnel remained responsible for any actual emergency response. No experimental reconnaissance would be converted into a route recommendation.

The smaller line in the photograph had now been enhanced enough to show that some text existed below the arrow. Its wording was still unreadable. An impatient colleague proposed filling it in from context. Enka refused. The absent words might change everything—or nothing. Guessing would change the evidence for certain.

The episode’s most convincing image had become four copies of a sentence nobody could yet authorize.

## Part 6: The Line Beneath the Arrow

By the start of the next working week, the programme manager had made a board with two headings: **ACTION AUTHORIZED** and **ACTION REQUESTED**. The first column was short. The second had become crowded.

Under the damaged arrow in the source photograph, a tiny line of print finally resolved enough to invite transcription. It appeared to refer to a controlled issue or a condition on the sign’s use; neither the exact words nor their authority had been established. The team refused to publish a guessed transcription. Instead, they logged the region of the image as a separate evidence item, with the original, enhanced version, method and uncertainty preserved.

The production coordinator proposed a visit to a local sign-printing workshop to understand how such labels could be produced. It would not locate the Kuala Lumpur document’s source, but it could help them design a fair comparison exercise. Enka approved a limited, clearly educational visit, not a covert investigation. On a nearby Accra street the coordinator examined sample stock, edge trimming and print registrations while the liaison recorded openly discussed methods. No private customer records were requested.

Back at Koware, the programme manager unfurled the visit report. The telescopic display stand refused to lock; the whole chart slipped downward until the title **CONTROLLED COPY** hovered just above the floor. Its presenter had to finish one sentence in a crouch. The staff applauded the improvised delivery once, then returned to the technical material.

The legal journalist’s pinboard now linked two externally reported debates that mattered to staff for different reasons: transport safety and digital-record authentication. The cleaner noticed a photograph of faded road markings. The software colleague noticed the article’s description of electronic notices. The journalist asked each what question their job would send to management, and their answers were not the same.

Edliex listened, gave feedback, then entered conversational mode. “An enhanced image is a derived exhibit,” it said. “The original remains the reference. You may discover candidate characters in the pixels, but you have not discovered a signatory.”

The field team forwarded one more permitted photograph: the lower edge of the notice appeared to align with an older adhesive rectangle beneath it. That could indicate replacement, layering or routine re-posting. No one was close enough, authorized enough or safe enough to peel it back. The team kept its distance.

The programme manager imposed a deadline on the report, not on the truth. By the evening check-in, the group would document which requests were pending and who had authority to respond. A due date could organize human work; it could not force a missing signature into existence.

Before leaving, Enka found the earliest uncropped photograph again. In the margin someone had written a small annotation: **Ask who owned the instruction before asking who copied it.**

## Part 7: The Name the Paper Could Not Give

The facility manager walked the compound at first light, checking entrances and shared boundaries before work gathered pace. Five buildings pressed against one another without losing their separate responsibilities. That was how he explained the notice problem when Enka met him by the gate.

“A message can cross a wall,” he said. “Authority does not cross automatically with it.”

The building manager in Kuala Lumpur had now confirmed that a records search was under way. The available correspondence still did not identify the person who approved the contested wording. Koware had the distribution scan, the photographs, the comparison, and a chain of receipt notes. It did not have the authorized master or a dependable link from master to posted copies.

The facility manager suggested mapping the evidence like a maintenance history. When a pump is serviced, a useful record shows the item, date, work, person and next inspection. A handwritten name alone is weak evidence; a system-generated mark alone is weak evidence; each needs a legitimate process behind it. The quality manager agreed to put the document equivalent into the case register.

A trolley wheel caught the seam between two paving stones as the manager carried an empty inspection clipboard. The clipboard launched into the air, flipped neatly and landed on his head like an officer’s peaked cap. He saluted with the pencil, recovered the book and continued walking. The shared courtyard had acquired its daily laugh.

Later, the legal journalist proposed a headline for the internal pinboard: **WHO SIGNED IT?** Enka changed it to **WHAT AUTHORIZING RECORD EXISTS?** The first question invited a face and a culprit before evidence. The second invited documents that could be tested.

They checked the external news desk too. A report about Ghana’s planned public-service digital transformation provided a reason for staff to discuss interoperability, audit trails and the limits of convenience. It had no known causal connection to the notice case. That distinction was printed beneath the headline so readers would not confuse thematic relevance with a lead.

Edliex’s feedback was unusually quiet: “One unknown may conceal several different failures. An instruction could be unapproved, approved but unrecoverable, obsolete, misfiled or incorrectly represented in the photographs. The record does not yet distinguish them.”

Enka asked the AI to turn the alternatives into a question list, not a verdict. The manager’s response would determine the next step. The field team stayed in observation status. No additional place, person or century was assigned to them by the Probe display.

As sunset changed the color of the compound wall, Enka scanned the record one more time. Every arrow was legible. Every copy had a place. The authority column remained blank because the evidence was blank, not because the team had forgotten to fill it.

For the first time he saw the blank as a useful result.

## Part 8: The Message That Stayed Unsent

The draft advisory occupied the centre of the control room screen. The legal journalist had written it in language that refused the temptation of drama: the reviewed photographs and distribution information did not establish the source, current validity or approval of the posted instruction; building occupants should seek direction from the responsible building authorities and applicable current emergency arrangements. The draft made no claim about which arrow should be followed.

It was clear, measured and potentially useful. It was also not Koware’s message to issue as an official building notice.

Enka read it aloud at the morning meeting. Edliex listened. The R&D director confirmed that no new Probe output had changed the field team’s verified physical status. The quality manager identified the remaining evidence gaps. The programme manager set out a communication chain that stopped at the building manager’s authority rather than running past it.

The journalist wanted to send the advisory to every contact on the building’s list. The facility manager objected: a broad message could be mistaken for an official emergency instruction. The journalist argued that silence could also be misunderstood. Neither dismissed the other’s risk.

Edliex entered feedback mode. “There are three actions, not one: preserve the draft; transmit a request for authoritative guidance to the responsible manager through the existing channel; publish or distribute only when the proper authority and content are confirmed.”

When conversational mode opened, Enka asked, “And the advisory itself?”

“Hold it.”

Someone at the edge of the table tried to pull out a jammed drawer of message receipts. It came loose suddenly, and a harmless rain of blank envelopes fluttered down around his shoulders. For one absurd second he looked like a magician who had failed to produce a single letter. The laughter passed quickly. No one tried the drawer again.

The team sent the narrow records request, not the broad advisory. The external-message queue showed the proposed advisory as **UNSENT — AWAITING AUTHORIZED CONTENT AND DISTRIBUTION**. The field observers received the same standing instruction: maintain lawful observation, do not test conflicting routes, and defer any real emergency to responsible local personnel. No fictional technological capability was promoted into a safety procedure.

A verified news clipping about Ghanaian law-enforcement training in data integrity remained pinned in reception. Beside it, the journalist left a smaller printed statement from their own internal case notes: *Evidence can be copied. Authority must be traced.* Both were marked with their proper scopes.

Just before the end-of-day review, an inbound attachment reached the case inbox. It contained a picture of a notice unlike the four already catalogued: the same bold arrow, a longer lower line, and a version mark too blurred to read. The sender’s identity and the image’s origin were not verified. The system assigned it an incoming evidence identifier; it did not treat it as a solution.

Enka asked the team to preserve the untouched attachment and its metadata, then to isolate it pending proper source validation. The quality manager opened an exception entry. Nobody altered the approved safety boundary. Nobody called the building to announce a discovery they had not made.

The message on the outgoing screen remained unsent.

Across the room, the newly received photograph displayed a detail the earlier photographs had concealed: below the arrow, there appeared to be room for two separate version marks.

Enka stared at the marks and understood how the next mistake could begin. If they called the image genuine because it was useful, they would be standing in front of the same danger that had started the case.

He closed the preview, kept the original, and said, “First we find out who sent it.”

The control-room clock continued forward. The historical record stayed where it belonged. The Millennium Probe quietly calculated a destination that no one in the room claimed to have reached.

And between the three clocks, the unanswered notice waited.

### A glimpse of the next episode

An apparent version mark, a missing approval trail and a fresh inbound image will force Koware’s researchers, journalists and facilities team to decide what evidence deserves trust before a new instruction is allowed to travel. The unanswered question is no longer only which notice is current; it is how an unverified message acquires the appearance of authority.

## The Notice in Four Languages

### Monday, 2 November — Four copies

Before sunrise in New Legon, Enka returned to his notes from the Eternal Dreamfall study. The week’s working theme was the Septuagint and the long journey of meaning across languages. The plan in hand did not establish a passage for that particular day, so he left the daily reading line blank.

On the table, four photographs waited.

The notices were in four languages. Three appeared to direct building occupants toward one stairwell; the fourth pointed elsewhere. Their layout looked official. The field team’s report, relayed from Kuala Lumpur in this fictional investigation, was observational: the notices had been recorded, but no one had chosen a route or tested one.

“Four languages,” Enka said, “but how many sources?”

The R&D Lab Specialist did not guess. “We don’t know yet. They may all come from one original.”

Enka wrote **OBSERVED** at the top of a page and listed only what the photographs showed: four versions, several languages, inconsistent directions, and a date visible on one copy. Beneath it he wrote **NOT YET VERIFIED**. The original instruction, its author, its approval, and the relationship between the translations all belonged there.

The date on the odd notice drew their eyes. It might help trace the document. It did not make the notice right—or wrong.

The distinction felt familiar to Enka. A copy could carry a message far from its source and still fail to show who had authorized it. For now, the team would preserve the evidence and keep the investigation open.

### Tuesday, 3 November — The person behind the notice

The next day, Enka’s questions narrowed. Who had requested the notice? Who had prepared its original wording? Who approved the translations, and when were the different versions displayed?

The building manager was asked to check the records. The request was deliberate: ask for the chain of responsibility, not for someone to endorse whichever copy looked most convincing.

At the Koware briefing, the chapter’s working-day role check placed Health & Safety and personnel-support responsibilities in view. Enka treated that as a reason to consider how a public instruction might affect people—not as proof that any named employee had performed a real task.

The uncertainty was not a license to improvise. The assignment remained observational, and the team had no authority to decide which route people should use. Enka asked the lab to keep the photographs and questions together, so that a later document would not be mistaken for an answer before anyone checked what it could actually establish.

The original authorization was still missing. But the inquiry now had a person to ask and a record trail to seek.

### Wednesday, 4 November — A record, not an answer

A response arrived with a distribution register. It showed that copies had been issued. It did not identify who had approved the source instruction.

Enka placed the register beside the four photographs.

“What does it prove?” he asked.

“That the copies were tracked,” the specialist replied. “Not that the instruction was authorized.”

The team added the register to the **OBSERVED** column and left the approval question open. A record’s silence about a signature could not prove that no signature had ever existed; it showed only that this record did not supply one.

Several draft accounts of this day in the archive disagree about other events. The episode does not treat any of those disputed developments as settled. The durable thread is the notice investigation: circulation is not authorization, and the source remains unknown.

A date-specific verse, a specific Health & Safety lesson, and a fully verified headline set were not established in the material used for this episode. Enka did not fill those gaps with invented certainty.

### Thursday, 5 November — The date beside the record

The Millennium Probe display showed a different calculated date from earlier records. The exact reading and the explanation for its variation were not consistent across the drafts. Enka told the lab to preserve each source and its timestamp rather than overwrite one reading with another.

“The date can inform a question,” he said. “It cannot answer a different one.”

The field team’s supplied geographic position was Kuala Lumpur. A Probe coordinate did not prove where the team was in time, that anyone had travelled, or that a mission state had changed. The investigation into the notices remained separate from the instrument’s changing output.

The work-order trail supplied another useful distinction. A record could indicate that something had been distributed without establishing who had the authority to approve it. The team prepared a more precise request: find the original instruction, its approval record, its effective date, and the authorized versions.

No one in Koware’s fictional inquiry claimed authority over the facility’s current emergency arrangements. If there was an immediate safety concern, that belonged with the responsible local people and their verified procedures—not with a remote team counting copies.

### Friday, 6 November — The copies that agreed

By Friday, the four versions were lined up again. Three appeared to agree. The fourth differed.

The Director of Academic Programs studied them with the Faculty Coordinator and the Quality Assurance & Compliance Manager. Their roles gave the conversation different angles: how clearly an instruction had been communicated, how it might be understood, and how to document a discrepancy without disguising it as a finding.

“Three agreeing copies aren’t necessarily three independent witnesses,” the Faculty Coordinator said. “They could all descend from the same mistake.”

The quality-assurance lead treated the conflicting instruction as a potential hazard requiring proper assessment, not a puzzle for majority vote. The Director asked the group to preserve every version and explain the uncertainty plainly.

A real safety decision could not be made from appearance, repetition, or a changing Probe display. The source, revision history, applicable plan, and responsible authority still mattered.

The three copies that agreed had not solved the mystery. They had made the central question sharper: **what, exactly, did their agreement depend on?**

### Monday, 9 November — The line beneath the arrow

When the team revisited the notices, a possible source trail had emerged: Work Order 44-E and a reference to a records transfer. The documents suggested that a box might exist. They did not establish what it contained or who could authorize its release.

The Programme Manager had begun with a plan to rehearse the competing routes. The Production Coordinator stopped the plan before it became a physical test. They drew two columns instead: **WHAT WE CAN REVIEW** and **WHAT REQUIRES AUTHORITY**.

The Director of Operations helped redirect the work toward preserving the copies, identifying the competent local safety authority, and requesting the current site plan. Any rehearsal would remain on paper until a verified procedure and appropriate authorization existed.

At the reception pinboard, the Legal Journalist kept headlines and claims in their proper categories. A report, a forecast, and an official finding were not interchangeable. The team’s own uncertainty deserved the same care.

By the end of the day, the location of a possible records box had become a lead—not a discovery of the original instruction. When a custodian required a documented request from the responsible office, no one tried to bypass that boundary.

The arrow on the page still pointed somewhere. The investigation still had to establish who had the right to draw it.

### Tuesday, 10 November — The name the paper could not give

The transfer paperwork looked complete until the team compared what each line could prove. It showed a date and a reference, but not a verified authorizing name.

A proposed message to the field team said, *We have found the instruction. Proceed to check the route.*

Enka read it, then removed the first sentence. The instruction had not been found. A possible container had.

The revised draft asked for a properly authorized review, preservation of the conflicting copies, and guidance from the responsible local safety authority. It remained a request for verification—not an operational command—and was not treated as delivered.

### Wednesday, 11 November — The empty space

At the pinboard, the Legal Journalist crossed out **CONFIRMED** and wrote **REPORTED**.

The change was small enough to miss if someone hurried past. It mattered. The Kuala Lumpur inquiry had a distribution record, but the approving authority was still unknown. The Public Relations & Press Coordinator withheld an unsupported conclusion. The Marketing Manager withdrew a headline that made the unresolved inquiry sound settled.

**AUTHORITY NOT YET VERIFIED**

The blank was more honest than a confident caption.

### Thursday, 12 November — The cost of a wrong verb

The International Relations Coordinator and partnerships team sorted the fictional inquiry’s paperwork by document number, not by the order in which the pages had arrived. The file remained unopened. Its contents remained unknown.

The four notices had not become trustworthy through repetition. The request had not become an instruction because it was well worded. And the missing name had not appeared simply because the team needed one.

At closing, the message was still unsent.

### Part 1: The Notice in Four Languages

Before the New Legon Estates compound had fully stirred, Enka had already written three questions in his notebook:

**What does the notice say?**  
**Where did it come from?**  
**Who had authority to issue it?**

The last question mattered most.

At the Koware Group’s New Legon Estates headquarters, four photographs arrived from the field inquiry in Kuala Lumpur. They showed four emergency notices in Malay, English, Mandarin and Tamil. Three arrows pointed toward one stairwell. The fourth pointed elsewhere.

The images were clear enough to show the disagreement. They were not clear enough to settle it.

“The odd one is lower on the wall,” the R&D Lab Specialist said, enlarging the picture. “It also has a date.”

“Could be newer,” said the Liaison Technician. “Could be an older form that still has its date.”

Enka studied the four images. Similar layouts could make the notices look like independent witnesses, but they might all have been copied from one source. A date might help identify a version; it did not prove that the version was valid.

“Record what the photographs show,” he said. “Keep that separate from what we think the signs mean.”

The specialist placed four small magnetic arrows on a metal-edged tray to mark the directions. One stuck to the tray; the others followed it in a rattling procession and attached themselves to Enka’s cuff. He looked down at the little line of arrows on his sleeve, then carefully lifted them off one by one.

The brief laughter passed. The questions remained.

The field team’s assignment was observation and documentation. No one was to test either route or offer guidance to the building’s occupants. Enka asked for the original, uncropped photographs and the notice versions to be preserved without alteration.

Four languages had carried the message across a building. They had not yet carried its authority with it.

### Part 2: The Cropped Truth

The next set of images arrived cropped.

The fourth notice was visible. So was the lift. The wall around them—the part that might have shown whether the notice had been moved, replaced or displayed beside another version—was missing.

At the headquarters, Enka asked the Human & Employment Resources Manager and the Health & Safety Technician to review what the image could and could not establish. The team requested the uncropped original, its creation date, and any current building arrangements that the responsible local personnel were prepared to share.

The building manager described a familiar chain: one person might request a notice, another prepare the wording, someone else handle translation, and another staff member display the finished sheet. The sheet on the wall did not reveal the whole chain.

At the same time, a handwritten sign inside the New Legon compound had been taped too high on a storage-room door. A cleaning trolley caught its loose corner, and the sign fluttered down onto the Janitor’s overalls. He looked at the words **KEEP THIS AREA CLEAR**, then at the cloths in his arms.

“I see they have already made their decision about me,” he said, peeling the paper away.

The staff laughed, then moved the trolley clear of the doorway.

The H&S Technician used the small mishap to make a practical point: an instruction has to be legible and appropriately placed, but even a perfectly placed warning cannot tell people what to do if it conflicts with the responsible safety plan.

The complete image came back later. It showed a faint line beneath the fourth arrow, but not clearly enough to authenticate the wording.

The crop had hidden a question. The full image restored it without answering it.

### Part 3: The Unsigned Fourth Notice

The building manager’s reply came with a distribution register, not the original instruction.

It listed copies and where they had been placed. It showed that the notices had circulated. It did not name the person who approved the instruction or establish that the translations matched an approved original.

Enka drew two columns on the meeting-room board:

**WHAT THIS RECORD SHOWS**  
**WHAT THIS RECORD DOES NOT SHOW**

The register went under the first heading. The missing approval went under the second.

“Silence about a signature doesn’t prove there was no signature,” the specialist said. “It means this register doesn’t supply one.”

The field team had not followed either direction or tested a stairwell. Enka told the group to preserve the register alongside the photographs and to seek the original instruction, its approval history, and the versions that had been issued.

A printer in the nearby office began producing the register’s pages in reverse order. The last sheet slid off the tray first and landed over Bonsu Bolt’s visitor badge. He lifted it, read the badge still visible beneath the paper, and gave the printer a look usually reserved for people who had ignored a clear instruction.

Nobody mistook the printer’s behaviour for evidence. The pages were sorted, checked and filed.

The next request was narrow: find the source instruction and any record showing who approved it, when it took effect, and which versions were distributed. The team would not decide which route was safe.

The register was a real lead in the story. It was not the answer.

### Part 4: The Instruction No One Could Sign

At the R&D console, the Millennium Probe’s displayed date differed from earlier archived readings.

Enka asked the specialist to preserve the new reading with its inputs and acquisition context, rather than replacing what had come before. The historical-reference, production and Probe readings belonged in separate records. None established the field team’s individual location or temporal state.

A loose label on a technical binder peeled away in the room’s air-conditioning and landed squarely on Enka’s notebook. He lifted it by one corner and placed it beside the relevant record.

“Even the label needs a source,” he said.

The conversation returned to the notices. The manager had not found the original instruction. A photograph of the line beneath an arrow was still too indistinct to authenticate. The group kept the question open rather than using the changing Probe display to explain the field evidence.

Enka opened an older design sheet. He could describe the Probe’s history as it appeared in the source material, but the team still needed the actual records and the accepted technology canon before treating an explanation as established.

“Keep the history beside the readings,” he said. “Not in place of them.”

Two inquiries now sat side by side: the authority behind the notice and the interpretation of the changing Probe readings. Neither was allowed to stand in for the other.

The dates on the display were precise. Their meaning still needed evidence.

### Part 5: The Copies That Agreed

By Friday, the four notice versions had been compared repeatedly. Three appeared to agree. The fourth differed.

James Evans, Gloria Mica and Moses Larbi reviewed the papers from their respective academic, coordination and quality-assurance perspectives. The question was not which page had the most supporters. It was whether any of the pages could be traced to an authoritative source.

“Three agreeing copies can still share one mistake,” Gloria said. “We need to know whether they came from the same master.”

Moses recorded the possible hazard without declaring that any route was safe or unsafe. James asked that each version be retained and that the next communication explain the uncertainty plainly.

A ceiling fan lifted the unfastened corners of the four sheets in sequence. For a moment they all bowed forward together, like a row of very serious delegates agreeing to something. Moses placed a paperweight across them before the pages could slide off the table.

The laughter was brief. The papers stayed in order.

The team agreed that a distribution record, a translation, a date or a majority of copies could each support a limited claim. None alone proved that an instruction was authorized and current.

The investigation had not found its source. It had found a more exact question:

**What did the agreement among three copies depend on?**

### Part 6: The Line Beneath the Arrow

The next Monday, Johnson Ortsin unfolded a site plan in a meeting room. Derek Shiraska marked a boundary on the work plan: paper review was within scope; a physical rehearsal of the disputed routes was not.

“If three copies say east, we have a majority,” Johnson said.

“A majority of paper,” Derek replied. “Not a verified exit.”

Matthias Agbenorku reviewed the operations notes. The documents pointed to **Work Order 44-E** and an older records transfer. A possible storage box had been located, but no one had opened it and nobody knew what it contained.

Johnson reached for a site-plan roll. Its elastic band sprang loose; the roll unfurled across the table and flicked a teaspoon into Matthias’s empty cup.

“We have identified the box,” Derek said, catching the roll before it swept the remaining papers aside.

“We have identified where the box is,” Johnson corrected.

The correction changed the next step. The team prepared a request to the responsible records and safety authorities for the source instruction, the applicable approval record and the current site plan. They did not conduct a route test or issue a temporary instruction.

A separate maintenance notice prompted a check of whether any listed work would affect Koware’s own facilities. It was treated as a reason to verify, not as proof that a particular building would lose power.

The sealed-box lead gave the inquiry somewhere to look. It did not give anyone permission to open it.

### Part 7: The Name the Paper Could Not Give

The release request looked nearly complete.

It had a transfer reference, a date and a stamp. The line for the person authorized to request the file was blank.

Mubarak Mohsen aligned the form with the transfer record. The two pages confirmed different things: one recorded a transfer; the other sought a release. Neither established that the right person had asked for it.

On the table, Elijah Peter’s arranged the envelopes without opening the correspondence inside. Lovemore Saiga brought in a faded plant label from the garden.

“Accurate date,” he said, turning the label over. “No idea what it belongs to.”

He set it beside the form. It was an apt comparison, though the label did not solve the paperwork.

Enka brought up a draft message to the field team:

*We have found the authorizing instruction. Proceed to check the route.*

Mubarak stopped him.

“We have found a possible container for the instruction. The box is still sealed.”

Enka deleted the sentence. The revised request asked the proper custodian to confirm the authorized requester and the release process. It also asked that qualified local personnel confirm what guidance applied to the building as it stood now.

Mubarak signed only as the preparer of a draft request. His signature could not grant authority he did not have.

A second question remained: even if the original instruction was recovered, did it still match the present building?

The authorization line stayed blank.

### Part 8: The Message That Stayed Unsent

#### Wednesday, 11 November

At the reception pinboard, the Legal Journalist drew a line through **CONFIRMED** on a draft card and replaced it with **REPORTED**.

Josh Lafa, the Public Relations & Press Coordinator, read the draft description of the Kuala Lumpur notices. It said that the instructions had been approved. The records supported distribution, not approval.

Terry Joan, the Marketing Manager, withdrew a headline that made the case sound solved. The team could describe the inquiry; it could not present an unverified direction as an official safety instruction.

The Legal Journalist’s marker ran dry halfway through **REPORTED**. The word ended as a thin, fading line. The journalist shook the marker once, tested it on the margin, and rewrote the label clearly.

A blank rectangle was left on the board:

**AUTHORITY NOT YET VERIFIED.**

Around it, dated items from Ghana and elsewhere were marked for source and status checks. The board was meant to help staff distinguish developing reports from confirmed information, not to imply that every headline was a Koware contract, decision or threat.

#### Thursday, 12 November

The International Relations and Global Partnerships colleagues compared the wording and provenance of the records. They kept the Malaysian notice inquiry distinct from any unrelated news concerning Malaysia.

A receipt could show that a request had arrived. It could not prove that the recipient understood it or that the sender had authority to ask for the file. The team drafted a secondary contact route and an offline record of decisions in case the ordinary channel failed.

The registrar added a careful annotation beside the box reference:

*Location indicated; contents unknown; release authority unresolved.*

No fresh authenticated field report established the individual team members’ conditions or whereabouts. The supplied geographic position remained Kuala Lumpur; it did not answer the other questions.

The annotation did not solve the mystery. It prevented the mystery from being mistaken for a solution.

#### Friday, 13 November

At the Friday meeting, the Legal Journalist reviewed the pinboard and its developing headlines. The Events & Tours Coordinator and Community Outreach Officer discussed a possible visit to a local small-business fair. The Booking Agent kept expressed interest separate from a confirmed arrangement.

As the meeting began, the Booking Agent tried to slide an overfilled folder beneath the door while balancing attendance forms. The folder jammed, the forms lifted into the air, and one landed over the agent’s face like a paper mask. The room laughed; the papers were gathered and the meeting continued.

Then Enka returned the group to the Kuala Lumpur file. Four notices. Three matching arrows. One different. A distribution record. Work Order 44-E. A sealed box. No verified approval.

Edliex had listened through the first part of the meeting. Enka invited its feedback. The system’s status light changed.

“Across the notice inquiry and today’s planning, we keep encountering the same distinctions,” Edliex said. “A record of receipt is not endorsement. A record of distribution is not authorization. A location reference is not proof of contents.”

Enka invited Edliex into conversational mode.

The AI proposed a verification sequence: establish the original source; identify the approving authority; verify the revision and effective date; compare the instruction with the current site plan; check the translations against the approved source; and have the responsible local safety authority determine what guidance applies.

“Would that let us announce a safe stairwell?” Derek asked.

“No,” Edliex replied. “It would let us explain what evidence we have. The responsible qualified local authority must make that determination.”

A draft sentence appeared on the screen:

*Use the direction shown on the three matching notices pending further advice.*

Enka read it once and deleted it.

The replacement request sought authorization to inspect the archive through a verified channel, asked for the source and revision history, and requested current guidance from the responsible local personnel. The sender-authorization and channel-authentication fields were still incomplete.

No one pressed **SEND**.

The box remained sealed. The original instruction remained unauthenticated. The team remained within its observation-only assignment. The Probe readings remained a separate technical question.

The message stayed unsent because it claimed more than the evidence could support.

## Cold Open: Four Languages, One Question

A building can print the same warning in four languages and still not say who was entitled to give it.

In Kuala Lumpur, four emergency notices hung near a lift in Malay, English, Mandarin and Tamil. Three pointed to one stairwell. The fourth pointed away. All four looked official, but looking official was not proof of anything.

The Classies field team had one job: observe, photograph and preserve. They were not to test a route or replace a safety instruction. Over ten days, Enka and the Koware team in New Legon would learn how much trouble a plausible document can cause.

## E02.01 — The Notice in Four Languages
**Monday, 2 November 2026**

Enka was awake before the compound stirred. His Eternal Dreamfall plan had reached Week 96 and the Septuagint, the long journey of meaning between languages. The weekly list named no passage for the day, so he left that line blank and carried the question with him: what survives translation, and what quietly changes?

In the Koware laboratory, three clocks sat on the console. The production clock read Accra, November 2026. The historical-reference clock read a July Sunday in 2003, Las Vegas. The Probe held its own separate reading. None of them said where the field team stood.

The analyst's photographs arrived from Kuala Lumpur. Three notices pointed to one stairwell, and one pointed elsewhere. The odd notice hung a little lower and was the only one with a printed date.

"Three copies can come from one mistake," the team lead said over the link.

The R&D Lab Specialist leaned in for a better look, and the chair under her slid away on a loose caster. She glided across the floor with a tray of printed photographs, and they fanned out behind her like a card trick. Enka caught the last one with two fingers and did not smile until she had stopped moving.

At the Monday briefing, Edliex listened through the status reports and then spoke.

"Three agreeing copies are one witness until someone proves they are three," Edliex said. "I'd record what the signs say and what the corridor is, and not let either stand in for the other."

Enka wrote **OBSERVED** on one page and **NOT YET VERIFIED** on the next. Under the second heading he listed the original instruction, its author, its approver and the translations' fidelity.

## E02.02 — The Cropped Truth
**Tuesday, 3 November 2026**

Enka's questions narrowed. Who requested the notice? Who wrote the wording? Who approved the translations, and when were the versions put up?

He called the Kuala Lumpur building manager. The video link froze mid-sentence on an expression of heroic concentration while Enka carried on talking to it for several seconds. He stopped when the Legal Journalist leaned into frame and said, "He can't hear you. He's a statue."

The link recovered. The manager explained that a notice passes through hands before it reaches a wall. One person asks, another words it, a third translates, and a fourth tapes it up. The finished sheet does not show that chain.

A team photograph arrived cropped. It showed the odd notice and the lift and nothing of the wall around them, and Enka asked for the uncropped original. He had learned to distrust a story that had been trimmed to fit. The manager promised to check the records.

The role check placed Health and Safety and personnel support in view. Enka took it as a reminder that a printed arrow moves people. It was not proof that any named person had done real work. The team stayed observation-only.

## E02.03 — The Unsigned Fourth Notice
**Wednesday, 4 November 2026**

The manager's reply came without the document Enka had asked for. It was a distribution register, and it showed that copies had been issued.

"Does it show who approved the instruction?" Enka asked.

"That the copies were tracked," the specialist said. "Not that the instruction was authorised."

Enka drew two columns: **WHAT THIS RECORD SHOWS** and **WHAT THIS RECORD DOES NOT SHOW**. Silence about a signature was not proof that no signature existed. It meant only that this record did not supply one.

He returned to the Health and Safety material on hazard identification. A disagreement between instructions was itself a warning sign, but that did not entitle a remote, unbriefed team to pick a route. Any assessment belonged to the building's responsible staff.

The register had to be printed for the evidence file, and the Koware printer chewed the first copy, shuddered and fed the page out in a spiral. It was stapled to the register's cover sheet. The specialist photographed it and filed it under **Evidence (Mechanical Opinion)**.

By evening the next request was specific: the original instruction, its approval record, its effective date and the issued versions. The fourth notice stayed unsigned.

## E02.04 — The Instruction No One Could Sign
**Thursday, 5 November 2026**

The Probe display changed in the middle of a review. The destination date flickered and settled on a new value. The lab lights dipped for half a second.

"Don't overwrite it," Enka said, already moving. "Save the raw response. Save every earlier reading."

The specialist caught the printout as it slid from the tray, then lunged for a thermos rolling off the bench. She came up tangled in a data cable with the thermos in one hand and the paper in the other, having saved both. The cable took longer.

The run record listed historical reference, current date, phase boundaries, oscillation index, amplitude and Probe value, each on its own line. The Probe recalculated against its inputs, so a changed date was not yet a changed destination. Enka's old design sheet, in his own handwriting, lay beside the console. So did the history that the cloned Edubeam had first been operated only by the Micro Computer Technology Institute of London. None of it replaced the readings.

He wrote three headings: **WHAT THE SOURCE RETURNED**, **WHAT THE CALCULATION MAY MEAN** and **WHAT WE HAVE NOT VERIFIED**. The Probe puzzle and the notice puzzle had to stay in separate files, so that neither could be used to explain the other away.

The team's supplied position was Kuala Lumpur. A Probe timestamp did not locate them in time.

## E02.05 — The Copies That Agreed
**Friday, 6 November 2026**

On Friday, the Director of Academic Programs, the Faculty Coordinator and the Quality Assurance and Compliance Manager lined up all four versions. Three agreed and one did not.

"Three agreeing copies aren't three independent witnesses," the Faculty Coordinator said. "They could all descend from one mistake."

At 10:40 Kuala Lumpur time, the building sounded an alarm. The field analyst's message arrived in a rush: a fire drill, announced only by the alarm itself. The team watched from the lobby and did not move.

Occupants poured from the lifts and split. Some followed the three matching notices and some followed the fourth. A knot of visitors stopped dead, looking from sign to sign. Then the building's wardens appeared in hi-vis vests with whistles and called people to one muster point by voice, cutting across the paper. The team recorded all of it and intervened in none.

"They used neither arrow," the analyst reported. "They used the wardens."

Enka read the footage twice. Whatever the wardens were following, it was not on the wall. The drill showed the danger of the disagreement but did not say which notice was right. The team agreed to preserve every version with its provenance, and the week closed with the original source still missing.

## E02.06 — The Line Beneath the Arrow
**Monday, 9 November 2026**

Four notices lay on the table in New Legon. Johnson Ortsin unfolded a site plan. "If three say east, we have a majority."

"A majority of paper," Derek Shiraska said. "Not a verified exit."

Matthias Agbenorku arrived with the operations notes. A work order marked **44-E** accounted for the notices being installed, but not for the authority behind them. The building manager had denied issuing them.

Edliex listened through the update and then spoke. "Don't argue about which arrow wins. Find out who had the right to draw it."

The team sent a narrow request to the responsible safety officer. A circled line in the Accra electricity-maintenance schedule raised equipment readiness. The schedule did not establish the supply status of Koware's own building, so Matthias asked for verification and did not declare an outage.

At 14:10, word came that the regional records office could trace Work Order 44-E to an older file transfer. A matching storage box had been found. Matthias and Johnson went at once, one on foot and one with the site plan under his arm. They made the office before closing, where a clerk showed them the shelf. The box was sealed, and neither of them touched it.

"We found the box," Johnson said.

"We found where the box is," Derek corrected, by phone.

## E02.07 — The Name the Paper Could Not Give
**Tuesday, 10 November 2026**

Mubarak Mohsen held the release request up to the office light. It had a transfer reference, a date and a handsome stamp. Beneath them was an empty line: **WHO IS AUTHORISED TO REQUEST RELEASE?**

The custodian would not move the box without a named, authorised requester. At the counter the air tightened. A queue formed behind Mubarak, someone checked a watch, and the stamp pad sat between him and the clerk like a dare.

On a screen in New Legon, Enka's draft message to Kuala Lumpur read: *We have found the authorising instruction. Proceed to check the route.*

"We have found a possible container for the instruction," Mubarak said. "The box is still sealed."

Enka deleted the sentence.

The revised request preserved the conflicting notices and asked the issuing office to establish its authority. It did not authorise a route inspection. Mubarak signed only for the request to be considered, never on behalf of the authority it needed. As he lifted the stamp, he planted it squarely on his own cuff, leaving a perfect official oval on his sleeve. The clerk looked at it and then at him. "That one," she said, "is valid."

A second question sat lower on the form: **DOES THE ORIGINAL INSTRUCTION STILL MATCH THE BUILDING AS IT STANDS TODAY?** Even a valid signature would not answer that.

## E02.08 — The Message That Stayed Unsent
**Wednesday 11 – Friday 13 November 2026**

**Wednesday.** The Legal Journalist's first act at the reception pinboard was to strike a word from a headline. **CONFIRMED** became **REPORTED**. The Press Coordinator read the draft. "It says the notices were approved and distributed."

"Distributed is what the work order suggests," the Marketing Manager said. "Approved is what we keep trying to prove."

Matthew 6:11, the day's Bible App reference, sat with Enka as he thought about reliable information for the next responsible step. The Legal Journalist reserved a blank rectangle labelled **AUTHORITY NOT YET VERIFIED**.

**Thursday.** The International Relations desk worked from three questions. What was the instruction's source? Who could authorise it? Who would check that it still suited the building? The Global Partnerships Registrar numbered the Kuala Lumpur papers. The Liaison logged each version's language, recipient and whether delivery was acknowledged. "An acknowledgement is not comprehension," the Liaison said. "And comprehension is not permission."

The Legal Journalist flagged a Ghanaian budget item for verification. It gave the Marketing desk something to study, but it was not a Koware contract. The team also watched reports of a communications outage and asked a practical question: if an approval request vanished into an inaccessible inbox, how would anyone prove what was sent? They proposed an offline decision record and a second contact channel. The registrar annotated the sealed-box line: *Location indicated; contents unknown; release authority unresolved.*

**Friday.** The pinboard was crowded. The Legal Journalist had sorted verified reports from developing ones: the Ghanaian budget item, the Ghana Stock Exchange discussion of blockchain and capital-market modernisation, the Ecobank JoyBusiness SME Fair and the Malaysian investigation into possible AI-chip re-exports. The Malaysia story shared a country with the Kuala Lumpur inquiry but none of its facts.

The Events and Tours Coordinator saw the SME fair as a chance for staff to observe local businesses. The Booking Agent kept a list of interested staff and a separate list of confirmed ones. "Those names mean they would like to attend," the Booking Agent said. "We don't have a confirmed booking."

Then the Booking Agent tried to slide an overloaded folder under the meeting-room door while balancing a stack of attendance forms. The folder wedged and the forms sprang up. One large sheet came down squarely on the Booking Agent's head like an oversized paper hat. The room forgot the paperwork for a moment.

Then it remembered. The Legal Journalist struck **CONFIRMED** from one more draft, because it was not.

The box still held a possible instruction. The release request still needed an authorised name. Even if the box opened, the document inside would have to be checked against the current building and the four language versions.

The message Enka had nearly sent said the team had an authoritative instruction. It would have implied more than the evidence showed, and it might have prompted action before anyone had verified a thing. It stayed unsent.

He looked at the blank authorisation line.

The notices had been distributed and the transfer record existed. The box had been found. No one had established who issued the instruction, whether it was authentic, or whether it still matched the building. The Classies team had not been authorised to test either route.

Enka closed the file. No one would turn a plausible document into a safety instruction because the story needed an ending.

The message stayed unsent. The investigation stayed open. For once, the blank line said exactly what it needed to say.

The team lead leaned into the frame. "The odd one is mounted a little lower than the others. It's also the only one with a printed date."
"Three copies can come from one mistake," the Specialist murmured.
Nobody on the team walked toward either stairwell. They photographed each notice, noted where it hung and how it looked, and wrote down every difference in wording and direction. Their assignment was to observe and document, not to test a route, not to advise the occupants, and not to decide which arrow was right.
"Record what the signs say," Enka told them. "Record what the corridor actually is. Don't let one stand in for the other."
At nine o'clock the Monday briefing filled the meeting room. Staff from across the five blocks took their seats, and Enka laid out the situation plainly. Edliex, Koware's AI, listened through the first part of the meeting as it always did, silent while the people talked. Only when Enka finished did its calm voice come through the room speaker.
"Three notices agreeing is a count," Edliex said. "It is not yet a source. I would want to know whether the four language versions were translated from one original, or from each other."
Enka wrote three questions at the top of a clean page:
• What instruction was actually issued?
• Who had the authority to issue it?
• Do the four translations faithfully represent the original?
That evening, on his way out, he passed the reception area. The Legal Journalist had already pinned a fresh card to the board where every employee would see it in the morning: Kuala Lumpur — four notices, one question.
Four languages had carried the message across the building. They had not yet shown whether it came from four sources or one.
Part 2: The Cropped Truth
Tuesday, 3 November. The photograph from Kuala Lumpur had arrived overnight, and it looked complete until Enka enlarged it.
The field analyst's first image of the odd notice had been trimmed by the messaging app's preview. When the full frame came through, a thin strip of print ran along the bottom edge, too small to read at the resolution received. It might be a footer. It might be a printer's mark. It might be nothing.
"The crop didn't lie," the lab liaison said. "It just left out the part that might matter."
"Ask for a closer shot of the bottom edge," Enka said. "Same angle, no cropping, with a ruler in frame for scale if they have one."
The request went out. Then Enka turned to the larger question: who stood behind the notices?
The team lead had spoken with the building manager in person that morning, and Enka drafted the questions to put to the manager next. Who asked for the notice? Who prepared the original wording? Who authorised the translations? When was each version put on display? Had the same instruction gone out through any other channel?
The manager's answer, relayed in the afternoon, was careful and honest. A notice could pass through many hands before it reached a wall. One person requested it, another drafted it, another translated it, another printed it, another hung it. By the time it hung in a corridor, nobody looking at it could tell which of those people had actually decided anything.
"So the chain exists," Enka said, "but it isn't printed on the paper."
Back in New Legon, the day's role check put Health and Safety and personnel support in the front of the room. Enka asked both to think about the notices as people would meet them: a stranger in a corridor, possibly in a hurry, reading whichever language they knew best and trusting it completely.
The real world kept pressing at the edges of the work. By mid-morning, a small knot of staff had gathered at the reception pinboard, where the Legal Journalist had pinned a headline about talk of rising commuting fares across Accra. The Janitor and the Maid, who both travelled a long way each morning, had a great deal to say about it, and they said it with feeling.
"If it goes up again, the trotro will take half my first hour of pay," the Maid said.
"At least you're not paying for the second leg," the Janitor replied.
The Legal Journalist reached up to straighten the card and knocked the little dish of drawing pins off its shelf. Fifty pins hit the tiled floor at once. Everyone in reception froze, then began hopping on one foot, trying to find a place to put the other. It took the Janitor and a broom a full minute to calm the floor.
The fares item stayed on the board. The Legal Journalist wrote the date beside it and added a note: Reported. Source to be checked.
The field team's closer photograph arrived just before evening. The strip along the bottom edge was still too faint to read. That was a result, too, and Enka entered it exactly as it was: Unreadable at this resolution. Not yet verified.
A cropped photograph hides what lies outside the frame. A convenient summary does the same. The team would keep asking for the whole page.
Part 3: The Unsigned Fourth Notice
Wednesday, 4 November. The reply from the Kuala Lumpur building manager came as an attachment, and for a moment the whole R&D block leaned toward the screen.
It was a distribution register. It listed four copies of the emergency notice, one per language, with the date each had been issued for display and the location where each had been hung.
Enka read it twice, then pulled the four photographs alongside it. "Does this show who approved the instruction?"
"It shows who handed the copies out," the R&D Lab Specialist said. "Not who decided what they say."
He took a fresh page and wrote two headings in capitals: WHAT THIS RECORD SHOWS and WHAT THIS RECORD DOES NOT SHOW. Under the first he wrote: four copies circulated; four dates; four hanging positions. Under the second: the author of the instruction, the approving authority, the translator, the relationship between the versions.
Then they went back to the photographs. The fourth notice, the one pointing right, still carried the only printed date. But on all four, the small box near the bottom where a name or an approving office would normally appear stood empty. The fourth notice was not the only unsigned one. It was simply the one with a date to make it look finished.
"A date isn't a signature," the Specialist said.
"No," Enka agreed. "It's a date."
The Wednesday meeting ran long. In the first half, department leads spoke about their own work, and the technical-support and operations roles of the day described their usual quiet routines: version control for shared files, backups, handovers between shifts. Enka listened with new interest. It sounded exactly like the problem on the wall in Kuala Lumpur, only turned inside out. A missing approval line in a shared file could be traced in minutes. A missing approval line in a corridor notice could stay missing for years.
The Technical Support Officer, carrying a power strip toward the projector, did not notice the extension cable hooked around one ankle. The officer got three steps further, towing a potted ficus across the carpet, before the plant's pot caught the leg of a table and the whole procession halted. Nobody laughed until the ficus was upright again. Then everybody laughed at once.
When the room settled, Edliex gave its feedback.
"The hazard-identification lesson in the Health and Safety programme is relevant here," it said. "Conflicting instructions are a hazard in themselves. Identifying the hazard is not the same as deciding which instruction is safe. That decision belongs to the people responsible for the building."
Enka nodded. The deployed team confirmed by evening that it had not followed either direction, had not asked anyone to follow one, and had not approached the stairwells. The notices were evidence. They were not permission to improvise.
Before he closed the day's file, Enka wrote the next request in plain words: the original instruction; the record of who approved it; the date it took effect; and the list of versions that had been issued.
Four languages had carried the message. The empty line had carried nothing at all.
Part 4: The Instruction No One Could Sign
Thursday, 5 November. The Probe's display had changed again overnight, and the newest member of the R&D team stood in front of it with the expression of someone who had been quietly worried for an hour.
"It was a different date yesterday," the new specialist said. "Is it broken?"
The R&D Lab Specialist did not look worried at all. "No. The Probe was designed by the founder of the Koware Group. The dates change on purpose. Every reading is a careful calculation built from historical data and the current date, so it moves as the current date moves."
"And the Edubeam?"
"The founder established the cloned Edubeam too. For a long time it was operated solely by the Micro Computer Technology Institute of London." The Specialist handed over the run log. "Keep every reading. A new value does not replace an old one. It sits beside it."
They were reaching for a marker to label the log when the Specialist leaned on the whiteboard's tray. The tray flipped. A dozen markers rose into the air, and for a moment the whole lab watched them hang. Enka, who had just walked in, caught the last one without breaking his stride and set it on the desk as though that had been the plan all along.
"Raw response first," he said. "Then interpretation. What did the source return?"
The three of them wrote it out in three short columns: what the Probe returned, what the calculation might mean, and what they had not verified. The deployed team's position, Kuala Lumpur, stayed in its own box. The Probe date said something about the Probe. It said nothing about whether a building's emergency notice had been approved.
That was the point Enka kept returning to as the morning went on. He had two investigations open on his desk. One was about a changing date. The other was about an unsigned instruction. They looked alike from a distance, and he was careful not to let either answer the other's question.
Midday brought another small clue. The distribution register's margin carried a work-order reference, the number partly illegible in the copy sent. The team lead was asked to request a clean copy of that margin, and Enka sat for a while with the page in front of him, thinking about how the notices had been made.
A request had been raised. Someone had drafted wording. A translator had rendered it four ways. A printer had produced copies. A work order had put them on the wall. At every step, the person doing the work seemed to have assumed that someone else, earlier or later, had signed it.
"That's the pattern," he told the Specialist. "Everyone touched it. Nobody owned it."
"An instruction no one could sign," the Specialist said.
"Or an instruction everyone thought somebody else had."
He wrote the next message to Kuala Lumpur with care. It asked for the original instruction, the approval record, the effective date and the issued versions. It did not ask the team to take any step except to keep documenting. Near the bottom he added a line he was beginning to treat as a rule: No action on any notice until the authority behind it is established.
The Probe's date would move again tomorrow. The empty signature line would not move unless somebody found whose hand belonged there.
Part 5: The Copies That Agreed
Friday, 6 November. By the end of the first week the investigation had piled up evidence of five different kinds, and none of them proved the same thing.
There were the notices themselves. There was the distribution register. There was the request for the original instruction, still unanswered. There were the building manager's careful messages. And there were the field team's photographs, now numbering more than forty.
The Friday status review gathered in the academic block, because the Director of Academic Programs had offered the larger table. James Evans, whom most of the staff simply called Director James, spread the enlargements across it with the Faculty Coordinator and the Quality Assurance and Compliance Manager on either side of him. Enka sat at the end with his notebook.
"Three of the four agree," Director James said. "That's the argument people will make when they see this. Three out of four."
"Three agreeing copies aren't three witnesses," the Faculty Coordinator replied. "They could all have been copied from the same page. If the page was wrong, they're wrong together, and they'll look very sure of themselves."
The Faculty Coordinator had brought the three agreeing enlargements in a neat stack, and was bending to lay them out beside the fourth when the stack slid from under one arm. Three sheets hit the floor, skated across the tiles, and came to rest in a perfectly aligned row, edges touching.
Nobody said anything for a moment.
"Even dropped," the Quality Assurance and Compliance Manager said at last, "they agree."
The laughter loosened the room, and the conversation turned practical. The Quality Assurance and Compliance Manager did not want to settle the matter by counting. To them, two conflicting instructions on the same wall were a hazard to be assessed properly: identify it, record it, and hand it to whoever was responsible for the building. Majority vote was not part of any risk assessment they had ever signed.
"Keep every version," Director James told the team. "Note which are identical in layout, which in wording, which in date. And write the uncertainty plainly. If we do not know something, the record should say we do not know it."
Edliex had been listening through the first half of the review. When Director James turned to ask for its view, it answered at once.
"Agreement between copies supports only one conclusion," it said. "That the copies are alike. Whether the instruction is correct depends on where they came from, and on whether the building has changed since."
That last phrase landed. Enka wrote it at the top of the page and underlined it: whether the building has changed since.
As the afternoon thinned out, the usual Friday sounds returned to the estate. In one corner of the administrative block, the Corporate Manager and the Liaison Officer were deep in a quiet conversation about a client who had begun talking of moving part of its operations out of the country. It was a different worry on a different desk, and a reminder that the notices in Kuala Lumpur were only one thread in a busy week.
Before he left, Enka summarised where things stood, aloud, so that the team would hear it in the same words. The notices had been distributed. Their source was uncertain. The approval record had not been found. Nobody knew which route, if either, matched the building's current emergency arrangements. The team had not tested a route and would not.
The week had produced a lot of paper. It had produced one clear idea. Whether three agreeing copies were right depended on one question, and the question was still open: what, exactly, did their agreement rest on?
Part 6: The Line Beneath the Arrow
Monday, 9 November. Four emergency notices lay in a row on the long table in the operations block, and Johnson Ortsin, the Programme Manager, was unrolling a site plan across them.
"If three copies say east," Johnson said, "we have a majority."
"A majority of paper," said Derek Shiraska, the Production Coordinator. "Not a verified exit."
The plan was large, and it did not want to lie flat. Johnson pressed one corner, and the far end rolled itself up, swept the table, and toppled a tray of sachet water. One sachet split. A bright arc of water caught Matthias Agbenorku, the Director of Operations, squarely across the shirt front. He had just walked in carrying his unfinished notes.
Matthias did not stop walking, and he did not stop talking. "The latest field report says the building manager denies issuing the notices," he said, setting down his notes and wiping a sleeve. "The manager says a distribution work order, number 44-E, accounts for how the copies were hung. It does not account for who decided what they say."
"So they were put up properly," Johnson said, "and we still don't know whether they tell anyone the right thing."
Enka had come in quietly and stood at the end of the table. "Let's not argue about which arrow wins," he said. "Let's find out who had the right to draw it."
The team agreed on a narrow plan. They would write to the responsible safety officer and ask for the current site plan, the original instruction and the procedure that applied until the matter was settled. And Johnson, who had started the morning planning to rehearse both routes, put the rehearsal back in his folder. Nobody on the field team would walk through either stairwell on the strength of a disputed notice. Any rehearsal would have to wait for a verified procedure and the authority to run it.
Derek wrote it on the whiteboard in two columns: WHAT WE CAN REVIEW and WHAT REQUIRES AUTHORITY.
During the weekly meeting that followed, Edliex listened through the first half as usual. When it spoke, it raised a point none of the staff had.
"Even if the original instruction is found," it said, "it may no longer match the building. A genuine old instruction can still be an unsuitable one."
The afternoon brought two small interruptions. The first was an Accra electricity-maintenance schedule that the Legal Journalist had pinned to the reception board, listing different communities on different days. It said nothing about whether the New Legon cluster would lose supply. Matthias circled the relevant line and asked for verification, saying plainly that nobody should announce an outage the schedule had not announced.
The second came at ten past two, when the team lead's update came through from Kuala Lumpur. The regional records office had traced Work Order 44-E to an older administrative file transfer. A storage box matching the transfer reference had been found in an archive room.
It had not been opened.
Johnson set down his pen. "We found the box."
"We found where the box is," Derek said.
The distinction was becoming a habit around the table. Nobody knew what the box held. Nobody yet knew who had the authority to open it.
That evening Enka laid the two investigations side by side on his desk: the Probe's changing dates and the building's disputed notices. The first asked what a reading meant. The second asked who had the right to give an order. Both demanded the same discipline. A record was only as strong as the claim it could actually carry.
Part 7: The Name the Paper Could Not Give
Tuesday, 10 November. The smallest space on the page was the one that caused the trouble.
Mubarak Mohsen held the release request under the office light in the administrative block and read it for the third time. The form had three things that made it look reassuring: a transfer reference, a date, and a handsome official stamp. Below them sat a single line of print.
WHO IS AUTHORISED TO REQUEST RELEASE?
The space beside it was empty.
"They all look complete," Mubarak told the colleagues around his desk, Elijah Peter at one elbow and Lovemore Saiga at the other, "until you ask what each one is supposed to prove."
He lined up the three papers. The transfer reference proved that a box had been moved. Work Order 44-E proved that copies had been distributed. The stamp proved that somebody had owned a stamp.
The custodian in Kuala Lumpur would not release the box without a properly authorised request. Mubarak had been asked to prepare one.
Meanwhile, on a nearby screen, Enka was reviewing a message he had drafted to the field team. It read: We have found the authorising instruction. Proceed to check the route.
Mubarak glanced over. "You can't send that."
"It's what the records suggest."
"It's what we hope the records suggest. We've found a place where an instruction might be. The box is sealed."
Enka looked at the sentence for a long moment. Then he deleted it, one word at a time, like someone removing a tooth.
The revised message asked the issuing office to confirm its authority, asked the responsible local personnel to state what interim safety guidance applied, and asked the team to preserve the conflicting notices exactly as they were. It did not claim the instruction had been found. It did not ask anyone to inspect a route.
Mubarak turned back to his form. He picked up the stamp pad, inked the stamp, and then leaned back in his chair to think about the wording. The chair, which had been tilting all morning, finally decided to tip. Mubarak caught the edge of the desk with one hand and saved himself, but the stamp swung up in the other and landed on his own forehead with a neat, wet thunk.
The room read the print in the mirror of his glasses at the same instant: RECEIVED.
"Well," Elijah said, "at least somebody has finally been authorised to receive you."
Mubarak wiped his forehead with a tissue, and the mark came off slowly, which seemed to amuse everyone more than it amused him.
When the laughter died, he went back to the form and signed it in the only way he honestly could. He wrote that the office requested consideration of the release. He did not sign on behalf of the authority the request needed. The authorisation line stayed blank.
Then he noticed a second question at the bottom of the page, in smaller type.
DOES THE ORIGINAL INSTRUCTION STILL MATCH THE BUILDING AS IT STANDS TODAY?
Even a perfect signature would not answer that one.
In the same hours, Enka kept three clocks on his display: the present in Accra, the historical-reference date in Las Vegas, and the Probe's own reading. A timestamp could be exact and still explain nothing. A name on a form could be legible and still give nobody the right to act.
By evening, Mubarak had finished the request and put it in the tray for review. He looked at the blank line once more before he switched off the lamp. It said what it needed to say. Nobody had given it a name yet.
Part 8: The Message That Stayed Unsent
Wednesday, 11 November — The Empty Space
The first thing the Legal Journalist removed from the reception pinboard that morning was not a headline. It was a word.
CONFIRMED.
The marker went straight through it. Beneath it, in smaller letters, went the replacement: REPORTED.
The board had become the place where the Koware community met the day's news. Staff paused there on their way to their blocks, reading about business, projects and public developments. The Legal Journalist wanted every item on it to carry its source, its publication date, its verification status and the reason it mattered to Koware.
The Public Relations and Press Coordinator studied the latest draft summary of the Kuala Lumpur inquiry. "This says the instructions were approved and distributed."
"Distributed is what the work order suggests," said the Marketing Manager. "Approved is what we keep trying to prove."
The Public Relations desk declined to publish any conclusion as an official safety notice. The Marketing desk withdrew a dramatic headline it had drafted the night before. They agreed to keep the disagreement in the internal record without presenting either route as safe.
That morning's Bible App reading was Matthew 6:11, and Enka held on to it while he walked the corridor. He thought less about the daily bread itself than about the daily word that came with it: reliable information for the next responsible step, enough for today, not a hoard for a week no one could see.
Before noon, the Legal Journalist put up a blank rectangle on the board, framed in black marker, with one line inside: AUTHORITY NOT YET VERIFIED.
The blank space was an honest piece of reporting.
Thursday, 12 November — The Cost of a Wrong Verb
By Thursday the International Relations desk was working from three separate questions. Where did the instruction originally come from? Who could authorise it? And who would be accountable for checking that it still suited the building as it now stood?
The Global Partnerships Registrar had arranged copies of the Kuala Lumpur paperwork by document number. The Global Partnerships Liaison had started a second register beside it, recording the language of each version, its recipient, and whether delivery had been acknowledged.
"An acknowledgement isn't comprehension," the Liaison said. "And comprehension isn't permission."
Nobody in the office claimed to know the deployed team's individual condition or whereabouts. Their supplied geographic position was still Kuala Lumpur, but no new authenticated message from the field had reached the desk since the box was found. Enka's research display carried the three clocks in their separate columns: the production present in Accra, the historical-reference date in Las Vegas, and the Probe's own reading. None of them established the team's physical position. None of them made any emergency route valid.
Later, the Legal Journalist queued a Ghanaian budget item for verification. A reported new-economy programme gave the Marketing desk something to analyse, but it was not a Koware contract, a grant approval or authority to announce a partnership, and the card said so in its first line.
The International Relations team also looked at a developing report of disruption to widely used communications services. The report raised a practical question for the Kuala Lumpur file. If an important approval request vanished into an inaccessible inbox, how would anyone prove what had been sent, received or authorised?
The Registrar proposed a secondary contact channel and an offline decision record, a book in which every request and reply was written by hand and dated. Nobody claimed Koware itself had suffered an outage. They only agreed it would be foolish to wait for one before building the habit.
That evening the Registrar added a line in the margin beside the sealed-box reference:
Location indicated; contents unknown; release authority unresolved.
The annotation did not solve the mystery. It prevented the mystery from being mistaken for a solution.
Friday, 13 November — The Unsent Instruction
By Friday morning, the reception pinboard was crowded.
The Legal Journalist had sorted the items into verified reporting and developing stories. A discussion on blockchain and capital-market modernisation at the Ghana Stock Exchange sat in one column. The Ecobank JoyBusiness SME Fair sat in another. A Malaysian investigation into the possible re-export of AI chips had its own card, set well away from the Kuala Lumpur inquiry, with a note that the two concerned the same country and nothing else. Each carried its source and its verification status.
The Events and Tours Coordinator saw the SME fair as a rare chance to watch Ghanaian businesses at work. The Community Outreach Officer wanted to find out what smaller exhibitors actually needed from a partner like Koware. The Booking Agent had a spreadsheet of staff who had expressed interest, with a separate column for confirmed arrangements, which was still empty.
"Those names mean they would like to go," the Booking Agent said. "They don't mean they are booked."
"Then let's not put anyone on a bus in the minutes," the Events and Tours Coordinator said.
They settled it as a proposal. When the fair opened, two of the interested staff would attend, collect what they could, and bring a report to the next Koware meeting. The Booking Agent was carrying a thick folder and a stack of attendance forms toward the meeting room to start the paperwork. At the doorway, the folder wedged against the frame. The forms sprang loose, rose in a white cloud, and one oversized sheet came down squarely on the Booking Agent's head like a paper hat.
For a moment the whole corridor forgot the Kuala Lumpur file.
The Booking Agent stood perfectly still beneath the sheet, then said in a calm, muffled voice, "Does anyone want to confirm I'm still here?"
"Reported," said the Legal Journalist.
When the laughter ended, the day's work returned to the file that still mattered.
The archive box remained a possible source of the original instruction. Its location was recorded. The transfer history had been traced. The request for release still waited for someone with the proper authority. And even if the box were opened, whatever lay inside would still have to be checked against the building as it stood and the four language versions on display.
In the quiet of the R&D block, Enka read once more the message he had considered sending on Tuesday. It had claimed too much. It had told a team in another country that an authoritative instruction had been found, when all the evidence showed was that a place where one might be had been located. It could have sent four careful people toward a stairwell on the strength of a sentence.
He thought of the empty line on Mubarak's form. He thought of the black-framed rectangle on the pinboard. The investigation had not given anyone the tidy ending the week had seemed to promise. It had given them something more useful: a clear account of what they knew, what they did not, and which decisions belonged to other people.
The notices had been distributed. The transfer record existed. The box had been found. The issuing authority had not been established. The original instruction had not been authenticated. The building's present layout had not been reconciled with the disputed arrows. And the team had not been authorised to test either route.
Enka closed the file.
There would be another request, another review, another chance to learn the truth. But nobody on his team would turn a plausible document into a safety instruction just because a week wanted an ending.
The message stayed unsent.
The investigation stayed open.
And the blank line, for once, said exactly what it needed to say.
